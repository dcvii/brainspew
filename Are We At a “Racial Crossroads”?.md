**[George Yancey](https://open.substack.com/users/301860943-george-yancey?utm_source=mentions)**

> **Editors’ note**: This is the second of several pieces by regular JFBT authors that we are running in response to Helen Andrews’ recent *Compact* essay, “ [The Racial Crossroads](https://www.compactmag.com/article/the-racial-crossroads/).” (The first is [here](https://freeblackthought.substack.com/p/helen-andrews-racial-crossroads-has).) She argued that America’s post-1960s “racial bargain” is breaking down because it was designed for a mostly white, biracial country and premised on temporary racial preferences that would disappear once racial disparities converged—but demographics have changed and convergence has not occurred.
> 
> Her thesis is that the country now faces a choice between restoring equality under the law or accepting permanent, increasingly pervasive racial favoritism across institutions.
> 
> She characterizes the latter path as “race communism”: an official ideology in which race functions like class did under communism, legitimating transfers of status, money, and power from disfavored to favored groups.

---

In her [recent piece](https://www.compactmag.com/article/the-racial-crossroads/) for *Compact*, Helen Andrews asserts that American race relations are currently at a crossroads. One implication of this is that we are in a moment when race relations may change. That may be true, but the crossroads image implies a dichotomy—we can go either one way or the other. For Andrews, this means we can either promote a progressive approach to race relations, which she refers to as “race communism,” or we can eliminate racial favoritism, which I would call an endorsement of colorblindness. Her essay lays out this stark choice, and she clearly argues for the value of eliminating favoritism. I do think we may be at a point where racial change can occur. However, I do not think the choices are as dichotomous as she implies. Indeed, once we understand the nature of race relations and how we have come to the situation we are in today, I believe we can see that there is a different and better path than racial communism or colorblindness. In this essay, I want to illuminate this different path.

When I talk about race, I assume that two truisms are accepted across the political spectrum. One is that we have had a history of racial abuse in this country. From the slavery of African Americans, extermination of Native Americans, oppression of Asian Americans, and displacement of Hispanic Americans, this reality of racial abuse cannot be denied. Only the most radical racists would argue otherwise. The second truism is that overt racism is currently stigmatized today. There is a reason why those on the left use the charge of racism against their political opponents. It is because it is rare that a person is willing to be seen as a racist. There are few other insults that are considered worse than being called a racist.

These two truisms lead to a conclusion about our current racial struggle. Decades ago, the argument was whether people of color are fully human and deserve human rights. Only the most virulent racists would argue that we are not human today, especially given the stigmatization of being labeled a racist. But the centuries of racial abuse have created lasting historical effects in communities of color, institutional structures that work against people of color, and residual attitudes of white preference. As such, the real question today is: how do we overcome the effects of historical racial abuse, but do so in a way that is fair?

That is the question driving the racial debate today. For those engaging in what Andrews calls race communism, fairness is pursued through various programs of [antiracism](https://freeblackthought.substack.com/p/what-is-antiracism-and-what-is-the) and [DEI](https://freeblackthought.substack.com/p/the-future-of-dei). Some have argued that these programs can be [unfair to whites](https://brodyandassociates.com/alleged-anti-white-discrimination-federal-attacks-on-dei-continue/) and can lead to [resentment](https://www.progressivepolicy.org/a-way-out-of-the-dei-wars/), which can impede race relations. The colorblindness advocated by Andrews answers this question by stating that the best way to achieve fairness is to ignore race. Some argue that colorblindness is inadequate for dealing with [historical](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/blog/a-jurisprudential-reckoning-how-conservatives-use-colorblind-ideology-to-obstruct-racial-justice/) and [contemporary institutional racism](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2013/10/legal-theorist-crenshaw-criticizes-colorblind-policies). There is evidence that both sets of critics make valid points, and neither race communism nor colorblindness is a reliable answer for our current racial struggles.

There is a tendency for us to look for a one-size-fits-all approach to social problems. It feels comforting to state that the answers to all racial problems are found by using an antiracism framework or by employing the tactics of colorblindness. But reality generally does not work that way. A better way is to learn in each situation what principles we can find in different approaches and to find solutions that address specific problems. I believe that the best way to find such answers is through what has been called [collaborative conversations](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tIbSmMMR1X8). A collaborative conversation [is a purposeful, outcome-driven conversation aimed at building on each other’s ideas](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/9-tips-creating-more-collaborative-conversations-patrick-kp-llcm/). We can use them to give everyone a seat at the table, listen to the concerns of others, and find solutions that do the best job of serving the interests of most individuals. These are real conversations where people are not dictated to about what they must accept but are encouraged to work together to solve our racial problems.

In our polarized society, power seems to swing between progressives supporting antiracism and conservatives supporting the notion of colorblindness. Programs started by one group can be ended by the other group when they gain power. As such, no set of solutions will be sustainable as they will be ended when the opposing party gains power. Finding solutions that address concerns across the political and racial spectrum is critical if we are going to find sustainable solutions. Collaborative conversations are a key way we can find those solutions.

What are the conflicting interests at play in most racial issues? Given that we have a history of racial abuse, people of color have a direct interest in ensuring that they do not suffer from the impact of that historical abuse. Many whites fear an overcorrection from these efforts, so that instead of a fair society, they will end up living in an unfair society that works against them. Both concerns are valid. That does not mean they have equal weight in all situations, but they should be recognized and adjudicated within a framework of collaborative conversations.

Let’s envision what this could look like. There is an ongoing conflict between law enforcement and African-American/Hispanic communities. How can we reduce or even eliminate this conflict? There are a variety of historical and institutional reasons why the experiences of African Americans and Hispanics differ from those of white Americans. [Stereotypes](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/black-brute-and-buck-stereotypes) of racial minorities as [violent](https://www.verywellmind.com/6-harmful-stereotypes-about-latin-americans-5113358), residential [segregation](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674018211), differential [sentencing](https://www.ussc.gov/research/research-reports/2023-demographic-differences-federal-sentencing), and [police profiling](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/3a5ieahzd170s5u7v49a3/fm_profiling_jul2.pdf?rlkey=3olgau8lbx0hxhh2iku74lr7n&e=1&dl=0) all contribute to making African Americans and Hispanics more suspicious of the police. So just ignoring these elements will not improve police relations in African-American and Hispanic communities. But many of us are not convinced the reforms offered by activists, such as defunding the police, are going to help.

But what if there were collaborative conversations between law enforcement and people in African-American and Hispanic communities? While some productive conversations do occur, such conversations are not commonplace. From these conversations relationships could develop that might help lessen tensions after police shootings. Law enforcement can also negotiate reforms that help them to alleviate some of the racialized issues that trouble those communities. Just having the conversations would also reduce the othering that occurs between both African Americans/ Hispanics and police officers. Would this not be a better outcome than weeks of protests or an institutional circling of the law enforcement wagons?

Take another hypothetical. Historically, we know that certain subjects in secondary school, such as history and language, have been written about from the perspectives of European-American and European-American culture. In response, many progressives have attempted to introduce corrective texts that bring in voices of color. This has led to a backlash by some whites complaining that their history is disappearing. Who could forget the school board protests of 2021? But even while that conflict was taking place, the solution was obvious. It was not to return to the previous books that celebrated Manifest Destiny. But it was also not to rewrite the text without regard to the interests of majority group members. The real solution is a mediated discussion in which whites and nonwhites can reach agreement and compromise on a set of texts that take into account everyone’s concerns. That is a solution that creates cross-racial, cross-political alliances rather than more enmity.

The crossroads that Andrews talks about is not the opportunity for us to adopt the colorblindness she advocates so strongly. There is a better path. One where we use the opportunity to deal with the problem of finding fairness after centuries of racial abuse to learn how to work together. We truly put aside our hatreds and suspicions so that we can solve racial problems in ways that are uniting rather than dividing.

Sounds wonderful, does it not? But I would be neglectful if I did not point out the large issue that will prevent us from moving towards productive conversations. Political polarization in our country prevents us from seeing those of the other political persuasion as fully human. President Trump has decisively made this polarization worse, but he was not the original source of this polarization. We must set aside the political animosities that so easily get entangled with our racial conflicts. Vote however you want. But understand that others will vote differently, and that does not make them evil. Holding on to notions of political moral superiority will keep us divided racially as well as politically.

Ultimately, the problem with Andrews’ approach is that it evinces a winner-takes-all mentality that will not bring people who disagree with her into the tent. They will fight against the dictates of colorblindness more than ever. If we can overcome our political polarization through mechanisms like collaborative conversations, we have a chance to take a road that brings us together rather than drives us farther apart. That is the road we need to travel.
