No, not an invasion or blockade. Chinese President [Xi Jinping](https://www.wsj.com/topics/person/xi-jinping) is craftier than that, at least for now, but he is using this week’s Washington summit to persuade President Trump to change the U.S. position on the status of [Taiwan](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/taiwan). But even this rhetorical shift would lead to bigger trouble down the road.

Mr. Xi on Thursday urged the U.S. to “adhere to the correct position of opposing Taiwan independence,” according to China’s official Xinhua News Agency. The current and longstanding U.S. position is that it “does not support Taiwan independence,” and that it expects relations between Beijing and Taipei to be resolved peacefully.

This is a semantic distinction with a big difference, which is why Mr. Xi wants Mr. Trump to change. China would use formal U.S. opposition to independence to raise doubts on the democratic island about American support. China would use it to justify further Chinese harassment of the island as its rowdy Legislature debates the island’s future.

The irony is that Mr. Xi and Beijing are the only parties currently preoccupied with Taiwan’s independence. The U.S. is content with the status quo that has prevailed for decades, and that previous Chinese leaders were happy to tolerate. He wants Trump to change the U.S. position on the island. Bad idea.

As for the Taiwanese, no major party is pressing for formal independence. A majority on the island want nothing to do with rule by the Chinese Communist Party, especially as they have viewed with alarm the crushing of China’s “one-country, two systems” promise to Hong Kong. But they are content to be left alone to govern themselves, rather than formally declare themselves independent. They understand the risks from the superior military firepower on the mainland.

Mr. Xi may have been inadvertently encouraged to make his new demand by Mr. Trump. After meeting Mr. Xi in Beijing in May Mr. Trump said he viewed a pending $14 billion arms sale to Taiwan as a “very good negotiating chip.” Negotiating for what is the question.

Mr. Xi may be offering to trade something Mr. Trump wants for the combination of formal U.S. opposition to Taiwan’s independence and a delay or cancellation of the arms package. Perhaps he’d offer to stop buying Iranian and Russian oil, or buy more U.S. products. But those promises aren’t worth the trade.

Either of those U.S. concessions on Taiwan would shake confidence in Taipei over U.S. support. Beijing would raise the volume on its propaganda aimed at Taiwan that the only way it can avoid an invasion or blockade is to agree to some kind of surrender to Beijing, a la the Hong Kong precedent.

The U.S. change would also shock its East Asian allies that count on American deterrence against Chinese aggression. That includes Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, Singapore, assorted Pacific island nations and even Australia. With its high-quality computer chip fabrication plants, Taiwan is a crucial part of the U.S. economic and security supply chain. Let’s hope Mr. Trump doesn’t fall for Mr. Xi’s Taiwan independence deception.
