I do not like the term “marginalized” to describe black Americans. I consider the label “marginalized” disaffecting and alienating. It frames one’s existence in the strange world of contrived oppression. It is a strange word. It is not a word one’s parents or grandparents would have used for self-understanding.

A few months ago, I was reading a copy of the D.C. Bar magazine. I was killing time and anticipating a light read. I came across an essay about local D.C. lawyers and the writer characterized local black lawyers as “marginalized.” This word choice made no sense to me. D.C. was a majority black city until recently. According to the latest census records, D.C. is around 41% black today. I lived in D.C. in 1984 when the population was 70% black. I don’t think black people in D.C. think of themselves as marginalized. This applies even more so to black lawyers. How many American cities can claim a black law school like Howard Law School in existence since the 1870s? Black lawyers have been around in D.C. since George B. Vashon was admitted to the U.S. Supreme Court Bar in 1867 and the D.C. Bar in 1869. Characterizing black lawyers in D.C. as marginalized seemed blind to black legal history and insulting to boot. There were generational black lawyer families in D.C. like the Houstons, the Hasties and the Williams. Perhaps, 25% of the federal judges in D.C. at all levels are black. U.S. Supreme Court Justices Clarence Thomas and Ketanji Brown Jackson both hail from the U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in D.C. Spottswood W. Robinson III became Chief Judge of the U.S. Court of Appeals for D.C. on May 7, 1981. William B. Bryant became Chief Judge of the U.S. District Court for D.C. in 1977.

Where is the woeful marginalization of black lawyers in D.C.?

\*  
We should not use the label “marginalization” when referring to black Americans for several reasons. First, marginalization implies some mysterious force field that keeps black people down and oppressed today. This is a falsehood. No invisible force prevented the building of an epic institution like Howard Law School since January 6, 1869. Marginalized people do not create law schools that change the world. People of agency like John Mercer Langston and Charles Hamilton Houston do such things.

Second, there is no single status of Blackness on planet earth. If there are over 1 billion black people in the world, there are over 1 billion life stories that span a spectrum from the most destitute to the most wealthy and Old Money. No single label should ever be applied to individuals living their lives every day. If you read my essay *[Belle with a Dash of Lennon](https://twyman.substack.com/p/belle-with-a-dash-of-lennon)*, one can see that marginalization doesn’t really explain one’s inner life.

Third, “marginalization” was shoe horned into the public discourse as a way to squeeze all black people into a box consistent with oppression. But suppose some (many-?) individuals do not engage the world as oppressed victims? Do we deny human dignity to individuals when we lazily write of black lawyers in D.C. as some marginalized blob of faceless souls? I think so. Ergo my visceral revulsion upon sight of the word “marginalized” in the D.C. Bar magazine.

Fourth, to perceive individuals as marginalized erases the individual soul. Has one met every black lawyer in D.C. and throughout D.C. history? Isn’t it a sign of hubris to ascribe a label of marginalized to thousands of black lawyers sight unseen? Isn’t it prejudice and bigotry to presume one knows everything one needs to know about a group with a throw away label of marginalized? There is no invisible force today pushing black lawyers to the margins of economic and political life. There simply isn’t. When one writes black lawyers in D.C. are marginalized, one degrades the authentic experience of black D.C. lawyers.

The last white Mayor of D.C. left office in 1967. I was six years old. Where is the marginalization?

Finally, I fault Critical Race Theory (CRT) for creating these make-believe labels that do not accord with reality. Once again, how are black lawyers pushed to the margins in D.C. today? The opposite is probably true, truth be told. Since Vashon’s admission to the U.S. Supreme Court Bar in 1867 and the founding of Howard Law School in 1869, D.C. has been a city on a hill for black lawyers. Review my series of essays on pioneer black lawyers. *[Pioneer Black Lawyers: Season Five, Episode 17](https://twyman.substack.com/p/pioneer-black-lawyers-season-5-episode-2e9?utm_source=publication-search) [Pioneer Black Lawyers: Season Five, Episode 12](https://twyman.substack.com/p/pioneer-black-lawyers-season-5-episode-f01?utm_source=publication-search) [Pioneer Black Lawyers: Season Five, Episode 13](https://twyman.substack.com/p/pioneer-black-lawyers-season-5-episode-175?utm_source=publication-search)* It is truly stunning how many pioneer black lawyers had a link to Howard. Even George Vashon was the first black professor at Howard in 1867.

**Conclusion:** I do not like the label “marginalized.” The term is misleading and mildly insulting to the best of our black American past. The last people to be characterized as marginalized are black lawyers in D.C. I challenge anyone to identify an American city more identified with black lawyers than D.C. From George Vashon to Howard Law School to Charles Hamilton Houston to William Henry Hastie, Jr. to Spottswood W. Robinson III to Justices Clarence Thomas and Ketanji Brown Jackson, black lawyers have been at the top of their game in D.C.

Where is the marginalization of black lawyers in D.C.? We should be focused on the impact of labels on black American flourishing. Otherwise, we are subjecting black lawyers in D.C. and others to a cognitive virus.
