Student protests are nothing new. Nor are violent riots on campus, like those organized by people inside and outside the University of Oxford on Tuesday evening. The intimidation of students and others was intended to prevent people from hearing from the academic Nathan Cofnas. He leaped to fame earlier this year when he was the first academic to blow the whistle on the fraud committed by another academic, Jason Arday of the University of Cambridge. Cofnas’s reporting revealed to the public what was long suspected within Cambridge’s walls: Arday had fabricated important parts of his life and career, and was unqualified for his job, not to mention his celebrity status.

Arday later took his own life as public scrutiny increased, and his suicide has led to a massive inflation of claims about his death and against those who exposed his fraud. These have been made by figures as prominent as the principal of Homerton College, Cambridge, Lord Simon Woolley, who told a demonstration in London’s Trafalgar Square that Arday, who was black, had been the subject of a “lynching” and a “public assassination.” These are strong terms to throw around, even metaphorically—which is what Woolley now claims he intended them to mean.

Not all of the demonstrators who turned out by the hundreds on the streets of Oxford on Tuesday night will have taken these words metaphorically. Enraged activists formed a human cordon, blocking not only attendees of the event but also other students trying to access their dormitories. When one student attempted to pass, he recalls that a protester rebuked him, saying, “Don’t try and break the picket line, mate. It makes you look as though you hate black people.”

In their eyes, Cofnas is guilty of two crimes. The first is his decision to speak up about the plagiarism, false CV claims, and other fabulist aspects of Arday’s career. Presumably, Arday’s supporters think either that Arday was eminently qualified for a Cambridge professorship despite the evidence, or that academics should be allowed to get away with such fibs if they fit the criteria of diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI). Cofnas’s employer, Ghent University, appears to agree. [It suspended him](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cevmrjd9lj1o) after Arday’s death, saying it intends to investigate him for discrimination.

---

The deeper problem with Cofnas, in the eyes of his opponents, is that he has researched and written about the relationship between race and IQ in the past. If there is one subject in our era likely to bring down the furies, it is this one.

The question of IQ differentials between racial groupings is probably the most verboten subject of our time. Prominent academics have occasionally addressed the issue. Charles Murray and Richard Herrnstein famously explored the question in a chapter of their 1994 book *[The Bell Curve](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780684824291)*. Others, including [Steven Pinker](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2009/jun/18/iq-steven-pinker-blank-slate) and [James Watson](https://www.the-independent.com/news/science/james-watson-racism-sexism-dna-race-intelligence-genetics-double-helix-a8725556.html), have spoken on the matter, as have people as prominent as David Reich, a professor of genetics at Harvard. In 2018, Reich even [addressed race and IQ](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/03/23/opinion/sunday/genetics-race.html) in a piece published in *The New York Times*. There, Reich argued that academics ought to explore differences in average intelligence among different populations, while striving to avoid stereotypes and hasty conclusions.

Reich laid out his research in [an accompanying book](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9781101873465) and explained that the job of someone doing the research he does is simply to describe the facts. He conceded that he had “deep sympathy for the concern that genetic discoveries could be misused to justify racism.” But as the backlash to him and that article showed, many people appeared to believe that experts like Reich were breaching a dam even by saying this much. The views of critics such as Ezra Klein, then [writing at](https://www.vox.com/policy-and-politics/2018/3/27/15695060/sam-harris-charles-murray-race-iq-forbidden-knowledge-podcast-bell-curve) *[Vox](https://www.vox.com/policy-and-politics/2018/3/27/15695060/sam-harris-charles-murray-race-iq-forbidden-knowledge-podcast-bell-curve)*, ranged from denying that the research was valid to expressing concern that the ignorant masses would interpret any link between race and IQ as a license to discriminate against any group that was suggested to have lower average intelligence.

In one respect, the fear was understandable. Once facts are out in the public square, nobody has very much control over how they can be used. But knowledge cannot be “secret,” particularly in the era of social media. And the fact that someone like Cofnas—who has described himself as a “race realist”—was speaking about DEI in his Oxford lecture is no coincidence.

Some years ago, I warned about exactly this scenario erupting. Specifically, I wrote that if the era of DEI were to continue, those who found themselves most beleaguered by that ideology—specifically, young, straight, white men—would be likely to pick up an alluring counterargument. Given the breadth and depth of DEI’s ambitions, affecting everything from education to job hiring, those who found themselves most harmed by the prejudices of DEI would in turn reach for the heaviest and most harmful weapon they could find to counterpunch. This was always likely to be the question of race and IQ.

I predicted this for several reasons. The first was that evidence about race and IQ has been shut down more often than it has been debated. The violence and thuggery on the streets of Oxford this week reinforce this point, as have the multiple attempts to [shut down](https://www.nationalreview.com/corner/charles-murray-students-college-caved-controversy-and-disinvited-him-why-youre-getting), and in one instance [even assault](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/05/24/us/middlebury-college-charles-murray-bell-curve.html), Charles Murray.

If academics and students think that someone holding Cofnas’s views is wrong, then they should be able to refute his claims. But that is not what they tried to do. They tried to prevent him from speaking to a small number of students in their spare time and then tried to stop students from getting into the lecture hall to hear him. This is not evidence of intellectual strength. It is evidence of intellectual failure. And that encourages interest in the forbidden subject.

The second reason this countermovement seemed so predictable was that this weapon is one whose power comes closest to that of DEI. Advocates of DEI claim that people should be judged throughout their lives not by their capabilities but by their characteristics. It inevitably leads to people from majority populations feeling that they are being discriminated against—because they are. Reams of books and articles have been written refuting the precepts and practice of DEI. But for anyone with limited time and patience, taking apart these arguments on their own terms may not suffice. Much more tempting, and harmful, is to turn around to the proponents of DEI and say, “You don’t want to talk about capabilities because your own innate characteristics are not good. In fact, you are genetically inferior.”

If academics and students think that someone holding Cofnas’s views is wrong, then they should be able to refute his claims.

I do not say that this is Cofnas’s view. But it is a view that may spread among many young white people. The eruption of interest in race and IQ differentials confirms this. For at least a decade, anybody who has appeared in public debates about any number of subjects will have found themselves up against this subject. There are many academic disagreements about genetics, but very few make it onto the opinion pages, let alone the streets. This one does because it is effectively an unexploded bomb waiting to go off beneath society as a whole.

The proponents of DEI have made sweeping claims about groups of people using terms like “privilege.” Each time they have done so, they have provoked their opponents into making equally sweeping claims of their own. It is hard for someone who has claimed that “all white people are X” to turn around and tell other people that they are not now allowed to make similarly sweeping claims. So that is what some opponents of DEI are seeking to do. In doing so, many are likely to ignore the considerable variables within racial IQ differences and focus instead on the [evidence that suggests](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/403261570_Meta-Analysis_of_American_Race_Differences_in_Intelligence) that some racial groups have, on average, higher IQs than others.

The obvious thing for anyone who believes that people like Cofnas are wrong in their claims and research is to produce claims and research of their own. But the treatment of someone who was until recently a very obscure junior academic, and the unbelievable physical and institutional firepower aimed against him, is likely to have the opposite effect to that intended.

For what it is worth, my own observation of these debates is that much can be told by the way someone debates IQ differentials. If they survey them as the ethical and moral minefield they are, then they will discuss them in a spirit of interest and concern. If they describe their findings—accurate or otherwise—with glee, then you can be fairly sure they are engaged in another game. The only way to surmise the tone in which someone like Cofnas addresses these issues would be to hear him.

His opponents think they know what he is saying. In fact, they have simply closed themselves off from hearing him. In doing so, they have set up the conditions for many more people to listen to him. And the violence that greeted Cofnas on Tuesday suggests that much of his audience, present and future, will want more than some statistical evidence at their side. They will want to settle on the evidence and go to war with it. If they do, they will be able to claim, with some justification, that it is not a war they started.
