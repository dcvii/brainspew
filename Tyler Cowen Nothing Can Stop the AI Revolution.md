Are you worried about an AI market crash or a company going bankrupt? Don’t be. Just as with railroads 150 years ago, nothing can stop technological progress.

A friend recently asked in a group chat, “What could stop or significantly slow down the advance of AI?” My answer to that question was simple: “Not much.”

The artificial intelligence revolution has cleared many major hurdles so far, and there is a clear path for further progress. The exact speed of advance will vary with economic and political circumstances, but the logic of artificial intelligence will do more than anything else to set the pace.

I realize that there are many people who fear that we are in an AI bubble, and I don’t dismiss the possibility. The latest scare came last week when an AI-oriented hedge fund, Situational Awareness, lost $35 billion [in the space of two weeks](https://www.thefp.com/p/how-to-lose-35-billion) in July. Although in truth, as I’ll get to shortly, I don’t view that as a particularly worrisome event.

Instead, I’d point to history. In the early 2000s, the dot-com bubble burst. In the mid-aughts, the housing bubble burst. As is always the case, few realized that these were bubbles until boom gave way to bust. Even so, important internet companies like Amazon, eBay, and Google continued to grow, and homes continued to be built.

Given that most of the major AI companies are spending billions while counting on future revenue that has yet to materialize, it is not crazy to think that one or more of them might go bankrupt. I am not here to give investment advice, and so I will not speak to the probability of this happening. The important point, from a social point of view, is that the foundation upon which AI models are being built will continue to exist—and to grow.

---

Indeed, bankruptcy will likely be little more than a bump in the road. There are multiple well-capitalized parties who will happily step in and take over the ailing entity. A short list of potential buyers could include Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Nvidia, not to mention Elon Musk. These are all parties that have their own AI models.

Then there is the potential involvement of the U.S. government, which already has assumed ownership shares in multiple tech companies, most prominently Intel. I [do not favor](https://www.thefp.com/p/tyler-cowen-trump-seizes-the-means-of-production-at-intel) these partial nationalizations, but I do not doubt that the feds would bail out a major AI company if it came to that. Such a move would be easy enough to justify on the grounds of national security—though whether or not the bailout would make us more secure is questionable.

A stroll through history shows what happens when a new technology so excites the imagination of the public that they drive up stock prices far beyond any sensible valuation. In the late 19th century, various railroads [went bankrupt](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/grant-panic/) —but that didn’t stop rail from knitting together much of the world. In the early part of the 20th century, there were [over 100 auto companies](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/america-on-the-move/online/americans-adopt-auto/building-fixing-cars) in the U.S. By the end of the 1930s, Ford, General Motors (GM), and Chrysler controlled 80 percent of the market. Bankruptcy, it turns out, doesn’t stop progress.

A similar logic holds for all the companies that built the AI infrastructure, whether we are talking about data centers, cloud computing, chips, energy, or other inputs into production. There is plenty of capital to step in and support any part of the AI supply chain that might be experiencing economic trouble.

Chips are perhaps the most vulnerable part of the supply chain; a Chinese blockade or invasion of Taiwan could create a major economic crisis for America’s technology industry, especially AI, which is so heavily dependent on the highest-quality chips from Taiwan’s major chip maker, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC). While there are some substitutes from Samsung, TSMC products supply the most advanced AI models.

The railroads, fiber-optic networks, and the internet are all still with us, despite the financial setbacks experienced by each along the way.

I view this as the worst-case scenario for AI progress, but it probably would not set back AI progress by more than a few years. Indeed, the very existence of China as an AI competitor gives further credence to the notion that AI progress will continue. It would be tragic if the AI lead fell to the Chinese, and very bad for global geopolitics, as future American influence would plummet. On the other hand, if China ends up using AI to cure cancer, it will not seem altogether tragic either. It’s another path along which AI will progress, even if it doesn’t take place in the U.S.

Critics like to stress the staggering amount of upfront money the major AI companies are investing in compute over the next year. By [one estimate](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-cost-of-compute-a-7-trillion-dollar-race-to-scale-data-centers) (and I do stress that word *estimate*), the data center build-out alone could cost perhaps $7 trillion by 2030. The commercial returns on that money are, of course, uncertain.

But from a social point of view, the more money put in up front, the easier it will be to sustain those data centers once they’ve been built, whether or not the initial investments turn a profit. I think of this process as “getting the costs out of the way.” From a historical point of view, the railroads, fiber-optic networks, and the internet are all still with us, despite the financial setbacks experienced by each along the way.

By getting the up-front costs out of the way, the companies are showing just how valuable the product is, and how fast progress can be made. About two years ago, these AI models struggled with basic arithmetic, but now they are proving and disproving math conjectures—hitherto unsolvable by humans—at a rapid clip.

Just last week, [front-page news stories](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/31/business/situational-awareness-leopold-aschenbrenner.html?smid=tw-share) reported that the AI-specific hedge fund Situational Awareness had to sell much of its portfolio, due to excess debt. But that event made me more bullish on AI, not less. Multiple parties were interested in the portfolio, and the day after the event, many AI-related stocks saw major gains. The sector as a whole did not panic, because so many investors know that the AI models themselves are highly effective.

---

Another possible roadblock: States might ban, restrict, or delay the construction of data centers, as New York recently did with its [one-year moratorium](https://www.thefp.com/p/new-yorks-ban-on-the-future). Admittedly, this is a real concern. On the other hand, we have 50 states, many of them hungry for revenue, and I do not think all of them are going to pull the plugs on these projects.

Or take the unlikely scenario where the U.S. government bans the release of future models, on the grounds they are too dangerous. That would make us sitting ducks for foreign cyberattacks, and so it is unlikely to happen. But even then progress would continue with open source models in China, and eventually in other parts of the world as well.

Or what if the global economy blows up? That is another outlier event, but again, the lessons from history are clear. The Great Depression of the 1930s was terrible, but it was followed by amazing progress in computers, atomic power, and modern airplanes, among other developments.

Here’s the key point: It is a mistake to view today’s AI moment through the lens of investing—who or what is going to make money. To do so is to mistake the forest for the trees. Market sentiments rise and fall, but in the grand scheme of things, the market will not doom the AI revolution. Instead, if you think in terms of the broad sweep of history, you will see that everything is on track, and we are putting more and more of the transition costs behind us.

The juggernaut is here, and I, for one, plan on enjoying it.
