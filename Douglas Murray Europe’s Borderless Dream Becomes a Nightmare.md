The West has sleepwalked into mass migration for more than a decade. Will a glaring breach of its borders jar it awake?

***The images were unforgettable. More than 60,000 migrants entered the tiny Spanish territory of Ceuta from Morocco over the course of 24 hours.***

***It’s arguably the most vivid illustration we’ve ever seen of the migrant crisis that has transformed the face of Europe in one decade and that continues to consume the continent’s politics. And no one saw it all coming more clearly than our own Douglas Murray. His 2017 book, [The Strange Death of Europe](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9781472958051), chronicles the errors of judgment that led Western leaders so carelessly to open their doors to unlimited migration.***

***Will the brief storming of Ceuta prompt a reckoning among European elites, or will the crisis linger on, with the same denials and failed solutions from the leadership class? Read Douglas’s take on what comes next. —The Editors***

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1b0k!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F984aef3a-0fa3-46dd-b890-83ebd90183b3_1320x30.png)

Eleven years ago, almost to the day, Europe was experiencing a migration wave that threatened to make the continent buckle. In the space of a matter of weeks, more than a million people from Africa, the Middle East, and the Far East made their way into Europe, principally by crossing to the islands of Greece and Italy: “the soft underbelly of Europe.”

As various European countries started to panic, it was German chancellor Angela Merkel who decided to control the day. “ *Wir schaffen das*,” she said in August 2015. “We can do this.” Merkel and some of her counterparts in the EU effectively suspended normal immigration procedures and opened the borders of Europe to anyone who wanted to come.

In those days, the route into Europe via Spain was relatively quiet. That was because the option of getting into Europe via Central and Southeastern Europe had been opened up, so there was less need for migrants to use the Spanish option. There were also effective agreements with the Moroccan authorities—unlike those in lawless Libya, for instance—which saved Spain from the most challenging of the 2015 waves.

Still, Spain remained uniquely vulnerable. Not least because, like Greece and Italy, the country had an accident of geography. This was the fact that, as well as being a remarkably short sail from North Africa, Spain has two territories actually in Northern Africa: the enclaves of Ceuta and Melilla.

They are peculiar places, left behind in more ways than one, and surrounded by huge wire fences. If you walk along these fences, you can see people from Africa—in particular, people who have come up from sub-Saharan Africa—walking along the fences on the other side. Always they are hoping to find a point of weakness in the fence, or a moment to break through. Ceuta and Melilla were volcanoes waiting to erupt.

---

This week, Ceuta finally did explode—and into a wider global awareness. Footage of migrants pushing by the thousands to get into the territory has caused many people to say that the subject of my 2017 bestseller, *The Strange Death of Europe*, seems to have been made into a film. In reality, it didn’t need to be. What I wrote about in that book was based on what I had seen with my own eyes. In a way, it is a good thing that the wider world is getting an opportunity to see again what I tried to describe and warn about. By late Friday, Spanish authorities claimed that most of the migrants had either left willingly or been pushed out by security forces. They were much less forthcoming about the many ways their government invited the crisis.

The immediate cause of the crisis in Ceuta is a decision by the Spanish government to normalize the status of migrants who have already broken into Spain illegally. The far-left Spanish government—which receives far less international attention and criticism than it deserves—has been stoking this situation for years. As well as corrupting and politicizing the Spanish justice system, the government of Pedro Sánchez has advertised itself as open to illegal migration. Perhaps Sánchez, like many left- and right-wing leaders in Europe, underestimated the number of people who wanted to come into his country. But by failing to deport illegals and, in fact, normalizing their status, he has done as much as any European leader could have done to increase the draw and thus the flow of people into Spain and therefore the rest of Europe.

This week, Ceuta finally did explode—and into a wider global awareness.

Sánchez’s European counterparts have already responded. Italian prime minister Giorgia Meloni, among other members of the government, has condemned the Spanish authorities for refusing to defend the borders of Europe and suggested that they might throw up their own border defenses to stop the waves of migrants into Spain from moving into other parts of Europe.

In reality, there is very little that they can do. In the late 20th century, Europe dreamed of a borderless continent. The Schengen Agreement made movement between European countries seamless. You didn’t even have to show a passport if you drove—for instance—from France into Italy. But a borderless Europe does not work if Europe has no external borders either. As my repeated trips to southern Europe have proved to me, most of the migrants who arrive in Greece, Italy, and Spain will not stay in these countries. They are merely a doorway into Europe. Most of the migrants want to head north for the larger welfare payments. Germany and other northern European countries will therefore be the ones who will end up paying the most for the decisions made by Pedro Sánchez.

---

The situation is, of course, unsustainable. Like many other European countries, Spain has a national debt that exceeds its annual gross domestic product. It cannot afford to pay to house illegal migrants. The migrants themselves will never put back in taxes what they take out in welfare. The vast majority of those who arrived illegally in the past decade remain a net drain on the state. And that is before you even begin to address the social and civilizational challenges that such movement brings. But Spain—like the rest of Europe—will continue to try to sustain the unsustainable. Something that I have come to realize can go on for a very long time. It has gone on for more than a decade already. Only four days ago in these pages, I described the spate of terrorist attacks conducted in Europe by Islamist migrants. And just as every week’s news includes stories of the worst effects of Europe’s illegal migration, so each week’s news ignores the long-term unsustainability of the economic migration model Europe has put in place.

Pro-migration activists like to talk about figures. Figures which, as I showed in my book, they either invent or actively lie about. But Europeans know that the official figures are baloney. They use their eyes and see what is going on. And they have long come to the conclusion that, in fact, “We can’t do this.” Whether the political leaders in Europe catch up with what the voters can see with their own eyes will decide no less a question than whether the continent of Europe lives or dies.
