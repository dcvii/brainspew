I’ve opposed them for most of my life. But right now, I’m considering getting a handgun.

***“You’re getting killed in the comments!” It’s not a text any writer wants to receive, but I wasn’t surprised. After all, I’d just written [a piece](https://www.thefp.com/p/america-is-better-than-this) that was gently critical of America, and Free Press readers are a patriotic bunch; of course you leapt to your country’s defense.***

***For context: I’m British, I’d just been in an active-shooter situation in Washington, D.C., and I was reflecting on the Second Amendment. “Is it worth it letting civilians have guns? Does it really make this country more free?” I wrote. “Can you explain it to me? I want to know.”***

***I meant what I said. I genuinely wanted to hear from someone who believes what [half the American population believes](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/07/24/key-facts-about-americans-and-guns/): That it’s important to protect a citizen’s right to bear arms. The whole point—and the privilege—of being a journalist is getting into the hearts and minds of people who think differently.***

***Which brings me back to that text I got—the one about being killed in the comments.***

***It was from my friend and colleague Larissa Phillips, who I’ve edited since she joined The Free Press two years ago—and who happened to be perfectly placed to answer my appeal: “Help me understand.” Because once upon a time, Larissa herself was vehemently anti-gun—and now, she’s planning to get one.***

***A thread that runs through a lot of Larissa’s [writing](https://www.thefp.com/w/larissa-phillips) here at The Free Press is how moving from bright-blue Brooklyn to a red area of upstate New York changed her perspective— [on Republicans](https://www.thefp.com/p/whatever-happens-love-thy-neighbor-trump-kamala), [on marriage](https://www.thefp.com/p/larissa-phillips-becoming-a-wife-marriage), [on America itself](https://www.thefp.com/p/im-finally-hanging-my-american-flag-america-at-250). In today’s essay, she writes that when she left New York City, she wouldn’t even let her kids have a water gun, but moving to a part of the country where firearms are a part of daily life, she slowly came to question her assumptions about the Americans who owned and used them.***

***This is the story of how she fired a bullet for the first time. —Freya Sanders***

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HLyR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fff218773-0b15-4e0f-8c55-4d66fb46ec40_1320x30.png)

I jammed my palm right up against the grip, wrapped fingers around my firing hand, extended both arms, and squinted at the target through my glasses. With all systems in place, there was nothing left to do but pull the trigger. *Bam!* At a velocity of about 800 mph, the bullet shot down the barrel of the gun in my hand and seemed to instantaneously punch through the target. I checked my handiwork. A tiny hole had appeared just a couple inches south of the white circle in the middle. Not bad for my first time ever firing a pistol. I looked at my instructor. “Can I go again?”

Until very recently, I could not have imagined taking lessons on how to shoot a handgun. I have spent most of my life extremely opposed to every single type of firearm. Guns are involved in most murders; they escalate volatile situations, land hotheaded young men in prison, and kill innocent people. I grew up the child of two anti-war idealists who deplored violence, and as a suburban kid, I had little exposure to hunting, which I imagined as a pastime for drunk slobs who wanted to massacre wildlife. When I grew up and became a parent myself, I was haunted by the specter of school shootings. The Columbine High School massacre in Colorado happened just two weeks after my first child was born in 1999; I held my tiny infant against me as I took in the horror on the news.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oEK1!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F424f9598-6aff-41e3-b359-006a450019c9_1024x555.jpeg)

When I grew up and became a parent myself, I was haunted by the specter of school shootings, like the Columbine High School massacre in Colorado. (Rita Reed/Star Tribune via Getty Images)

Not long after that, I set down roots in Park Slope, Brooklyn, where the default position on guns was: They’re bad; let’s get rid of them. We wondered: *What if we stopped making them? Can we hold gun manufacturers liable for gunshot deaths? What about a buy-back program like Australia did?* I readily participated in these conversations, even though I was not as extreme as some of my friends, who said things like: “No guns. *Ever*.” I thought *some* people should have guns, but only highly trained, background-checked professionals. At the time, I didn’t know anyone who had a gun—and the prospect of owning one myself never crossed my mind.

But since moving from one of the most liberal places in the country to a farm in a red part of upstate New York, my views on firearms have shifted dramatically. In fact, I’m actually thinking about getting a handgun.

My reasons are mundane and apolitical. First, I live on a working farm. At the very least, we should have a rifle to shoot predators or put injured animals out of their misery. But we’ve been putting off buying one for years because, despite all my reasoning, owning a gun, being near a gun, even talking about getting a gun, still just feels... *wrong.*

But recently, a second reason for wanting one has arisen. I’m worried about security.

In the last six months, we’ve lost two of our three dogs to natural causes. One was a 100-pound livestock guardian who patrolled our farm like an elite security force. He was looking for foxes and coyotes, but anyone who didn’t belong on our property would have been treated like a code-red alert. The other was a large, goofy pit-bull mix who loved everyone, but his massive chest and boxy head identified him as a breed with a dangerous reputation. These two were just the latest in a series of sweet but formidable-looking dogs I’ve had in the last 30 years. I didn’t get any of them for personal protection, but they always made me feel safe when I was home alone, whether I was letting an unfamiliar man into my Brooklyn house to read the electric meter, or, upstate, opening the door to random strangers knocking to offer landscaping services. “I’ll hold my dogs,” I’d say, as they emitted a ferocious racket behind me.

With just one docile dog remaining, my rural home feels nerve-rackingly vulnerable, and a gun is starting to feel like a good idea. I wouldn’t be the first American to come to this conclusion. Nearly a third of the country owns guns, and around [88 percent of this cohort](https://news.gallup.com/poll/357329/gun-owners-increasingly-cite-crime-reason-ownership.aspx) name personal safety as a reason for having one. (This is up from 67 percent in 2005.) Still, getting a handgun feels so extreme.

And would it actually make me safer—or just *feel* safer?

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HLyR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fff218773-0b15-4e0f-8c55-4d66fb46ec40_1320x30.png)

My recent ancestors wouldn’t have understood my dilemma. Before I was born, many of the older men in my family were hunters; when I was a child in the 1970s, my great-uncle still kept his rifles hung over the mantel next to the duck calls and decoys. My grandfather, on the other side of the family, kept bird dogs as pets even though he’d retired from hunting years earlier. My mother told me that during her own childhood, this same grandfather kept a loaded rifle leaned against her bedroom wall, the better to shoot the groundhog that kept invading his garden.

But my family also suffered from proximity to guns. My great-grandfather on my father’s side was shot and killed in a holdup in Carbondale, Illinois, in 1927. It is because of his death that my family exists. After his father was killed, my then 14-year-old grandfather drove himself and his mother halfway across the country to the East Coast, where she knew people who would take them in. Some years later, he met the woman who would become my grandmother.

---

I sometimes wonder if my future grandmother and my future grandfather connected over the issue of gun violence when they first met, because *her* father had also been shot—on a commuter train somewhere between Newark, New Jersey, and Manhattan—when he attempted to intervene in an unfolding crime. He ended up in the hospital but survived.

My family lore is not that unusual. Nineteen percent of Americans say that they have a relative who [has been killed by a gun](https://www.kff.org/other-health/americans-experiences-with-gun-related-violence-injuries-and-deaths/), and plenty more know someone who’s been harmed or threatened by one. My husband’s sister, for instance, is also a survivor of gun violence: She was shot in the stomach as an 8-year-old, in an accident at a friend’s house.

Would getting a gun actually make me safer—or just *feel* safer?

But what happened to my relatives was rarely discussed and never used as a political capital. My parents rebelled against gun culture, but I think they were affected more by the era they came of age into than their family’s experiences. Manhattan in the 1960s, where my parents lived as young adults, was neither the time nor place for guns, especially if you were pursuing the life of an urban professional; guns were for criminals, or country rubes. (Although, somehow, the liberal-idealist objection to guns didn’t apply when they were wielded by, say, the Black Panthers.) There was no hunting for varmints in the city or its nearby suburbs, where my parents brought us up. Food came from the store, not the forest. By the time my siblings and I came along, toy guns were still normal—my brother had a cap gun, a toy pistol that produces a bang and a puff of smoke when the trigger is pulled—but real guns were anathema. In our regular visits to our grandparents’ house in North Carolina, we all learned to fish, gutting and frying up the trout we caught, but I never saw my grandfather use his rifles.

I arrived in college having thoroughly absorbed my parents’ anti-gun stance—and discovered this was the water everybody swam in at my East Coast liberal arts institution. A glimpse of a wild animal filled us with appreciation for the natural world, not a deranged desire to *kill* it. My senior year, an oddball kid shot a deer and butchered it in his manor house’s bathtub, which filled all of us with horror. We assumed he was a psychopath, as we thought all hunters probably were to some degree. And even when my friends and I participated in Take Back the Night marches to promote women’s safety on campus, it never occurred to us that we could arm ourselves.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!c35b!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F635bb349-e22e-4d9d-8896-9f2e2e8147f9_1200x800.jpeg)

In 2010, my family moved to one of the many places in America where firearms are just a part of ordinary life. (Cindy Schultz for The Free Press. )

It was the same in Brooklyn, in the early aughts: We were all so against guns that we didn’t give our kids toy weapons. I have no doubt my son would have loved a cap gun, but he didn’t even get a water gun; instead, when he was 5 or 6, I gave him a plastic dolphin that spouted a weak spurt of water when you pulled the trigger. Along with so many around me, I held on to my parents’ idealistic view that the world would be a better place if only we gave up our attachment to violence. Through those years, as the number of school shootings increased, beliefs about guns became a mark of one’s tribe. On the left, we passionately railed against them; on the right, they furiously defended the [Second Amendment](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-2/).

Then, in 2010, my family moved to one of the many places in America where firearms are just a part of ordinary life.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HLyR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fff218773-0b15-4e0f-8c55-4d66fb46ec40_1320x30.png)

Living on a farm in a semirural area, we learned quickly that when you’re raising livestock, there are times when you need to kill an animal—either for slaughter or to provide a merciful death. And as even a hippie-type farmer I know once allowed: “The quickest way to put down a suffering animal is with a bullet.”

Early in our farming years, we would borrow a.22 to kill the pigs we’d raised for meat. My husband was invariably the one to deploy the rifle; I don’t think I ever even touched one. (I remember one of the pigs falling to the ground, instantly dead, as the friends who were there to help with the butchering burst into applause, relieved it had gone so smoothly.) Sometimes we would ask a neighbor to come over with his rifle, or offer a few bucks to a guy down the road to do a quick job. One time, a friend came over to kill an old goat who had been on her deathbed for days. By then we had seen enough animals suffer—from vicious attacks by predators, or brutal diseases—to know how preferable a gunshot is to a slow death, and I invariably felt relief when I saw the light leave their eyes.

---

Of course, we could leave it to the professionals. But it can take hours for a farm vet to arrive at our house; I’d rather have the ability to end a tragedy quickly. Recently, a guy I know told me about waking up in the middle of the night to the sound of unearthly screams from his barn. He found one of his horses had somehow horribly broken its leg, a fatal accident. Instead of waiting for a vet, he was able to immediately end the horse’s agony with his personal firearm. It was hearing stories like this that made me believe it was a good idea for certain civilians to have guns.

Living upstate, I also got to know many hunters—not a single one of whom was the type of idiot I’d imagined years earlier, who would use hunting as an opportunity to get drunk while holding a loaded rifle. They were mostly outdoor types who liked retreating into the woods for hours on end, skillfully taking down a deer, and providing several months’ worth of venison for their families. Sure, some of them were rowdy guys who perhaps enjoyed the noise of shooting, and the power of it, but I had stopped thinking that made them bad people. I got used to receiving gifts of game. My vet’s assistant would offer us his own homemade venison jerky when we brought in one of our dogs. One November, a friend offered us the second deer he’d shot that season, because his vegetarian wife refused to fit another one in the freezer. Just yesterday, my grown-up daughter and her boyfriend brought over a bag of frozen venison steaks and sausages, a gift from the boyfriend’s parents. I have long since learned how to cook venison and love including it in my meals.

Through the years, rifles had come to seem uncontroversial to me, part of the rhythms of a rural community. But handguns? They were an entirely different thing. Handguns seemed like tools for murder, their owners the kind of people who would stalk their ex-girlfriend or let their kid play with guns. I had little reason to make this judgement; I’d never even met someone who owned a handgun—as far as I knew, of course. While rifles had come to seem like a familiar and necessary tool, this type of firearm remained alien, and connected only to terrible outcomes.

But when you live in the country, it slowly dawns on you that you have to be able to keep yourself safe.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HLyR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fff218773-0b15-4e0f-8c55-4d66fb46ec40_1320x30.png)

A Brooklyn friend once told me a story about the time she rented a house in the country: As she was standing outside in the middle of nowhere, a stranger pulled up, got out of his car, and started making conversation in a way that made her uncomfortable. The man eventually drove away and nothing bad happened, but she found it unnerving, even terrifying. Along with many of my other city friends, she thought I was crazy to move upstate.

They weren’t entirely wrong. One of the big differences about living in a rural area is you feel as if you are on your own here, in a way that you don’t in the city. Your neighbors will help you out, of course, but they don’t share a living room wall with you; they can’t, well, hear you scream. Our water comes from our own well, we manage our own septic tank, and we pay for our garbage removal. The town doesn’t provide these things for us. We have never had a break-in, and our local police blotter is mostly about domestic violence or drunk driving—very rarely stranger-on-stranger crime. Emergency responders can get to our house quickly, but we can go days without seeing an authority figure. This changes how the world looks. I feel the need to be self-sufficient, to prepare for the worst. Lately there’s also part of me that thinks, *I have a right to bear arms, and I want to exercise that right.*

The seeds of this feeling were planted after Donald Trump was elected in 2016. Shocked and confused, I heeded the exhortations to “get out of your bubble,” and began reading conservative news outlets, where I quickly discovered how differently so many stories were reported—including those about guns. Accustomed to reading left-leaning media, I had absorbed the idea that using a gun for self-defense all but guaranteed the gun would be apprehended by the aggressor and used against the victim. Now I learned that there are actual instances of a “good guy with a gun” stopping a crime—like the dad with a concealed-carry permit who used a handgun to [incapacitate a man](https://www.foxnews.com/us/alabama-mcdonalds-gunman-killed-by-armed-dad-who-is-injured-in-shootout) who opened fire in a busy McDonald’s. They happen [all the time](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11540955/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) but are virtually never reported in certain outlets.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!44OD!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa02f9bde-28ed-472c-a679-349afb0ce381_1200x800.jpeg)

I still didn’t think about getting a gun for protection myself—until the dogs died. (Cindy Schultz for The Free Press. )

But I still didn’t think about getting a gun for protection myself—not until the dogs died. In their absence, especially on the nights when my husband is out of town, I feel exposed and vulnerable. My house has many ground-floor windows, and my kids are grown up and out of the house. I could get more big, loud dogs, of course—but they are a liability. Ours were daunting enough that we advised friends not to bring their pets to our house, as it was too much trouble, and too risky, to introduce their dogs to our wolf pack. My huge livestock guardian loved to roam, and we were constantly upgrading our fences in an attempt to keep him in. As much as I miss my big dogs, it’s a relief not to worry about them anymore; I can go away for the weekend, or just out for the day, more easily. But the fact remains that I don’t feel secure without them.

At the same time, I haven’t been able to shake the feeling that handguns are guaranteed to cause death and misery to everyone in their vicinity. So I decided to look at [the statistics](https://www.cdc.gov/firearm-violence/data-research/facts-stats/index.html). I learned that the vast majority of American gun owners have never been involved in a criminal shooting, an accident, or a suicide; that safe storage is a critical factor in preventing them; and that women are more likely to follow safety protocols. According to [one study](https://source.washu.edu/2015/12/study-provides-picture-of-firearm-injuries-deaths-among-st-louisarea-children), about 82 percent of all firearm victims in St. Louis are male, most of them between the ages of 12 and 18. As a white, middle-aged female, the only significantly elevated risk I’d encounter from keeping a safely stored firearm would be suicide. But with no history of mental health issues, that likelihood approaches practically zero. If I got a gun, it would be locked up except for occasional target practice. I’d almost certainly never feel the need to use it.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!h6sN!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0a9067af-0669-41b3-b836-cc49cf38e795_1200x800.jpeg)

I signed up for a one-hour lesson in firing a pistol at the American Firearms Training Academy near Albany. (Cindy Schultz for The Free Press. )

As I grappled with this dilemma, I figured I could at least learn how it felt to handle a gun, so my husband and I signed up for a one-hour lesson ($79.99 for the two of us) in firing a pistol at the [American Firearms Training Academy](https://aftausa.com/) near Albany. The atmosphere there was friendly and upbeat—but with an edge, like a boxing gym. And, of course, there were multiple guns on display behind the counter. After meekly accepting recommendations as to which type of pistol each of us should use *(um, whatever you suggest, sir*), we purchased two dense little boxes of bullets and followed Lee, our burly, friendly instructor, into a classroom for a preliminary lesson. He showed us how to hold, carry, load, and generally handle a firearm.

It was the first time I’d ever held a pistol. It was heavier than I had imagined, but it fit nicely into my hand. I can’t lie; I felt cool holding it—and tough. I admire people who can prevent or neutralize a gnarly situation. Maybe I could be like them.

After about 20 minutes of instruction, we put on ear protectors and headed into the shooting gallery. Lee attached a paper target—concentric red and white circles—to a mechanical arm and sent it about five yards down the 25-yard shooting alley. We could hear other groups talking and laughing—and firing, of course—in the other lanes. The vibe was like a private tennis lesson, except that instead of rackets and fuzzy balls we held instruments of death. But safety was built into every move, with repeated reminders of how to properly hold the gun or check the chamber.

My husband and I took turns, firing a full magazine each time, and then trying out each other’s gun. Here’s the most unsurprising thing I learned: Shooting at a target is *fun*, in the same way that throwing darts or shooting hoops is fun—just much more exciting. By the time we left, I wanted to sign up for more lessons, and was eyeing the weekend-long certification course for a concealed carry license. Not that I wanted to carry a concealed weapon; it was more that practicing, and becoming proficient, was appealing to me. I liked our instructor, and trusted him. Popular culture had taught me that gun enthusiasts were psychopathic maniacs firing at human silhouettes, but Lee’s motives for working with firearms made sense to me. He told me that after growing up shooting and doing a stint in the Marines, he got hooked on improving his gun-handling skills, and teaching others to do the same.

And yet, I haven’t purchased a gun, or signed up for more classes.

Why? Because I remain torn. Perhaps it’s the partisan fog still clouding my judgment. But as invigorating as I found it to shoot a pistol, it was also sobering to remind myself how much death and damage these small, potent objects can inflict. We really do need a rifle on the farm. We’re going to get one. As for something more than that, a handgun for protection and peace of mind, well, that remains to be decided.
