Was the recent disaster in the Himalayas caused by global warming? A study published by an influential group said yes. But its methods turned out to be deeply flawed.

On August 26, a wall of rock and ice broke away from Langtang Lirung in the Himalayas, resulting in a massive flood and mudflow that killed thousands of people along the border of Nepal and Tibet. Less than a month later, on September 17, an influential organization called World Weather Attribution (WWA), housed at Imperial College London, published a report on the catastrophe along with [a press release](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/rapid-warming-in-the-himalaya-exacerbates-geohazard-cascades-beyond-adaptation-limits/) touting its alarming findings.

[Major media](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/sep/17/climate-crisis-likely-behind-deadly-nepal-tibet-floods) outlets [ran with](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/sustainable-switch-did-climate-change-trigger-nepals-glacier-collapse-2026-09-17/) it, claiming that WWA’s scientists had concluded that the collapse was the result of human-caused global warming. WWA also asserted that early warning systems were useless against an event of that scale, and called for a rapid exit from fossil fuels to reduce the risks of future such events.

With this supposedly authoritative report in hand, Nepal’s government now plans to take a [$20 million claim](https://www.outlookindia.com/international/how-a-new-climate-study-on-nepal-floods-could-back-20-mn-un-fund-claim) for compensation to the United Nations for loss and damage due to climate change.

WWA scientists have now repeated this sequence more than 120 times since its founding in 2014: They issue a press release and technical report after a significant weather event, and link the event to climate change. The scientists then purport to explain how much climate change has tilted the odds for such events—three times more likely, 10 times more likely, impossible without climate change, etc. The media around the world then publicize the claims, invariably without challenge.

Over the past months I have [replicated 96 WWA studies](https://rogerpielkejr.substack.com/p/taking-us-for-fools). Beyond the headlines and under the hood, I can now document how the methods of the WWA project do not actually perform any legitimate analysis that would actually link the weather event to climate change. They simply assume it.

Further, applying the WWA methods I can link *any* measure that increases or decreases with time to climate change, whether that trend is weather-related or not. What’s more, I can replace increasing global temperatures with any other measure that is trending up or down, and show how that measure makes the weather event worse or more likely. Did increases in global patent applications make rainfall worse in a 2019 typhoon in Japan? If you use the WWA’s attribution methodology, you can “prove” it—as I did.

---

Despite the media embrace of the WWA results, there is not only much less there than meets the eye, but the effort risks displacing legitimate climate science, which to date has been assessed periodically and authoritatively by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).

The IPCC has spent more than three decades assessing the data and scientific literature on trends in extreme weather, using two steps.

The first step is [detection](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Full_Report.pdf): whether the long-term statistics associated with a particular type of extreme weather change—a century of hurricane landfalls, say, or peak river flows—can’t be explained by the normal fluctuations in weather over years and decades, called internal climate variability. The second step is attribution: The IPCC starts with a detected change and assesses the possible causes underlying that change—greenhouse gases, aerosols, volcanoes, land use, modes of climate variability, etc.—using sophisticated computer models of the earth’s climate system.

This is the methodology required by the IPCC’s precise definition of “climate change.” The IPCC [defines it](https://apps.ipcc.ch/glossary/) as a change in the state of the climate, identified by statistical tests and lasting decades or longer. The group treats climate change as a consequence, not as a cause.

I can now document how the methods of the WWA project do not actually perform any legitimate analysis that would actually link the weather event to climate change.

The IPCC judgment that global average temperatures have increased (now approaching 1.5 Celsius above the period 1850 to 1900) and that it is primarily due to human influences first occurred three decades ago, and is one of the most robust conclusions across climate science.

The IPCC has applied the same detection and attribution framework to extreme weather events and produced a much shorter list of detection and/or attributed findings. In its [most recent full assessment](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/), in 2021, it reported increases in the intensity, duration, and number of heat wave days at the global scale, and increases in the frequency and intensity of heavy rainfall across most land regions.

For nearly everything else, the IPCC recorded low confidence in detection, in attribution, or in both, including for river flooding, meteorological and hydrological drought, tropical cyclones, winter storms, tornadoes, hail, lightning, and extreme winds.

These conclusions have changed little across [successive assessments](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/6/1/014003/meta), which is unsurprising; scientists rarely overturn the findings of many decades within a few years of measurements. But a reader of the daily news would form a very different impression of what mainstream climate science has concluded.

Which, for the WWA, seems to be the whole point.

The alternative facts generated by WWA flip the IPCC’s careful approach around. WWA treats “climate change,” measured as a smoothed record of global temperature, as the only possible cause of any localized trend in rainfall, heat, flooding, or other types of extreme weather.

Contrary to what the IPCC works to explain as an outcome, the WWA uses that outcome as the explanation for everything. In the [protocol](https://ascmo.copernicus.org/articles/6/177/2020/) that WWA teams follow, they explain observed trends in weather: “It is assumed that the main changes in the distribution are due to global warming.”

The *A* in WWA means *attribution*, but it really ought to mean *assumption*.

The WWA methods have no way to evaluate the role of accumulating greenhouse gases in the atmosphere versus ocean cycles, land use, local conditions, and data quality as rival explanations of a local trend. They simply measure how closely the local variable tracks global temperature.

A reader of the daily news would form a very different impression of what mainstream climate science has concluded. For the WWA, that seems to be the whole point.

The awkward result is that any measure that increases or decreases over time—and I mean *any* measure—will correlate with rising global temperatures. But as we all learned in high school, correlation is not causation.

WWA has not been shy about the fact that it produces its studies to influence the public and to promote climate advocacy. Authors of one of the WWA’s foundational papers [openly acknowledged](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-021-03071-7) that a primary reason for creating their methods is political: by “increasing the ‘immediacy’ of climate change, thereby increasing support for mitigation.”

The WWA methods are couched in technical descriptions and equations, but at their core, they are trivially simple.

In a WWA tutorial, they use as an example demonstration of their methods the hottest day of each year at De Bilt, a Dutch town with a long temperature record. They first correlate the warmest day of a year at that location with a metric of global warming, and at De Bilt they get a ratio of about 2.8 degrees Celsius warming locally for every 1 degree Celsius globally. They then use that relationship to adjust the historical temperature record at that location down to represent a past climate, say in 1900, and adjust it up to represent the current climate.

The difference between the two reflects a change in climate and the cause of those differences is “climate change.” They repeat the analysis using modeled temperatures, and typically combine the results. Attribution achieved!

WWA moves from correlation to cause by assumption. When you read a media report that a flood or heat wave is X percent more likely, you are seeing the results of creating the two simplistic curves and the assumption that they are different because of climate change, and that is why whatever specific event just happened was so much more likely or intense.

The WWA methods are couched in technical descriptions and equations, but at their core, they are trivially simple.

WWA writes each analysis up in dense, scientific language, posts it [without peer review](https://rogerpielkejr.substack.com/p/is-single-extreme-event-attribution), and issues a press release the same day. Reporters typically work from the press release.

Which brings me back to [my own replication](https://rogerpielkejr.substack.com/p/taking-us-for-fools) of WWA’s methodology. As I mentioned earlier, I can attribute any measure that increases or decreases over time with climate change. Major League home run totals have increased over the decades and thus correlate with increasing global temperature, so I applied the WWA procedure to the annual MLB home run totals and found that the record 6,776 home runs of 2019 would have been impossible in the climate of 1900. Don’t laugh—in 2023 the Associated Press covered a [real attribution study](https://rogerpielkejr.substack.com/p/climate-change-causes-home-runs) (though not by WWA) claiming that climate change was increasing MLB home runs.

The method works in reverse as well. WWA published a [2022 study](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/climate-change-added-4bn-to-damage-of-japans-typhoon-hagibis/) claiming that climate change added $4 billion of the damage from 2019’s Typhoon Hagibis in Japan. I repeated its calculation with one substitution: I used a time series of increasing global patent applications in place of global temperature. I found that “patent change” made the Hagibis rainfall about four times more likely, turning a once-in-28-year downpour into a once-in-seven-year event. I can see the headlines now: Until the world curbs its inventors, Japan faces a rising risk of floods.

[WWA’s funders](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/about/) are philanthropists: the Grantham Foundation, the European Climate Foundation, and the Bezos Earth Fund, three of the largest backers of climate advocacy in the world.

Attribution science has become embedded in the institutions that governments and the public expect to assess science accurately and without a focus on political outcomes. For instance, the committee that wrote a recent [National Academy of Sciences report](https://rogerpielkejr.substack.com/p/attribution-stealth-advocacy-at-the) on extreme event attribution cited WWA’s staff and collaborators more than anyone else.

---

Remarkably, the Bezos Earth Fund has funded both WWA and that NAS committee. For its [next assessment](https://rogerpielkejr.substack.com/p/a-takeover-of-the-ipcc), due by the end of the decade, the IPCC placed nine extreme event attribution scientists on the 20-person writing committee for its extreme events chapter, including two WWA scientists, with its co-founder among the chapter leads.

I wonder if the traditional and reliable detection and attribution framework of the IPCC is now at risk, in favor of using the WWA’s unscientific assumptions.

The WWA’s advocacy campaign has worked brilliantly, of course; [50 percent of Americans](https://heatmap.news/daily/why-saying-climate-crisis-falls-flat) now believe policies addressing climate change would reduce the number or severity of the extreme weather events that they individually, or their communities, will experience.

The scientific reality—long understood in mainstream climate science—is that any emissions policy a government adopts today will not noticeably reduce the frequency and intensity of extreme weather, through the lifetimes of children or their children’s children.

There are many good reasons for smart and pragmatic climate policies—including aggressive mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions. However, the prospect of modulating future weather events is not on that list.
