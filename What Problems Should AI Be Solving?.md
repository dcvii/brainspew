Martin Kozlowski

I’ve spent much of the summer talking with technology executives about artificial intelligence. The only word I hear more often than “AI” is “abundance.” Not far behind is “socialism,” usually accompanied by bewilderment. Why, they wonder, are so many Americans, especially young ones, attracted to an economic system that has failed so spectacularly elsewhere? I’m beginning to think their enthusiasm for abundance and their bewilderment about socialism are connected.

The abundance pitch is hard to resist. Intelligence becomes cheap. Productivity soars. Scientific discovery accelerates. We live longer, get richer, even look sexier. Where do I sign up?

Then I ruin the mood by asking: Abundance for whom? Who decides? It may be the fastest way to end a conversation since I asked the Harvard economics department what we were doing about minority hiring.

Most people hear the question and assume I’m headed toward taxes, universal basic income or some other scheme for redistributing AI’s gains. But I’m not asking how we divide the wealth AI creates. I’m asking a prior question: What will we use AI to create in the first place? Before we divide the spoils, entrepreneurs and investors have to decide which problems are worth solving.

We are in the middle of a gold rush. Thinking Machines Lab [raised](https://www.reuters.com/technology/mira-muratis-ai-startup-thinking-machines-raises-2-billion-a16z-led-round-2025-07-15/) $2 billion in an initial funding round at a $12 billion valuation. Unconventional AI [raised](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/unconventional-ai-raises-475m-seed-213649847.html) $475 million with a $4.5 billion valuation, also at the seed stage. Technology executives I respect have told me some version of the same thing: There is simply too much money to be made right now to worry about anything else.

There is a reasonable defense of all this. “AI investment” lumps together very different bets: foundational models, chips and compute, physical infrastructure and applications. Technological revolutions often build the infrastructure before anyone knows all the applications it will enable. Perhaps the transformative AI applications simply haven’t arrived yet. But if we are waiting to discover them, where we look—and who looks—may matter most.

What if Silicon Valley has it backward? Not because they have a moral obligation to save the world, but because some of the biggest fortunes may be hiding in the problems they are ignoring. Medicaid spent [more than $900 billion](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/state-indicator/total-medicaid-spending/?currentTimeframe=0&sortModel={%22colId%22:%22Location%22,%22sort%22:%22asc%22}) in 2024. Gallup estimates that workers who aren’t engaged or actively disengaged account for [about $1.9 trillion](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/608675/new-workplace-employee-engagement-stagnates.aspx) in lost productivity nationally. Why aren’t problems of this scale a bigger focus in Silicon Valley?

A [paper](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20231417&from=f) by Elias Einiö, Josh Feng and Xavier Jaravel, [accepted](https://x.com/AEAjournals/status/2082122846050947229) for publication in the American Economic Review, offers a clue. Innovators disproportionately create products for people like themselves—in sex, income and age—even accounting for market size. Female entrepreneurs create products with an 18% higher female customer share than male entrepreneurs in the same category. Innovators from high-income families are more likely to create products bought by high-income consumers. More strikingly, entrepreneurs exposed to lower-income peers during higher education become more likely to enter necessity industries. The same pattern appears in biomedical innovation: Female inventors are disproportionately likely to produce inventions related to women’s health.

In venture capital, we have a name for this: founder-market fit. A founder who has lived a problem knows things outsiders don’t—what actually hurts, what customers will pay for, which workarounds they already use, and which elegant solution will die on contact with reality.

That age-old venture principle has an uncomfortable corollary. If certain Americans rarely become venture-backed founders, the problems they understand intimately are less likely to become startup ideas.

Venture capitalists know how to exploit founder-market fit when they recognize the market. In defense, they prize founders who understand weapons, procurement and the Pentagon. The success of companies like Anduril suggests they are right to do so. They’re better at finding founders who understand missiles than Medicaid.

I see the same mechanism every year at Harvard. I teach a class called Using Markets to Solve Social Problems. For their capstone, students identify an important social problem and build a company to solve it. Their choices are revealing. Last semester one team, led by a cancer survivor, proposed an AI-native care coordinator for cancer patients. Another team was deeply concerned that Harvard Square lacked hot-cookie delivery at 3 a.m. Its solution: HotCookies.ai.

In nearly a decade I have never had a student say: You know what really sucks? Getting government benefits. Or coming home from prison and trying to find a job. Or running a police department with antiquated software. My students aren’t selfish. Many simply have never encountered these systems. You can’t have founder-market fit in a market you’ve never met.

That is one reason Bill Helman and I started a venture fund. We believed entrepreneurs who understood overlooked markets could build enormous companies. Traditional venture capital often treated these as bad markets. Impact investors treated them as worthy causes that required accepting lower returns. We thought both were mispricing the opportunity.

AI makes that bet far more interesting. It changes both sides of the equation—who can start a company and which businesses can scale. For years, the venture playbook treated technical expertise as the scarce input: Find a brilliant engineer and build the rest of the company around him. AI doesn’t make engineers irrelevant. But it can make some technical execution cheaper—and deep market knowledge relatively more valuable.

Until recently, someone who had spent 20 years running Medicaid programs might understand the customer better than almost anyone alive and still have little chance of starting a software company. Today, AI can help him prototype, analyze data, write code and get remarkably far before assembling a large technical team.

AI also changes which businesses can be venture-scale. Benefits navigation, career coaching and case management have historically been labor-intensive. If serving twice as many customers requires twice as many employees, the unit economics don’t work. AI-native service businesses can break that relationship.

I’m not asking Silicon Valley to be less greedy. Don’t choose Medicaid over missiles. Be greedier—there is serious money to be made in both.

That brings me back to the other word I keep hearing: socialism. My tech friends are exuberant about abundance, reluctant to discuss who will benefit from it, and baffled that so many Americans are anxious about AI and questioning capitalism. I’m baffled by their bafflement.

Nearly 8 in 10 Americans [expect](https://news.gallup.com/poll/712751/americans-cool-toward.aspx) AI to reduce the number of U.S. jobs over the next decade. The Democratic Socialists of America says its membership hit an all-time high of 120,000 on July 4. Meanwhile, 54% of Americans [tell Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/694835/image-capitalism-slips.aspx) they view capitalism positively, down from 60% in 2021. Yet 81% view free enterprise positively and 95% view small business positively.

Maybe Americans aren’t souring on markets. Maybe they’re souring on the markets they see.

Imagine the AI optimists are right. We create staggering wealth and productivity. Products and services once reserved for the wealthy become cheap enough for almost anyone. But that promise will be realized only if entrepreneurs build for people beyond themselves.

If one America uses AI to eliminate every friction in an already comfortable life while another still spends an afternoon trying to figure out whether it qualifies for food stamps, we shouldn’t be surprised if the second concludes that capitalism has failed it—and asks government to divide the abundance some other way.

The tragedy would be to blame capitalism for our failure of imagination. AI will create abundance. How much and for whom depends on whether Silicon Valley keeps solving the problems it already knows—or learns to see the ones it doesn’t.

*Mr. Fryer, a Journal contributor, is a professor of economics at Harvard, a founder of Equal Opportunity Ventures and a senior fellow at the Manhattan Institute.*
