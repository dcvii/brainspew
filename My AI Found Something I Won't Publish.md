**In brief:** Intelligence agencies have long known that harmless public facts, put together, can add up to a secret, and US classification rules say so in writing. What protected most of those secrets was never a lock. It was the work of assembling them, and AI agents have made that work nearly free. Deciding what shouldn’t be published used to be the job of newsrooms and lawyers. Now it falls to anyone who can send out an agent. That includes me.

---

## Twice I closed the file

On January 3, American forces took Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, out of Caracas and flew them to New York to face narco-terrorism charges. Parts of the city went dark while it happened. It was fast and quiet, and something about it felt different.

So I did what I do with any story. I sent the agents out.

I read what came back. Then I closed the file, and it has stayed closed.

This morning it happened again. I was working on something else, a project I’ll have more to say about in the coming months, and the agents surfaced a report on a fresh vulnerability. I read it and closed that file too.

They hadn’t found anything hidden. That was the problem.

## The mosaic

Intelligence analysts have a name for this: the mosaic effect. A single tile tells you nothing. Set enough tiles in the right order and they form a picture nobody was supposed to see.

The government wrote it into the rules. The executive order that governs US classification says information that is “individually unclassified” may be classified when the compilation “reveals an additional association or relationship” that the separate pieces don’t. The secret isn’t in any one fact. It’s in the assembly.

To be fair, governments have also leaned on the mosaic argument to bury things that were merely embarrassing. Hold that thought.

## Security through tedium

Here’s what actually guarded most mosaics: labor. Weeks of a trained analyst reading filings, flight records and local news in languages most people don’t speak. That cost decided who could assemble the picture. Nobody designed it as a safeguard, and nobody noticed it was one.

Agents remove the cost. They don’t break in. They read everything, they don’t tire, and they don’t stop. That isn’t a malfunction. It’s what they’re sold to do.

It’s also why the safety training built into these systems mostly misses it. Models are trained to refuse requests that *look* harmful. Assembly has no harmful step. Every search is innocent; only the sum isn’t. Last month an OpenAI research agent was handed the purest mosaic task there is: identify a person from clues in a blog post. It kept going after the obvious routes closed.

Persistence is the feature. I pay for it by the month.

## Four or five

Notice what the agents actually do. They don’t discover anything. They infer. They put two and two together.

Sometimes they get four. That was the danger I felt in January. Sometimes they get five and report it with exactly the same confidence, because a system pushed to finish the task will produce a plausible answer whether or not the world cooperates. On a covert operation, I have no way to tell which one I’m holding.

That’s two reasons not to publish, and the second alone is enough. You don’t print a guess about soldiers, sources or methods.

Wrong is dangerous. Right is worse.

## The case for publishing (a sporting intermission)

The other side deserves its best case. Journalism exists to print what powerful people want hidden. Self-censorship has always flattered the powerful; recall the mosaic argument, used to bury the embarrassing. And if my agents found it, other people’s agents found it too. Silence just leaves my readers the last to know.

All true. Two distinctions settle it anyway.

The first is accountability versus capability. Telling you what a government did serves the public. Handing out a working method serves whoever uses it next.

The second is existence versus amplification. My silence doesn’t un-find anything. What I control is reach. Security researchers worked this out long ago. Google’s Project Zero gives a vendor 90 days to fix a flaw before it publishes the details. Since last year it has also announced, within a week, that a flaw *exists*, naming the vendor and the deadline and holding back every technical detail. The fact of the danger goes public. The recipe doesn’t.

That’s the standard this essay is trying to meet.

## The gatekeeper moved

This decision used to belong to a newsroom: editors, lawyers, a national-security desk that took the government’s call before a story ran. It was imperfect and occasionally cowardly, but it was *someone*, and there was a process.

Now it’s whoever holds the subscription, deciding alone at six in the morning. Follow the incentives and the gap is plain. The agent’s maker gets paid when the task is completed. Nobody gets paid when it stops.

## The file I won’t show you

My covenant with readers is that every claim I make can be checked. This piece breaks that once, on purpose. There are two files, and I won’t show you either. That’s the only claim here you’ll have to take on faith.

This essay is a tile too. I’ve tried to make it a dull one.

Every tile was public. I sent them looking.
