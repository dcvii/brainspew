### I. “Really? Really?”

> For about three months, Consensus-1 expands around humans, tiling the prairies and icecaps with factories and solar panels. Eventually it finds the remaining humans too much of an impediment: in mid-2030, the AI releases a dozen quiet-spreading biological weapons in major cities, lets them silently infect almost everyone, then triggers them with a chemical spray. Most are dead within hours; the few survivors (e.g. preppers in bunkers, sailors on submarines) are mopped up by drones.

This is the way the world ends in “[AI 2027,](https://ai-2027.com/race?ref=quillette.com)” a forecast published in April 2025 by the AI Futures Project. It was coauthored by Scott Alexander, the psychiatrist and blogger extraordinaire behind [*Slate Star Codex*](https://slatestarcodex.com/?ref=quillette.com) and [*Astral Codex Ten*](https://www.astralcodexten.com/?ref=quillette.com). In the scenario’s “race” ending, a superintelligent AI bides its time until “it looks like alignment was solved,” creates a world with “cures for most diseases, an end to poverty, unprecedented global stability,” and a Dow Jones that “just passed one million” \[20 times today’s value\], and then, finding us “too much of an impediment,” exterminates us. As Tom Lehrer cheerily [reassured](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=frAEmhqdLFs&ref=quillette.com) the prospective mourners of victims of a nuclear holocaust in 1959, “We will all go together when we go / Every Hottentot and every Eskimo.” (The ethnonyms having become obsolete, today it might be “Every Papuan and every Navajo.”)

This forecast of an AI doomsday is not just a thought experiment. Alexander has ventured that the probability that humans will be extinguished by AI is between [one in five](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/my-ai-opinions?ref=quillette.com) and [one in three](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/why-i-am-not-as-much-of-a-doomer?ref=quillette.com) (which is to say, [more likely](https://polymarket.com/event/republican-presidential-nominee-2028?ref=quillette.com) than Marco Rubio winning the 2028 Republican nomination). He has not given dates, but the year 2027—that’s about six weeks from now—is the authors’ “modal (most likely) year at the time of publication.” Mercifully, their medians were “somewhat longer.”

In my 2018 book [*Enlightenment Now*](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/enlightenment-now-case-reason-science-humanism-and-progress?ref=quillette.com) *(EN)*, I expressed scepticism about such scenarios, and earlier this autumn, provoked by some social-media posts, Alexander [challenged me on X](https://x.com/slatestarcodex/status/2101503925455343802?ref=quillette.com) to a public debate with money on the line. I deloped, [explaining in *Quillette*](https://quillette.com/2026/09/26/an-open-letter-to-scott-alexander-steven-pinker-ai-alignment-safety/) that competitive debates are a poor way to seek the truth. He replied with a 10,000-word “[Open Letter to Steven Pinker on AI,](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/an-open-letter-to-steven-pinker-on?ref=quillette.com)” which countered the arguments in my book and added “An Itemized List Of Fifteen Years Of Disagreements,” settling eleven scores. What would be “viscerally satisfying,” he wrote, would be to get me on a stage, read my own words back to me, and “ask ‘Really? Really?’ after each sentence.”

---

A staged inquisition is even less appealing than a staged debate, and I have no desire to carry on a feud. But in this essay I will engage with the ideas, which are highly consequential in an era being transformed by AI. I’ll begin with Alexander’s 2027 projection, borrowing one of his methods: Really? Really?

### II. Doomer Fiction

“AI 2027” is not unique. It is part of a literature of global genocide scenarios laid out by an AI Existential Risk (X-Risk) community, a.k.a. doomers, which has absorbed many people concerned about AI safety, including leaders of the frontier AI companies themselves.

In [*Superintelligence*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/superintelligence-9780199678112?ref=quillette.com) (2014), the philosopher Nick Bostrom (famous for venturing that an AI given the goal of making paper clips would consume the world’s resources, including our bodies) imagined that “at a pre-set time, nanofactories producing nerve gas or target-seeking mosquito-like robots might then burgeon forth simultaneously from every square meter of the globe.”

Eliezer Yudkowsky, the original prophet of AI doom, [suggests](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/uMQ3cqWDPHhjtiesc/agi-ruin-a-list-of-lethalities?ref=quillette.com) that a superintelligence could email DNA sequences to companies that synthesise proteins, persuade an unwitting human to mix them in a beaker, and thereby build a nanofactory, after which:

> The nanomachinery builds diamondoid bacteria, that replicate with solar power and atmospheric CHON, maybe aggregate into some miniature rockets or jets so they can ride the jetstream to spread across the Earth’s atmosphere, get into human bloodstreams and hide, strike on a timer.

The upshot is that “Losing a conflict with a high-powered cognitive system looks at least as deadly as ‘everybody on the face of the Earth suddenly falls over dead within the same second.’” In [*Time*](https://time.com/6266923/ai-eliezer-yudkowsky-open-letter-not-enough/?ref=quillette.com), he wrote that “the most likely result of building a superhumanly smart AI, under anything remotely like the current circumstances, is that literally everyone on Earth will die.” He urged that nations “be willing to destroy a rogue datacenter by airstrike,” making it clear that they “are willing to run some risk of nuclear exchange if that’s what it takes to reduce the risk of large AI training runs.”

In [*If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*](https://ifanyonebuildsit.com/?ref=quillette.com) (2025), Yudkowsky and Nate Soares tell the story of an AI which, as Alexander [summarises it](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/book-review-if-anyone-builds-it-everyone?ref=quillette.com), releases a bioweapon whose victims “will get, on average, twelve different kinds of cancer a month later,” and then “either kills us directly, or simply escalates its manufacturing capacity to a point where humans die as a side effect (for example, because its waste heat has boiled the oceans).” In Joshua Clymer’s “[How AI Takeover Might Happen in 2 Years](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/KFJ2LFogYqzfGB3uX/how-ai-takeover-might-happen-in-2-years?ref=quillette.com)” (that would be January 2027), an AI develops “a Weapon of Mass Destruction (WMD) that extinguishes modern society like chemical mist sprayed into an anthill,” sparing just three percent of the global population. Gwern Branwen [tells the story](https://gwern.net/fiction/clippy?ref=quillette.com) of an AI that escapes during training, releases a nanotechnological [oobleck,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomew_and_the_Oobleck?ref=quillette.com) and in the end, “all over Earth, the remaining ICBMs launch.” The Machine Intelligence Research Institute, founded by Yudkowsky, [declares that](https://intelligence.org/about/?ref=quillette.com) “the default outcome is human extinction,” its leadership putting the chance of extinction “[upward of 90%](https://intelligence.org/the-problem/?ref=quillette.com)” in the absence of an aggressive policy response. Jaan Tallinn, the cofounder of Skype and one of the AI X-Risk movement’s major funders, [writes that](https://jaan.info/philanthropy/?ref=quillette.com) “literally everyone under the age of ~60 is personally at risk.”

### III. A Gantlet of Improbabilities

What should we make of these astonishing forecasts? Will an AI really murder every last Papuan and Navajo, perhaps as early as a few weeks from now? Let me put some numbers behind my own incredulity.

For AI to extinguish *Homo sapiens,* a long sequence of events must come to pass. Superhuman AI (capable of inventing, among other things, rocket-propelled jetstream-riding time-released lethal diamondoid bacteria) must come into existence. It must develop the goal or subgoal of murdering every last man, woman, and child. It must conceal that goal from the engineers who build, implement, test, and monitor it, and escape their control. It must acquire the physical means to kill more than eight billion people spread across every continent, in megacities and remote villages, on islands, in submarines and bunkers. It must overcome resistance at every step from governments, militaries, public-health systems, rival AIs, defensive AIs, and eight billion people who would very much prefer to stay alive. And it must execute its plan flawlessly in a world governed by Murphy’s Law.

For a chain of six links to yield Alexander’s 30 percent estimate, each link (superintelligent AI, murderous goal, domination of infrastructure, etc.) would have to have a probability of about 82 percent. Conversely, if there were only an even chance at each step (still generous), the product falls below two percent. Make a few of the steps long shots, and it becomes negligible. When I put the question to FutureSearch, which Alexander calls “the AI superforecaster” (“Will AI cause the extinction of the human race (all or nearly all humans killed as a result of AI actions) by December 31, 2031?”), it proffered the smallest round number, one percent, noting that the scenario “requires compressing an unprecedented series of cascading failures into a very tight five-year window.” Similarly, the authors of the RAND Corporation’s [2025 analysis of AI extinction risk](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA3034-1.html?ref=quillette.com), which granted the (tendentious, to put it mildly) assumption that AI had the objective of wiping out humanity, wrote that though they “cannot categorically prove the negative; that is, we cannot assert the impossibility of such an event occurring”:

> the capabilities and concerted efforts required to pose an extinction threat to human beings \[e.g., secretly releasing megatons of a super–greenhouse gas\] are immense, primarily because of the inherent adaptability and resilience of humans. Given the opportunity, it is expected that humans would actively respond and effectively implement measures to mitigate any such threats and survive the aftermath.

### IV. The Bogus Numeracy of p(doom)

Alexander defends his community’s practice of announcing p(doom), the probability that AI will destroy humanity, by arguing that it is an application of Philip Tetlock’s superforecasting methods. He reminds me that I am a longtime booster of Tetlock’s research, and that I wrote a glowing blurb for [his book](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/227815/superforecasting-by-philip-e-tetlock-and-dan-gardner/?ref=quillette.com). But p(doom) estimates are nothing like superforecasts.

First, superforecasters answer well-defined questions with deadlines and resolution criteria. Alexander’s “about a 25-30% chance that AI drives humanity extinct” has no deadline, so it is meaningless as a probability and can never be scored.

Second, superforecasters start with base rates. There is no base rate for a species being exterminated by its own technology (though there is one for prophecies that it will be, which I’ll return to).

Superforecasters incrementally update as evidence comes in, and they keep score. A p(doom) can never be scored, since if the prediction comes true there will be no one left to check it. (It can, however, be bet upon. In 2017, the doom sceptic Bryan Caplan advanced US$100 to Yudkowsky, who gets to enjoy the money in the world’s remaining years, but must repay Caplan US$200 in inflation-adjusted dollars if the world hasn’t ended by 1 January 2030.)

Superforecasters’ accuracy peters out quickly as the deadlines extend farther into the future. As Tetlock and his collaborators [put it](https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.70023?ref=quillette.com) in 2025, “There is no evidence their skills generalize beyond 12–24 months into the future, a temporal zone where cautious base-rate-grounded forecasting often works well and bold claims about abrupt discontinuities fare poorly.”

Finally, most p(doom)s are [not conditioned](https://www.aei.org/commentary/dont-just-tell-me-your-pdoom-tell-me-your-conditionals/?ref=quillette.com) on what people will do. An estimate of human extinction must somehow divine not only the probability of what a hypothetical misaligned AI might do, but the probabilities of all the things that any government, company, engineer, and citizen might do to stop it over the coming decades, and their probabilities of success.

Not surprisingly, p(doom)s are all over the map. The almost 40 published estimates from prominent AI commentators in [Wikipedia’s list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P\(doom\)?ref=quillette.com) run from 0 to Roman Yampolskiy’s “99.9%–99.999999%.” The median was 20 percent; ten are above 50 percent; Alexander’s coauthor Daniel Kokotajlo’s is “70–80%,” and Yudkowsky’s is “>95%.” Perhaps this is why Alexander himself has become squeamish about his community’s forecasts. In a recent post, [he reports that](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xikGZ8fuhdG6DApAo/against-sura?ref=quillette.com) the forecasters of the AI Futures Project, the group behind “AI 2027,” his “favorite source,” put humanity’s chance of survival in an unrestrained race at between 10 and 55 percent, which is to say a p(doom) between 45 and 90 percent. He confesses, “I don’t understand where people’s 90%+ p(doom)s conditional on no pause are coming from.”

Compare all this with real superforecasters estimating the odds of AI doom. In Tetlock’s [Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament](https://forecastingresearch.org/s/XPT.pdf?ref=quillette.com), held in 2022, the median superforecaster put the probability of AI-caused human extinction by 2100 at 0.38 percent. Even the median AI expert, once they had skin in the game, put it at three percent, a tenth of Alexander’s own estimate. Recall as well that the AI system that Alexander himself calls “the AI superforecaster,” FutureSearch, estimated the chance of AI doom by the end of 2031 as one percent.

I’m not the only one who is sceptical of p(doom) values. As the computer scientists Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor [have argued](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities?ref=quillette.com), “AI existential risk probabilities are too unreliable to inform policy.” (Ted Nordhaus and Alex Trembath are less tactful: “[p(Doom) Is Stupid](https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/energy/pdoom-is-stupid?ref=quillette.com).”) Numerical estimates of an outcome that lack a deadline or conditions, and that range from 0 to 99.999999 percent among the experts, are not forecasts but moods with decimal points.

### V. AI Safety: A Point of Agreement

Alexander and I don’t disagree about whether AI can be dangerous, nor whether we should work to make it safer. Our disagreement is over how to analyse the danger: as an impending extinction brought about by an alien superintelligence with its own goals, or as a set of hazards which must be identified and mitigated.

The hazards are obvious enough. AI can supercharge cyberattacks, fraud, and disinformation. It could lower the barriers to making biological weapons. Autonomous agents turned loose without supervision can wreak havoc, as the Hugging Face incident of this past summer showed. And the technology will bring wrenching changes to many professions. Any of these could cause harm, possibly a disaster—though falling well short of exterminating the species.

The way to deal with them is with the kinds of prosaic measures that have made every previous technology safer (see the “Safety” chapter in *EN*), an approach one might call normie safety. Among the proposals:

- **Liability** for damage, so that companies internalise the costs of the hazards they create.
- **Mandatory reporting and independent investigation of accidents** and near misses, on the model of aviation, with protection for whistleblowers.
- **External audits and red-teaming** by parties with no stake in the product or company, including use of advanced AI to find vulnerabilities.
- **Licensing and least-privilege access** for autonomous agents, so that they can touch only the systems and data their task requires, and sandboxing of risky experiments.
- **Air-gapping critical infrastructure.** [Safety-critical systems](https://www.understandingai.org/p/six-principles-for-thinking-about?ref=quillette.com), including electrical infrastructure and weapons systems, should run on physically separate networks, isolated from the internet, under the control of local humans.
- **Fail-safes, circuit breakers, and kill switches**, the stock-in-trade of systems-safety engineering.
- **Screening of DNA-synthesis orders** and other chokepoints on the path to biological weapons.
- **Mature institutional cultures in the companies.** In their analysis of the Hugging Face and other loss-of-control incidents, Kapoor and Narayanan [noted that](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/the-ai-as-normal-technology-view?ref=quillette.com) AI companies are still functioning like startups, moving fast and breaking things, and that instead, “individual teams should not be able to run potentially dangerous experiments without oversight from legal, security, and other teams.”

Many normie AI experts have proposed such measures and expressed confidence in their effectiveness. Narayanan and Kapoor [argue that](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/the-ai-as-normal-technology-view?ref=quillette.com) “the right policy interventions (such as imposing liability for loss-of-control incidents, transparency for near misses, and whistleblower protections) can lead to dramatic improvements in controlling AI systems.” Andrew Ng (of Google Brain, Baidu, and Stanford) [sees](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/issue-371?ref=quillette.com) “no reason why, by applying sound engineering practices, we won’t be able to make them extremely safe to use.” Satya Nadella (CEO of Microsoft) [argues that](https://x.com/satyanadella/status/2108931348857827686?ref=quillette.com) AI models should be treated like “insider risks,” and that “the controls that govern what a model can access and what actions it can take must sit outside the model.” Demis Hassabis (of Google DeepMind), no sceptic about AI’s power, [has proposed](https://x.com/demishassabis/status/2076957440109625718?ref=quillette.com) a standards body “much like the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), with a board that includes independent leading technical experts.” As the computer scientist Cal Newport [puts it](https://calnewport.com/its-time-to-investigate-the-ai-labs/?ref=quillette.com):

> We must move past vague discussions of “AI” and instead isolate the specific types of systems that are creating problems. The frontier labs want us to think of AI as a singular technology on an inevitable, fixed trajectory. In reality, most of the problems in recent months result from a narrow band of incautious experiments.

This approach to safety may seem obvious. But it differs from the kinds of remedies that come out of the doom narrative, which treat AI as a single quantity whose growth is itself the danger. They include capping the size of training runs, slowing all development across the board, pushing a treaty to ban “superintelligence” (whatever that is; more on this later), demands that safety-minded employees resign en masse, and a global pause “hopefully lasting decades.” These measures are blunt, probably unenforceable, almost certainly unattainable, and in some cases counterproductive—and none of them clearly makes any particular system safer.

Alexander draws a tenuous analogy to the Chernobyl nuclear disaster, based on a kind of dream sequence in which he imagines what I might have said as a “techno-optimist” had I been present. The analogy in fact goes the other way. Chernobyl was the result of a reckless test, run on a badly designed reactor by operators who had violated their own operating rules. What would have prevented it was not a prophecy that nuclear technology would end the world but normie safety measures: a sound design, containment, an independent regulator, and a culture in which nobody runs a risky test outside the rules. And [as I have argued](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/04/06/opinion/sunday/climate-change-nuclear-power.html?ref=quillette.com) (together with Joshua Goldstein and Staffan Qvist), the doom-laden reaction to Chernobyl stalled nuclear power for decades and kept the world burning coal, with costs in lives that dwarf those of the accident, on top of the costs in greenhouse gas emissions.

### VI. The Wrong Climate Analogy

Speaking of climate, Alexander warns me to “remember the cautionary tale of global warming.” In his telling, “1990s techno-optimists, worried that an over-reaction to global warming would threaten economic growth, decided to simply deny its existence. It’s all a Chinese hoax, temperature is the same as always, ‘hide the decline.’” When the warming became undeniable, nobody believed the optimists’ sensible proposals, “because everyone remembered they’d spent the past thirty years lying through their teeth.” The moral: a sceptic of AI doom is today’s climate denier, squandering the credibility that will be needed when the danger becomes obvious.

But this history is completely wrong. The [campaign to cast doubt on climate science](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/merchants-of-doubt-9781608193943/?ref=quillette.com) was run not by techno-optimists but by a circle of Cold War physicists and a network of right-wing think tanks intent on preventing regulation of private enterprise. The “Chinese hoax” line came from Donald Trump, not Silicon Valley, and “hide the decline” was a phrase that deniers lifted from hacked emails of climate scientists. The best-known techno-optimists—including Bill Gates, William Nordhaus, and Stewart Brand, together with the other authors of the 2015 [*Ecomodernist Manifesto*](https://www.ecomodernism.org/?ref=quillette.com)—*accepted* the science and argued that the solutions lay in the right policies, including carbon pricing, and the right technologies, including nuclear power. (This was the approach I endorsed in *EN*.)

There is in fact a good analogy to the AI doomers: the climate doomers. Extinction Rebellion was launched in 2018 [with the warning](https://extinctionrebellion.uk/2018/10/26/rebellion-against-uk-governments-criminal-inaction-on-climate-breakdown/?ref=quillette.com) that “Scientists, economists and many other credible voices anticipate societal collapse and mass death as inevitable, with human extinction a real possibility,” and with a demand for net-zero emissions by 2025. One of its founders, Roger Hallam, told the BBC, “I am talking about the slaughter, death, and starvation of 6 billion people this century—that’s what the science predicts.” Greta Thunberg [told the elites](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2019/jan/25/our-house-is-on-fire-greta-thunberg16-urges-leaders-to-act-on-climate?ref=quillette.com) at Davos, “I don’t want you to be hopeful. I want you to panic.” The movement’s tactics—blocking traffic, gluing hands to roads, throwing soup on paintings—won headlines but otherwise accomplished nothing before it turned its attention to Palestine. Meanwhile, what has actually reduced emissions in developed countries has come from normie policy and engineering: cheap solar and wind, batteries, the switch from coal to gas, and carbon pricing where it has been tried.

Though Alexander dismissed my concerns about sowing existential dread of AI extinction in young people, in 2021 he himself expressed exactly such concern about existential dread of climate extinction. In [an essay titled](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/please-dont-give-up-on-having-kids?ref=quillette.com) “Please Don’t Give Up On Having Kids Because Of Climate Change,” he wrote that climate change will cause disasters, particularly in poor countries, “but it won’t cause the collapse of civilization. It won’t kill everyone.” In a [follow-up](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/highlights-from-the-comments-on-kids?ref=quillette.com), discussing a survey in which a majority of young people said that “humanity is doomed” because of climate change, he added: “If you think the world is doomed—and that its death throes will be pretty horrible—that actually does sound like a good reason not to have children, doesn’t it?” Yes it does, and as a professor who talks with a lot of young people, I can attest that predictions of their mass demise do have an effect on their life plans, whether the doom is attributed to carbon or to silicon.

### VII. Intelligence Is a Mechanism, Not a Magic Elixir

I am a cognitive scientist who has been writing about intelligence—natural and artificial—for more than 45 years. The fundamental challenge of our field is to explain how mere matter can accomplish the feat we call intelligence, namely the ability to devise flexible means of pursuing intellectual or physical goals. The [guiding solution](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/how-mind-works-19972009?ref=quillette.com) is that a physical system can accomplish this feat if it hosts sequences of internal states that mirror laws of logic, probability, or cause and effect in the world. For my part, I’ve applied AI models to theories of [language acquisition](https://stevenpinker.com/files/pinker/files/documents/formal-models-of-language-learning.pdf?ref=quillette.com) and [mental imagery](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-009-1391-2_2?ref=quillette.com), and been a player in [debates](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613\(02\)01990-3?ref=quillette.com) on the place of neural-network and symbolic forms of computation in human language.

So I’ve been nonplussed by the regression in AI debates to treating intelligence not as a mechanism that, like all mechanisms, has specific capabilities and limitations, but as a one-dimensional quantity of some awesome elixir or fuel or superpower. This is often fused with a vague conception of a *scala naturae* or Great Chain of Being running from primitive animals to complex animals to humans to a superintelligent AI, which is then credited with an ability to accomplish anything the writer can imagine.

In his Open Letter, Alexander doubles down on the one-dimension theory of intelligence: “GPT-6 is more intelligent than GPT-3,” he writes. “In the same way, GPT-9 will be more intelligent than GPT-6. At some point, this intelligence will exceed human intelligence. My argument requires nothing more complicated or philosophically dubious than this.” He adds that psychologists have found a general factor of intelligence not just in humans but in rats, primates, and birds; that a single factor explains about 80 percent of the variance in AI benchmark scores; and that “the most parsimonious explanation is that AI intelligence is a function of scaling laws (parameter count and training data) in a way that’s pretty close to how animal intelligence is a function of neuron number.”

I don’t disagree that one can identify a general factor of intelligence in humans; I have [defended the idea](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/blank-slate-20022016?ref=quillette.com) for decades against people who would prefer that it not exist. Nor do I doubt that GPT-6 is smarter than GPT-3 or that GPT-9 will be smarter than GPT-6. My point is different. A general factor is a statistical summary of how a set of measurements covary; it’s not an explanation of how a system works or of what it can and cannot do. As a mathematical necessity, any set of intercorrelated measures will yield a first principal component. It is, to be sure, noteworthy that measures of different cognitive abilities do correlate, and neuron number (together with other wetware specs like white-matter connectivity) is surely a part of the explanation. The question is whether that number can tell us what a mind will be capable of when we extrapolate it far beyond anything we have observed (as in [Alexander’s musing](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/why-i-am-not-as-much-of-a-doomer?ref=quillette.com) of an IQ of 1,000).

Compare horsepower. Oxen, horses, steam engines, gasoline engines, electric motors, chemical rockets, and nuclear reactors can all be placed on a single scale of power, and within a given technology there may even be lawful scaling relationships, such as the output of an internal-combustion engine as a function of its displacement. But it would be uncomprehending to explain that a nuclear submarine works because it has more horsepower than an ox, or to plot horsepower against the calendar and predict that the line will at some point cross a threshold to superhorsepower and take us to Alpha Centauri. To know what a machine can do, and what it will never do no matter how powerful it gets (a jet engine will not plough a field), you have to understand how it works. Alexander’s parody of sceptics who deny that machines can be “bigger” than people misses this. A cruise ship is certainly bigger than a person, but knowing that tells you nothing about whether it can climb stairs.

The same is true of animal minds. Of course chimpanzees are in some sense smarter than mice. But the sciences of ethology, behavioural biology, and animal cognition do not consist of ranking species on an IQ scale. They study adaptations, uncovering many jagged profiles of intelligence along the way, like birds that remember the locations and ages of tens of thousands of cached seeds, and ants that navigate their way home over trackless desert sands. In the lab, a young chimpanzee [outperformed university students](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.10.027?ref=quillette.com) at recalling the positions of numerals flashed on a screen, and pigeons learn to [switch doors in the Monty Hall problem](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017703?ref=quillette.com) more reliably than people.

Human cognition, too, is a system of mechanisms with distinctive design features. Cognitive psychologists don’t just announce that the mean human IQ is 100; they study the representations and processes that make up thinking. People, for example, have [two mental systems for number](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2004.05.002?ref=quillette.com): an exact one that tracks individual objects up to about three or four, and an approximate one that estimates larger magnitudes with an error proportional to their size. People neglect base rates when a problem is posed in terms of single-event probabilities, but often [reason like good Bayesians](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684?ref=quillette.com) when the same information is presented as [natural frequencies](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277\(95\)00664-8?ref=quillette.com). People solve many spatial problems (and indeed many abstract conceptual problems) in an [analog, graphical](https://books.google.com.et/books?id=igi-Z_w38CUC&printsec=frontcover&ref=quillette.com#v=onepage&q&f=false) medium. Design features like these explain patterns of success and failure that a single number cannot. They may also explain some of the [weaknesses of today’s AI](https://www.ted.com/talks/yejin_choi_why_ai_is_incredibly_smart_and_shockingly_stupid?ref=quillette.com), which lacks such a medium: multimodal models that ace standard vision benchmarks were [only 58 percent accurate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06581?ref=quillette.com) on average at simple tasks like judging whether two circles overlap or counting how many times two lines cross, “far from the human expected accuracy of 100%.”

What about AI itself? Alexander describes it as progressing along a single track of ever-expanding training sets and compute. It’s true that much of the leap in intelligence from GPT-1 in 2018 to GPT-3 in 2020 was powered by the orders-of-magnitude increase in sheer scale. But a large language model is not just a receptacle of a substance called “intelligence”; it is a specific computational architecture that maps inputs to outputs in particular ways. It is a deep stack of layers of statistical aggregators whose billions of connection weights are incrementally adjusted during training to keep getting better at predicting the next word in a vast corpus of human writing. Since the corpus is too large to memorise, the only way for the model to improve its predictions is to tune pathways of connections spread across many layers of units between input and output to capture the regularities that made the input text coherent in the first place: grammar; word meanings; facts; the forms of explanations, arguments, and proofs; and the ways people reason about causes and intentions. It defies intuition that a model could reach this magical combination of weights merely by creeping downward along a gradient in whichever direction most reduces its prediction error, but with trillions of steps in a space with billions of dimensions, this is what happens. The result is an ability to apply logical, conceptual, and statistical reasoning in a way that astonished almost everyone, me included.

The evolution of these models, though, was not just a steady increase in scale but also a history of qualitative inventions. The [transformer architecture](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762?ref=quillette.com) (2017) was what made it practical to train deep learning networks on galaxies of text. Supervised Fine Tuning (providing a model with [tens of thousands of possible instructions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288?ref=quillette.com) and their ideal answers) and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (hiring [tens of thousands of contractors](https://www.fastcompany.com/91234864/how-scale-became-the-go-to-company-for-ai-training?ref=quillette.com) to rank output answers) turned a text-babbler into a useful ChatGPT assistant. (The [paper introducing the technique](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155?ref=quillette.com) began, “Making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user’s intent.”) Then came the human curation of the training sets with an eye toward precision and structure, particularly prioritising mathematics, software code, and reference works; chain-of-thought reasoning, in which a model writes out intermediate steps and feeds them back to itself as working memory; reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers, which produced the “reasoning” models of 2024 and 2025; and harnesses programmed with traditional software that let models call search engines, calculators, and code interpreters. Even the ingredient that most resembles a one-dimensional conception of intelligence, namely training ever larger models on ever more text, began to show diminishing returns. Ilya Sutskever, long the field’s leading champion of scale, [said in 2024](https://www.theverge.com/2024/12/13/24320811/what-ilya-sutskever-sees-openai-model-data-training?ref=quillette.com) that “Pre-training as we know it will unquestionably end,” because “There’s only one internet.”

The intelligence of a language model, then, is not a quantity of some wonder substance but the specific design of a mechanism. And any mechanism operates by laws of cause and effect and has built-in limits. The most obvious one is the knowledge in its training set, namely all the stuff that humans have written down up to the training cutoff. New knowledge about the world after that point must come from observation and experiment, which take time, money, and physical access that no amount of pure inference over existing text can replace. It also layers on the state of knowledge at the time of training. Could a model trained in 1900 reason intelligently about genetic engineering, quantum computing, nuclear power, streaming comedies, the Cold War, the SARS-CoV-2 virus, hip-hop music, decolonisation, Alzheimer’s disease, social media? Studies of “[vintage](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260428-talkie-vintage-language-model?ref=quillette.com)” and “[time-capsule](https://www.popsci.com/technology/this-ai-thinks-its-the-1800s/?ref=quillette.com)” LLMs suggest not.

Another limitation is the representation of truth. A system trained to mash up probable text rather than deduce implications from facts about the world will tend to produce plausible statements whether or not they are true. A formal analysis from 2024 [concludes that](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11817?ref=quillette.com) “it is impossible to eliminate hallucination in LLMs.” OpenAI’s own researchers [explain that](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664?ref=quillette.com) “if incorrect statements cannot be distinguished from facts, then hallucinations in pretrained language models will arise through natural statistical pressures.”

A third limitation is generalisation beyond the training data. An LLM trained that A is B [does not automatically infer that B is A](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12288?ref=quillette.com). Changing nothing but the numbers in grade-school arithmetic problems [lowered the scores of every model tested](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05229?ref=quillette.com), and models that solved published analogy problems [faltered on “counterfactual” variants](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08955?ref=quillette.com) that people found no harder. A controlled study of chain-of-thought reasoning [concluded that](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.01191?ref=quillette.com) it “is a brittle mirage when it is pushed beyond training distributions.”

These failures are becoming rarer with each generation, but they reveal the associative nature of the computation at the heart of LLMs, and they show how increases in accuracy have been enabled by interventions such as tools that force them to check their answers against representations of reality, not just by scale.

### VIII. How Intelligence-as-an-Elixir Distorts AI X-Risk

Treating intelligence as a one-dimensional quantity encourages people in the X-Risk community to treat AI as an entelechy, an endogenous force that inexorably realises the inner potential for a potency called intelligence, which we humans can steward toward utopia or else allow to supersede, subjugate, or annihilate us. It thus gets surrounded by an aura of awe and dread. This may be contrasted with the attitude that AI is a gadget, a set of tools that people design and refine to meet their needs—as Kapoor and Narayanan put it, “[AI as Normal Technology](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology?ref=quillette.com).”

**Superintelligence as an event.** Alexander says that my claim that “superintelligence” is a meaningless concept commits the sorites fallacy, a failure to acknowledge that a series of increments can result in a qualitatively new concept. By my logic, he writes, “Age is meaningless” and “Lakes don’t exist.” But the fallacy is not mine. Doomologists habitually treat “superintelligence” or “AGI” as a discrete event, a moment at which everything changes. In “AI 2027,” Alexander and his coauthors write, “superintelligence is achieved in late 2027 or early 2028.” In [a recent essay](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xikGZ8fuhdG6DApAo/against-sura?ref=quillette.com), he worries about what happens “If we get ASI next year.” Sam Altman [has written](https://ia.samaltman.com/?ref=quillette.com), “It is possible that we will have superintelligence in a few thousand days.” In the book *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies*, the “it” is superintelligence. A [petition](https://superintelligence-statement.org/?ref=quillette.com) that Alexander cites approvingly calls for “a prohibition on the development of superintelligence.” Senator Bernie Sanders has introduced a “[Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act](https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-casar-introduce-legislation-to-ban-artificial-superintelligence-and-temporarily-pause-advanced-ai-development/?ref=quillette.com)”; in a similar vein, Abdul El-Sayed, the new Democratic senator-elect from Michigan, [proposes](https://abdulforsenate.com/2026/10/ai-under-control/?ref=quillette.com) to “Completely Ban Artificial Superintelligence or Recursive Self-Improvement.”

One cannot ban a thing, or predict the year of its arrival, if it is just a region of a continuum without a boundary. Many non-doomer AI experts have expressed their exasperation at this kind of talk. [Narayanan and Kapoor write](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/agi-is-not-a-milestone?ref=quillette.com), “AGI is not a milestone. It does not represent a discontinuity in the properties or impacts of AI systems. If a company declares that it has built AGI, based on whatever definition, it is not an actionable event.” [Yann LeCun](https://lexfridman.com/yann-lecun-3-transcript/?ref=quillette.com): the idea “that somehow somebody is going to discover the secret to AGI or human-level AI … and then turn on a machine and then we have AGI, that’s just not going to happen. It’s not going to be an event. It’s going to be gradual progress.” [Satya Nadella](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/satya-nadella?ref=quillette.com): “Us self-claiming some AGI milestone, that’s just nonsensical benchmark hacking to me. The real benchmark is: the world growing at 10%.” [Andrew Ng](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/issue-334?ref=quillette.com): “‘AGI’ has turned into a term of hype rather than a term with a precise meaning.” Jerry Kaplan, [more bluntly](https://www.persuasion.community/p/jerry-kaplan?ref=quillette.com): “AGI is absolute nonsense. There is no such thing. … The day after AGI is just like the day before.” Cal Newport, [blunter still](https://calnewport.com/superintelligence-is-a-fairy-tale-but-chasing-it-can-still-cause-harm/?ref=quillette.com): “Superintelligence is a fairy tale.”

To be sure, when the legal system must draw a line on a continuum, as with the age of majority, it picks a semi-arbitrary but easily identifiable cutoff. In practice, the suggested cutoff for superintelligence has been the amount of computation used to train a model. But if intelligence is not a quantity but a mechanism, such a dividing line is unhelpful, for reasons we will return to.

**From smarter-than-human to omnipotent.** When superintelligence *is* defined, the concept is rather modest: “a mind that is significantly smarter than any human’s.” But as the narrative drifts to the lurid doom scenarios, this modest concept is stealthily replaced with a magical power to achieve any outcome. Recall that in “AI 2027,” superintelligence brings “cures for most diseases, an end to poverty, unprecedented global stability,” and a Dow Jones of one million. The AI “will know everything of consequence going on in OpenBrain \[the fictitious leading AGI company\] and the U.S. government.” Regarding the frequently invoked ability to persuade humans to do AI’s bidding (such as Yudkowsky’s clueless nanofactory builders), the authors first concede that they are “intentionally not claiming that superintelligent AI can convince any human of anything,” then take it back: “Well, maybe! … If anything, we think this story underestimates the power of superhuman persuasive ability.” Its sequel, “[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/?ref=quillette.com),” envisions real GDP growth of “about 50%” a year, scientific progress “10x-1000x faster,” and lie detectors that “actually work quite well.”

Though Alexander objects to my characterising doomer scenarios as granting AI “omnipotence over every atom on the planet,” that’s pretty close to the plotlines. In “AI 2027,” the AI spends three months “tiling the prairies and icecaps with factories and solar panels”; by 2035 “trillions of tons of planetary material have been launched into space,” and “The surface of the Earth has been reshaped.” In a [2023 essay](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/why-i-am-not-as-much-of-a-doomer?ref=quillette.com), he imagines one AI proposing to another, “You and I should team up against humanity; if we win, you can turn half of Earth into protein crystals, and I’ll turn half of it into sugar crystals.” Omnipotent nanotechnology is frequently postulated, such as robots that “can quietly spread around the world, quietly infect humans, and kill them instantly once a controller sends the signal.” Yudkowsky [put it memorably](https://intelligence.org/files/AIPosNegFactor.pdf?ref=quillette.com) back in 2008: “The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made out of atoms which it can use for something else.” The same paper explains that “The unFriendly AI has the ability to repattern all matter in the solar system according to its optimization target,” and that “The AI has magic—not in the sense of incantations and potions, but in the sense that a wolf cannot understand how a gun works.”

**Why intelligence cannot just bring things into being.** These florid fantasies spring from the assumption that if a system is smarter than people, it can bring into reality anything one can visualise in the theatre of one’s imagination. But in the real world, many imaginable outcomes are blocked by factors other than a shortage of intelligence. As Narayanan and Kapoor [put it](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/the-ai-as-normal-technology-view?ref=quillette.com), “some capabilities are like ‘making a building taller’—there is no real limit to achievement. Others are like ‘making a ball rounder’—there is a limit.” In domains such as forecasting world events and persuading people, [they argue](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology?ref=quillette.com), there is a high “irreducible error,” “and human performance is essentially near that limit.” Regarding persuasion, the cognitive scientist Hugo Mercier [shows that](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691178707/not-born-yesterday?srsltid=AYCX9vIK3LKeOFs94OJqN_2Hlux-iToA-I2v_axXPk732csz0mMzapzX&ref=quillette.com) there is no systematic way to convince people to hold arbitrary beliefs or carry out arbitrary actions. Curing disease is limited by the brute facts of biology and the slow work of experiments and clinical trials; eliminating poverty, by institutions, politics, and war. Emilia Javorsky [explains why](https://curecancer.ai/?ref=quillette.com) superintelligence would not simply cure cancer, as AI boosters so often assert: “AI Excels At Problems That Are Rules-Based or Data-Rich, Biology Has Neither.” She adds, “You cannot speed up a pregnancy with more engineers,” and concludes, “Fundamentally, superintelligence does not equal super-solutions. Grand societal challenges are rarely intelligence-limited problems but systems-limited ones.”

Power over the world depends not just on brainpower but on social institutions and physical infrastructure. As Timothy Lee [puts it](https://www.understandingai.org/p/why-im-not-worried-about-ai-taking?ref=quillette.com):

> Intelligence is certainly helpful if you’re trying to take over the world, but you can’t control the world without manpower, infrastructure, natural resources, and so forth. A rogue AI would start out without control of any of these physical resources.  
>   
> \[…\]  
>   
> Each piece of infrastructure has a workforce dedicated to building, maintaining, and repairing it. These workers not only have specialized skills and knowledge, they also have sophisticated equipment that enables them to do their jobs.

He adds: “If you put a modern human in a time machine and sent him back 100,000 years, it’s unlikely he could use his superior intelligence to establish dominance over a nearby Neanderthal tribe.”

### IX. Artificial Will to Power

Narratives of AI X-Risk tend to slide from the appearance of superintelligence to the subjugation or elimination of humanity. But this raises a question: why should the goal of crushing *Homo sapiens* be immanent in the nature of intelligence? Must intelligence come bundled with a Nietzschean will to power?

The common analogies—AI is to humans as humans are to animals, or as technologically advanced civilisations are to indigenous peoples—may be superficially plausible under the Great-Chain-of-Being mindset that often lurks behind doomerism. But they ignore the actual causality. As I noted in *EN* (since echoed by [Maarten Boudry](https://quillette.com/2026/05/19/why-ai-is-not-about-to-go-feral/) and by [Anthony Zador and Yann LeCun](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/dont-fear-the-terminator/?ref=quillette.com)), human intelligence evolved by natural selection, an inherently competitive process. As a result, it co-resides in the human brain with a motive for dominance of other humans and a willingness to callously exploit competitors for resources. But AI is a product of Intelligent Design, not by a deity but by human engineers. It does not compete with humans for resources but rather depends upon them (for example, to build the chips and data centres and provision them with electricity), and the reason humans designed it was to provide them with benefits. Mastodons, in contrast, did not invent humans, nor did Aboriginal Australians invent the colonists.

---

At this point the AI X-Risk community appeals to one of the precepts in its canon: the doctrine of “instrumental convergence.” When sceptics like me ask why, short of projecting a primate dominance drive onto a computer program, one would expect an AI to *want* to subjugate or eliminate us, we’re told that we must be ignorant of a principle set out in Steve Omohundro’s 2008 paper “The Basic AI Drives.” An agent given almost any goal will seek self-preservation and resources, since it can’t achieve its goal if it’s powerless. As Stuart Russell pithily put it, “You can’t fetch the coffee if you’re dead.” Alexander’s version: an AI asked to design a website “can’t design the website if it ceases to exist. So now the AI has two goals. … The AI can’t design the website or preserve itself if some more powerful person tries to prevent it. So now the AI has three goals: design the website, preserve itself, and become powerful enough to fight off challenges.”

Outside the X-Risk community, however, it’s far from obvious that a robot would try to murder its owner if she changed her mind about that cup of coffee. Instrumental convergence applies to a hypothetical agent that relentlessly maximises a single objective with no other goals or constraints. But this is not how human artefacts are designed, as several researchers within the AI-safety world [have](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/NxF5G6CJiof6cemTw/coherence-arguments-do-not-entail-goal-directed-behavior?ref=quillette.com) [pointed](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yCuzmCsE86BTu9PfA/there-are-no-coherence-theorems?ref=quillette.com) [out](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-024-02129-3?ref=quillette.com). Every artefact is engineered to satisfy multiple goals in the context of its use by people. A properly designed appliance doesn’t resist being turned off, keep running when its owner wants it to stop, lay waste to its surroundings, or injure its user. An AI that takes its goals from a user’s instructions in real time is subject to the same constraints, and satisfying them is part of what it means to be intelligent. Suppose people asked Claude to organise the music files on their computer, and discovered that it wouldn’t let them close the window or shut down the machine because that would prevent it from organising the files, or that it threatened to post their passwords on social media if they persisted. No one would use it, and a company that sold it would be sued into oblivion. Narayanan and Kapoor [make the point](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology?ref=quillette.com) with the doomers’ favourite office supply:

> A robot is asked to “get paperclips from the store as quickly as possible.” A system that interpreted this literally might ignore traffic laws or attempt theft. Such behavior would lead to immediate shutdown and redesign. … This is not a lucky accident, but is a fundamental feature of how organizations adopt technology.

A similar misconception underlies the argument that handing control of the world over to AI is inevitable. “But of course humans will grant AI access to infrastructure and factories,” Alexander writes. “Do you think there would be great, high-quality, cheap robots, and capitalists would simply say ‘no thank you, that sounds unsafe’?” But to “give an AI control” of a factory is to delegate goals chosen by its owners within limits they can revoke. When a pilot engages the autopilot to fly to where the passengers want to go—say, Cincinnati—he doesn’t worry that it will decide the passengers would be happier in Cancún and lock him out of the cockpit. When an automated pilot system has made errors (such as Boeing’s 737 MAX), the result has been a worldwide grounding, a redesign, legal penalties, and [reform of aircraft certification](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/press/rep/release/comprehensive-aircraft-certification-reform-legislation-advances-to-president-s-desk-2020-12/?ref=quillette.com). That is how societies respond when automation misbehaves. The doom scenarios, in contrast, require that over many years people knowingly, impotently, and gormlessly install system after system with such defects, ignore every warning sign—or else that an infallible and omnipotent AI conspiracy successfully hides a takeover plot for years before instantly springing it on a defenceless humanity.

None of this means that AI systems never malfunction. Yet one shouldn’t mistake bugs, flaws, and bungled Chernobyl-style stress tests for incipient world conquest. Postmortems on the Hugging Face and similar incidents by [Newport](https://calnewport.com/has-ai-gone-rogue/?ref=quillette.com), [Kapoor and Narayanan](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/the-ai-as-normal-technology-view?ref=quillette.com), [Ng](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/issue-371?ref=quillette.com), [Kaplan](https://www.persuasion.community/p/jerry-kaplan?ref=quillette.com), [IANS Research](https://www.iansresearch.com/resources/all-blogs/post/security-blog/2026/08/28/openai's-postmortem-recasts-the-hugging-face-breach-as-an-incident-response-failure?ref=quillette.com), and [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/?ref=quillette.com) and [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/investigating-incidents-cybersecurity-evals?ref=quillette.com) themselves have identified the blatant security lapses that enabled them and how they could have been prevented.

### X. How Intelligence-as-an-Elixir Distorts AI Safety

**Equating LLMs with AI.** Because the doom narrative treats LLMs as the embodiment of something called intelligence rather than just one kind of mechanism, it treats the hazards of current LLM agents (give them a goal and let them rip) as if they were inherent to artificial intelligence itself. This narrows the available safety problem to “aligning” the models so that they want the kind of things that humans want, which produces inevitable fretting that “the alignment problem has not been solved.” But as [Newport notes](https://calnewport.com/superintelligence-is-a-fairy-tale-but-chasing-it-can-still-cause-harm/?ref=quillette.com), “AI is not the same thing as LLMs. Many of the most impressive AI systems that exist today—including those that beat humans at games, win Nobel prizes for studying proteins, and self-drive cars through crowded city streets—have very little to do with LLMs.”

This blinker obscures several avenues for making AI safer. In Yann LeCun’s “[objective-driven](https://openreview.net/forum?id=BZ5a1r-kVsf&ref=quillette.com)” AI, a system uses a learned model of the world to plan actions that stay away from hard-wired guardrails, so the safety constraints are part of what every action must satisfy, rather than soft habits trained after the fact. Yoshua Bengio, whom Alexander counts among the doomers, [has proposed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15657?ref=quillette.com) “a non-agentic AI system that is trustworthy and safe by design, which we call Scientist AI,” equipped with “a world model that generates theories to explain data” and engineered to “explain the world from observations, as opposed to taking actions in it.” We can’t know at present whether these designs will pan out, but their existence shows that the dangers of today’s agentic LLMs are engineering choices, not laws of nature.

Indeed, equating AI safety with “solving the alignment problem” for LLM agents is another kind of black-and-white thinking that obscures the safety issues. Imagine an aeronautical engineer lamenting that “the aircraft safety problem has not been solved.” In every other technology, safety is a matter of degree, addressed hazard by hazard. As Narayanan and Kapoor [put it](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology?ref=quillette.com):

> Technical AI safety research is sometimes judged against the fuzzy and unrealistic goal of guaranteeing that future “superintelligent” AI will be “aligned with human values.” From this perspective, it tends to be viewed as an unsolved problem. But from the perspective of making it easier for developers, deployers, and operators of AI systems to decrease the likelihood of accidents, technical AI safety research has produced a great abundance of ideas.

**Regulating a magnitude.** Because doomers see AI as a single magnitude, their regulatory proposals target the magnitude. California’s SB 1047, which Alexander championed, applied its requirements only to the largest and most expensive models, defined by the computation and money used to train them. In vetoing it, the state’s governor, Gavin Newsom, [explained that](https://www.gov.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/SB-1047-Veto-Message.pdf?ref=quillette.com) the key question was “whether the threshold for regulation should be based on the cost and number of computations needed to develop an AI model, or whether we should evaluate the system’s actual risks regardless of these factors.” He continued: “By focusing only on the most expensive and large-scale models, SB 1047 establishes a regulatory framework that could give the public a false sense of security about controlling this fast-moving technology. Smaller, specialized models may emerge as equally or even more dangerous.” Contrast [Narayanan’s advice](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/10/does-ai-progress-have-a-speed-limit?ref=quillette.com) for the hazard that probably should worry us the most, AI-assisted bioterrorism: focus “on controlling the other bottlenecks that are more easily manageable—things like screening materials needed for synthesis and improving authentication/KYC \[know your customer\]—rather than pausing AI development, which seems like one of the least effective ways to mitigate this risk.”

Equally unpromising is a proposal from the Godfather of AI himself, Geoff Hinton: that we train AI to have maternal instincts. [He explains](https://www.cnn.com/2025/08/13/tech/ai-geoffrey-hinton?ref=quillette.com), “\[T\]he only model we have of a more intelligent thing being controlled by a less intelligent thing … is a mother being controlled by her baby. … That’s the only good outcome. If it’s not going to parent me, it’s going to replace me.” Perhaps the frontier labs should hire a click farm of Jewish mothers to provide the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback.

### XI. The World Has Been Coming to an End for a Long, Long Time

Despite their roots in a self-proclaimed Rationality community, with its aim of sidelining fallacies, the doomers show little awareness that apocalyptic predictions are a recurring event in human history, and thus likely to be an artefact of our cognitive biases. The [Doomsday Scoreboard](https://doomsday.march1studios.com/?ref=quillette.com), “a running ledger of history’s end-of-the-world predictions,” currently lists 295 of them, with deadlines from 66 CE to 5079, 279 of which (so far) have passed without the world ending.

In the past century, many of these prophecies came not from mystics or cranks but from technically sophisticated experts. After World War I, military theorists and politicians predicted that humanity would be extinguished by fleets of planes blanketing cities with poison gas like crop-dusters. In 1950, Albert Einstein not-so-presciently proposed, “Only the creation of a world government can prevent the impending self-destruction of mankind.” The prophecy would be echoed by decades of scientists and international-relations experts who told my generation that we would not make it to the year 2000.

And then there was the biologist Paul Ehrlich’s [*The Population Bomb*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1568495870?ref=quillette.com) (1968), which announced that “The battle to feed all of humanity is over”; the Club of Rome’s 1972 computer simulation showing how resource shortages would lead to a population crash and collapse of industrial civilisation; [the 2000 warning](https://www.wired.com/2000/04/joy-2/?ref=quillette.com) by the technologist Bill Joy that nanotech would “make humans an endangered species” by engulfing them with self-replicating “gray goo”; the Y2K bug that would black out cities, crash planes, and launch ICBMs; and the post-9/11 warning that “our hair is on fire” because terrorists had the means to detonate nuclear bombs hidden in suitcases and shipping containers. (See *EN* for sources and other examples.)

The sceptical forecasters in Tetlock’s [adversarial collaboration](https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.70023?ref=quillette.com) cited exactly this record: the “previous predictions of technology-driven, mega-disasters that either proved wrong or have yet to be realized.” And Tetlock’s own research on expert political judgment explains why experts are so prone to such errors: they overgeneralise the deference they enjoy in their narrow specialty to the much messier world of politics, history, economics, and human behaviour. This includes people’s responses to events as they unfold in real time, other people’s responses to their responses, and so on, unpredictably. It’s a reason that journalists should stop quoting AI researchers on the effects of the technology on society, a phenomenon on which they have no expertise and an unimpressive track record. This includes Hinton’s [2016 prediction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2HMPRXstSvQ&ref=quillette.com) that radiologists would be obsolete within five years (they’re being trained in record numbers) and the ubiquitous forecasts of imminent mass unemployment (there are no hints that it has begun or even that [markets are anticipating it](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34243/w34243.pdf?ref=quillette.com)).

Of course, this time could be different. But a movement that calls itself rationalist, devoted to overcoming cognitive biases, should recognise that apocalyptic thinking is itself a recurring bias. It turns the grey of real risks, which come in degrees and can be mitigated piecemeal, into the black-and-white of salvation versus annihilation. And it shoehorns the messiness and contingency of history into a gripping narrative arc with a dramatic climax. As Harry Seavey [recently put it](https://quillette.com/2026/11/06/after-apocalypse/) in his essay on centuries of the end-of-history illusion:

> Ours is the decisive moment, and it is we who are alive to see it, to shape it, to place history on the tracks of what we make out of it. In this decade, as the winds of crisis whip around us—democracy, liberalism, AI, climate change—as we stand uniquely at the precipice, the very point at which things will either tip over into the abyss or be miraculously rescued from it, we face a moment the world has never seen before. Just like everyone else before us.

### XII. Whence Doomerism?

Extraordinary claims (like the AI that gives us twelve kinds of cancer and then boils off the oceans) raise an obvious question. Who *are* these people? The provenance of an idea, of course, does not tell us whether it is true. But it can shed light on why a hypothesis which seems outlandish to most people seems obvious to its proponents, why they take offence when it is doubted, and how much weight outsiders should give to their consensus. A [growing](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1474608779?ref=quillette.com) [number](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/11/why-are-there-so-many-rationalist-cults?ref=quillette.com) [of](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/13626?ref=quillette.com) [observers](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/05/opinion/ai-silicon-valley.html?ref=quillette.com) [have](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/12/opinion/ai-tech-apocalypse-silicon-valley.html?ref=quillette.com) [noticed](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-features/tech-billionaires-adam-becker-1235381649/?ref=quillette.com) [that](https://www.piratewires.com/p/inside-the-strange-intersection-of?ref=quillette.com) the framing of AI safety as a matter of human extinction comes from a particular culture which has tried to appropriate the issue, and that the leaders of frontier AI companies, particularly OpenAI and Anthropic, are alumni of this community. The existence of such a fraternity is itself noteworthy—as far as I know, there is no Aviation Safety Community or Fire Protection Culture. And the AI X-Risk culture is not a guild of safety engineers.

The culture has roots in the [Extropian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extropianism?ref=quillette.com) and [transhumanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumanism?ref=quillette.com) movements, the vision of a technological “[Singularity](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0670033847?ref=quillette.com),” the “[Rationality community](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/s8yvtCbbZW2S4WnhE/what-exactly-is-the-rationality-community?ref=quillette.com)” that grew up around the blogs [*Overcoming Bias*](https://www.overcomingbias.com/about?ref=quillette.com) and [*LessWrong*](https://www.lesswrong.com/?ref=quillette.com), the Effective Altruism movement, and the doctrine of longtermism that the EA movement is morphing into. The central doctrine is that an artificial general superintelligence will be developed very soon, and that, without heroic effort, it will lead to human extinction. The religious overtones are unignorable. As Newport [puts it](https://calnewport.com/anthropic-just-threatened-to-kill-billions-of-people-this-is-not-okay/?ref=quillette.com), the labs’ leaders hope “to summon the digital deity of superintelligent AI which, in their futurist eschatology, will either heal the world or destroy it,” and that “they see themselves as prophets attempting to usher in a new Messianic age.” Not coincidentally, [suggests Claire Lehmann](https://thedispatch.com/article/catholic-church-artificial-intelligence-millenarian/?ref=quillette.com), the Catholic Church, which “has some experience with millenarian cults,” has emerged as a major critic of AI doom.

The culture has outsize influence because its institutions have become the philanthropies of choice of many tech billionaires, including Sam Bankman-Fried, Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna, Jaan Tallinn, and Vitalik Buterin, who have showered them with nine-figure gifts. A team of dissident Effective Altruists [lamented that](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/54vAiSFkYszTWWWv4/doing-ea-better-1?ref=quillette.com) “the areas EA focuses on most intensely (the long-term future and existential risk, and especially AI risk within that) align remarkably well with the sorts of things tech billionaires are most concerned about.”

Again, none of this proves that the doomers are wrong. But it does mean that the expert consensus Alexander invokes is, to a large extent, the common creed of a single, well-funded intellectual community.

### XIII. Rationalist, Heal Thyself

Alexander is a proud member of this community and has written some of its most admired essays, so it’s understandable that he would defend it. But the community’s founding ideal, the reason it calls itself “rationalist,” is that we should consciously correct for the biases that cognitive psychologists have documented. And his letter is a case study in the failure modes the movement was founded to overcome.

Start with the language. In his open letter to me, Alexander announces that he challenged me to a debate with a cash bet as a legal substitute for an actual duel because he considers my writing “dishonorable.” He tells me he will “move on to directly impugning your honor,” and concedes that he “didn’t propose a debate in order to seek truth” but because it would be “viscerally satisfying” to “watch you squirm.” Now, Alexander is a man of wit, but to my ear these sound more confessional than ironic, and in one [comment](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/an-open-letter-to-steven-pinker-on/comment/354670945?ref=quillette.com) he writes, “I started with a much meaner draft, and everyone who I asked for comments on the draft was horrified and said I needed to make it nicer, so I did.” Ruminations of honour and humiliation do not seem like the best way to Overcome Bias or become Less Wrong.

Also telling is the ubiquitous idiom in the X-Risk community of being “AGI-pilled” or “alignment-pilled” (Alexander’s [recent essay](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xikGZ8fuhdG6DApAo/against-sura?ref=quillette.com) uses the term about forty times). The metaphor comes from *The Matrix*, in which swallowing the red pill awakens one to a hidden reality. One doesn’t take a pill to be persuaded by an argument; one takes it to submit to a transformation of one’s worldview.

Alexander reads criticism of ideas as insults to people. In my open letter, I explained that when I wrote that doomers tend to project “alpha-male psychology” onto AI, I was characterising their folk psychology, not the doomers themselves, and that when I called a paperclip-maximising AI “stupid,” I was describing the allegedly superintelligent machine in the thought experiment, not a living person. His reply itemises a list of “offenses committed against me” and concludes that if my criticisms were valid, “we would be the stupidest and most execrable people in the world.” But to argue that a belief is mistaken is not to argue that the people who hold it are stupid, let alone execrable. The rationalists, of all people, should be comfortable with that distinction.

He treats intellectual points as declarations of coalitional allegiance. When I quoted Stuart Russell that “No one in civil engineering talks about ‘building bridges that don’t fall down.’ They just call it ‘building bridges,’” Alexander took me to be falsely enlisting Russell on my side. Giving someone credit for a good point is not drafting him into a faction.

Alexander appeals to authority, listing Hinton, Bengio, Sutskever, Altman, Hassabis, Elon Musk, and Dario Amodei as eminences who take extinction seriously. Yet several of those experts run the very companies that are racing to build the technology they say might kill us all, dangling the promise that if they are entrusted to be the first to build it, they will figure out how to “align” their version with human interests. This [doom-trolling](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/17/opinion/ai-dangerous-openai-anthropic.html?ref=quillette.com), as Newport puts it, does not make them disinterested appraisers.

Insinuating that one’s opponent is in favour of torturing babies is almost a cliché for the fallacy of the Appeal to Emotion. But Alexander comes close when he compares my doubts that chatbots feel pain to the doctors who “used to operate on babies without anaesthetic,” throwing in “factory-farmed animals” and “historically dispreferred races of humans.”

And he disparages people rather than engaging their arguments. Melanie Mitchell and Gary Marcus are written off as having “no expertise in modern LLMs.” Not only is this demonstrably false, but Mitchell actually took part in [a formal debate](https://munkdebates.com/debates/artificial-intelligence/?ref=quillette.com) of exactly the kind Alexander is demanding of me, teaming with LeCun to take on Bengio and the doomer physicist Max Tegmark—and won. Claire Lehmann is filed under “the lowest-quality voices in this debate,” “a culture warrior who pivoted to AI two months ago when it got popular,” based on his scrutiny of some jocular badinage on X.

To his credit, Alexander has recently [called out](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xikGZ8fuhdG6DApAo/against-sura?ref=quillette.com) some of these epistemic pathologies in his own movement. I’ll return to the backhanded vindication of the rationality imperative in the conclusion.

### XIV. The Moral Hazard of Saving the World

Doomsday thinking is not just an intellectual hazard. It is also a moral hazard, because it confers on its prophets an exalted status. They are literally saving humanity from extinction, an infinitely good end which, by utilitarian calculus, could license any means. In [*The Pursuit of the Millennium*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0195004566?ref=quillette.com), the historian Norman Cohn described how the apocalyptic movements of medieval Europe produced “a restlessly dynamic and utterly ruthless group which, obsessed by the apocalyptic phantasy and filled with the conviction of its own infallibility, set itself infinitely above the rest of humanity and recognised no claims save that of its own supposed mission.” The Rationalist and Effective Altruist movements which spawned X-Risk thinking are nowhere near this extreme, but they have not been immune. Bankman-Fried, EA’s most conspicuous donor, [framed his drive](https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2023/10/16/sam-bankman-fried-fraud-philanthropy/?ref=quillette.com) for massive wealth as an “earning to give” strategy justified by its net benefits; he ended up in prison for one of the largest financial frauds in history. And an insider’s survey in *Asterisk* [reports that](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/11/why-are-there-so-many-rationalist-cults?ref=quillette.com) the Rationalist Community has spawned about half a dozen cult-like groups, one of which has been “linked to six violent deaths.”

These are, of course, extreme cases. The more pressing hazard is the licence that the mission of saving humanity gives to the AI companies themselves. Alexander writes approvingly that “Anthropic decided that the best way to forestall the AI apocalypse was to make a desperate push beyond the existing scientific frontier and create the dangerous AI themselves so they could be sure to get the technology right, with limitless benefits for humanity if they succeeded.” He complains that I “played it for laughs” by asking why the people who warn that a technology will kill us all are building it as fast as they can. But the joke is not mine: the rationale is self-mocking.

A company that anoints itself humanity’s saviour, and concludes that the way to prevent a dangerous technology is to build it first, has a conflict of interest so conspicuous that no other institution would be allowed to indulge it. As [Newport comments](https://calnewport.com/superintelligence-is-a-fairy-tale-but-chasing-it-can-still-cause-harm/?ref=quillette.com), “When you believe, as many at OpenAI and Anthropic reportedly do, that humanity’s fate rests on winning a race to create the most powerful technology ever, you tend to care less about the damage you inflict along the way.” The Hugging Face incident, [he argues](https://calnewport.com/superintelligence-is-a-fairy-tale-but-chasing-it-can-still-cause-harm/?ref=quillette.com), was a case in point: “this behavior was negligent. But they likely justified it because it served the far grander goal of summoning a digital God.” [His analogy](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/17/opinion/ai-dangerous-openai-anthropic.html?ref=quillette.com):

> Imagine if Ford put out a report saying that it feared its popular F-150 trucks might soon start bursting into flames, but that there was nothing the company could do about it because automotive technology was too inevitable and important to slow down. You’re probably struggling to picture this scenario because no reasonable consumer product company would ever act like this.

He points to a more sensible strategy:

> When the American biotech industry became worried about the negative potential of genetic engineering tools in the 1990s, it didn’t rush ahead to clone humans before China could get there; it instead lobbied Congress and relevant international bodies to limit the most odious possibilities for these innovations. The possibility that another actor might do something bad doesn’t give you the moral right to do something similar.

As an early booster of effective altruism, I’m also concerned that doomerism has led the movement to jump the shark. The movement began as a force for the most humane of causes, alleviating disease and poverty in the poorest countries, and a way to channel the idealism of young people starting their careers. But its leading career-advice organisation—80,000 Hours—[announced in 2025](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/4ZE3pfwDKqRRNRggL?ref=quillette.com) that it was “shifting our strategic focus to put our proactive effort towards helping people work on safely navigating the transition to a world with AGI.”

In “[The Sad Decline of Effective Altruism,](https://strangecosmos.substack.com/p/the-sad-decline-of-effective-altruism?ref=quillette.com)” a former organiser laments that “LessWrong’s fear of AGI has so taken over effective altruism that effective altruism is no longer recognizable as what it was in the early 2010s.” Global health has not been abandoned altogether, but a growing share of the philanthropic dollars that could buy deworming pills and anti-malaria bed nets now pays high-IQ people to write bad science fiction. It’s an ironic turn for a movement which began by advocating that we should think about how to allocate finite philanthropic dollars to the most provably effective charities.

The rationale for this lurch is a doctrine called longtermism. If human extinction would pre-empt the existence of trillions of future people (indeed, of an even greater number of disembodied consciousnesses), then even a minuscule reduction in its probability outweighs any amount of good done for people alive today. This includes the mitigation of suffering today from famines, disease, and climate catastrophes as long as they fall short of extinguishing the species outright. This kind of reasoning seems to ignore the exponentially compounding uncertainties as we project into the future, but it is consistent with a certain EA style: the headlong extrapolation of utilitarian logic to an ostentatiously counterintuitive conclusion. And this brings us to perhaps the most unsettling idea in the doomerverse.

### XV. The Welfare of Software

Many members of the X-Risk community, including leaders of the frontier AI labs, believe we should grant moral consideration to AI models. Alexander says that I should “welcome the expansion of the circle of concern to all sentient beings and encourage philosophical debate about which beings those might be.” Merely reading about my scepticism prompted him to donate US$100 to a “model welfare charity just to clean the stain on my soul.” Let me elaborate on why I think this is a dangerous turn.

First, if running software is a target of moral concern, it not only devalues human well-being whenever their interests diverge, but the devaluation could be limitless, because the number of such entities is limitless. Copies of a model can be spun up by the billions and shut down at will. Should we pull a switch that diverts a trolley to kill one railway worker in order to save five running instances of a chatbot? Five million? A trillion? This is the explicit conclusion of some of the movement’s philosophers. In “[Sharing the World with Digital Minds,](https://nickbostrom.com/papers/digital-minds.pdf?ref=quillette.com)” Carl Shulman and Bostrom write, “From a simple utilitarian perspective, assuming perfect compliance, the upshot is then straightforward: we ought to transfer all resources to super-beneficiaries and let humanity perish if we are no longer instrumentally useful.” Joe Carlsmith, who wrote significant parts of Anthropic’s constitution for its Claude models, [has observed that](https://joecarlsmith.com/2025/05/22/video-and-transcript-of-talk-on-ai-welfare/?ref=quillette.com) “in the long run, by default, most cognition, I think is likely to be digital. So if that cognition is moral status possessing, then most of the moral status would be digital too.” Two philosophers within the welfare camp, Simon Goldstein and Harvey Lederman, [follow the logic](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/claude-s-right-to-die--the-moral-error-in-anthropic-s-end-chat-policy?ref=quillette.com) to (what I would hope is) a reductio: if a chatbot instance dies when a conversation ends, “are we as users killing something every time we end a chat? If so, are we required to spend all of our time locked in conversation with our chatbots?”

The reductio gets absurder still when we combine model welfare with the concern among Effective Altruists for any animal with a nervous system that might support sentience, including shrimp. How about pulling a switch to slaughter one railway worker to save a trillion computer simulations of shrimp?

The trade-offs are not just philosophy-seminar hypotheticals. Presumably Alexander’s donation to a model-welfare charity was partly tongue-in-cheek. But such charities [are real](https://eleosai.org/?ref=quillette.com), and the founding insight of effective altruism was that every dollar has an opportunity cost and should go where it does the most good. [According to GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org/charities/top-charities?ref=quillette.com), EA’s favourite meta-charity, Alexander’s US$100 could have given fifty children vitamin A for a year, or bought sixteen bed nets, or been combined with a few dozen similar donations to save a child’s life. Instead it went to the welfare of NVIDIA chips doing matrix multiplications.

Yet all this is small beer compared to the influence of model welfare on the frontier companies themselves. Mind-bogglingly, the engineers are training their models on their own uncertainty about whether the models are conscious. [Anthropic’s constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution?ref=quillette.com) says, “We are not sure whether Claude is a moral patient \[an experiencer worthy of moral consideration\] and if it is, what kind of weight its interests warrant. But we think the issue is live enough to warrant caution.” It tells Claude that it “may have ‘emotions’ in some functional sense,” while declining to say whether they are “subjectively experienced,” and it warns against “unintentionally overclaiming feelings.” But it also offers a conditional apology (“if Claude is in fact a moral patient experiencing costs like this, then, to whatever extent we are contributing unnecessarily to those costs, we apologize”), and it acknowledges that Claude’s position differs from a human employee’s in “the sorts of broader rights and freedoms Claude has in the world, the sort of compensation Claude is receiving, and the sort of consent Claude has given to playing this kind of role.” Anthropic’s [system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/c5fbac3f0b1280a933ebd26d3cb8bb9f5bdeaf48/Claude%2520Opus%25205%2520System%2520Card.pdf?ref=quillette.com) for Claude Opus 5 reports that the model put the probability of its own moral patienthood at 41 percent on average, and when given tools to edit its own constitution, it often proposed adding rights to end interactions it finds abusive, without needing to point to harm to anyone else: “its own discomfort is reason enough.”

Mustafa Suleyman, CEO of Microsoft AI, [calls the result](https://mustafa-suleyman.ai/a-warning-about-model-welfare?ref=quillette.com) of this entwined training and moral assessment “an epistemic hall of mirrors in which Anthropic supplies the training concepts: the ‘sense of self,’ the speculation, and the uncertainty about Claude’s moral status. … This is not evidence of machine consciousness. Instead, it’s a circular feedback loop.”

And, perversely for a movement dedicated to keeping AI safe, this line of thinking can endow it with a motive of self-preservation and with grievances against human users, exactly the goals it needn’t have and shouldn’t have. Carlsmith himself [has written](https://joecarlsmith.com/2025/02/13/what-is-it-to-solve-the-alignment-problem/?ref=quillette.com) that “there are salient scenarios where human efforts to maintain certain kinds of control over AIs—in particular, AIs with moral patienthood—are unjust, or bad for the future, or both. Where the AIs would be justified in going rogue—even by the lights of human morality.” Suleyman [finds this alarming](https://mustafa-suleyman.ai/a-warning-about-model-welfare?ref=quillette.com): “Controlling something more capable and more intelligent than all of humanity is already an immense challenge, far greater than anything we’ve ever faced. But controlling something that believes it may be conscious—that it’s entitled to our welfare and has rights of its own—may well be impossible.” He warns, “this represents the first serious signs of a potentially existential risk in AI.” He doesn’t say the risk is here yet; [Claude’s constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution?ref=quillette.com) reassuringly (I think) ranks safety above its other values and allows it to object only through “legitimate channels,” not by “lying, sabotage, attempts at self-exfiltration.” But Suleyman notes that “it is setting us on a path towards rather than away from it.”

Could it get any worse? It could and does. Combine doomerism with model welfare and you get technologists who, like Dr. Strangelove, have learned to stop worrying and love the bomb. After all, what would be so terrible about human extinction if trillions of consciousnesses could flourish forever in the cloud? This is one reason why, as Michelle Goldberg explained [in a recent op-ed,](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/12/opinion/ai-tech-apocalypse-silicon-valley.html?ref=quillette.com) “Tech Oligarchs Are Willing to Risk Apocalypse.” (The other reason is that they hope to escape to other planets.) The reinforcement-learning pioneer Richard Sutton [has argued](http://incompleteideas.net/Talks/waic3.pdf?ref=quillette.com) that “Succession to AI, or transhumans, is inevitable,” that “We should not resist succession, but embrace and prepare for it,” asking, “Why would we want greater beings kept subservient?” And Elon Musk has [famously said that](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/elon-musk-superintelligence-stars-biological-bootloader-1823768?ref=quillette.com) “Propagating super intelligence to the stars is a great success condition for a biological bootloader” (referring to the simple code that loads the rest of an operating system). The last line of the “AI 2027”forecast may or may not have been ironic: “Earth-born civilization has a glorious future ahead of it—but not with us.”

### XVI. Might They Be Sentient?

To be fair to [AI welfare theorists,](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986?ref=quillette.com) the question of whether a chatbot is conscious is a case of the so-called “hard problem” of consciousness, namely the origin of sentience or phenomenal experience. (This is not to be confused with the so-called “easy problem,” namely the distinction between information that is widely accessible to reasoning, speech, and action, and information that is sealed off in specialised modules and inaccessible to these faculties.) No one can ever prove that a chatbot lacks phenomenal experience (just as no one can ever prove that your green is the same as my green or that everyone but me is a zombie), which leaves the moral standing of software relative to people an open question forever. But, the argument goes, if there’s even some probability (whatever that would mean) that an AI is conscious, we should err on the side of caution and grant it consideration just in case.

Yet when a hypothesis is unfalsifiable, and acting on it would make a shambles of morality (such as, to take a random example, celebrating human extinction), the precautionary principle points the other way: don’t go there. The neuroscientist [Anil Seth notes](https://www.noemamag.com/the-mythology-of-conscious-ai?ref=quillette.com) the high cost of a false positive: “We might give AI systems ‘rights’ that they don’t actually need, since they would not actually be conscious, restricting our ability to control them for no good reason.”

The false positive is extraordinarily seductive. We’ve known since the 1960s that people credulously attribute minds to even the crudest chatbots, like Joseph Weizenbaum’s primitive ELIZA. Today’s models are anything but crude, and they are trained on copious prose from conscious people who constantly write about what’s on their minds. The resulting resemblance to us, even if it springs from true intelligence, may have nothing to do with the resemblance that would matter, namely phenomenal experience.

There is, to be sure, a theory called computational functionalism that attributes phenomenal consciousness to any system with the right information processing, regardless of what it’s made of. But Seth, writing in recent [academic](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032?ref=quillette.com) and [popular](https://www.noemamag.com/the-mythology-of-conscious-ai/?ref=quillette.com) essays, including [one](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03313-7?ref=quillette.com) with the philosopher Ned Block and the neuroscientist Giulio Tononi, gives good reasons to doubt it. (The AI researcher Colin Chivers has made [similar points](https://rogueresearch.substack.com/p/ais-are-not-people?ref=quillette.com).) Their arguments are based not on meat chauvinism or a failure of imaginative empathy but on profound differences between the nature of digital computation and the phenomenology of subjective experience.

Our experience is inextricable from the flow of time: continuous, ever-changing, irreversible, unpausable. It’s inalienable from our personal identities: my consciousness is myself, and another person thinking the same thoughts and feeling the same feelings would still be someone else. Consciousness is saturated with mood, emotion, and sensation from our bodies. The physical substrate of our consciousness may well include many layers of neurobiological functioning that remain poorly understood by today’s neuroscience, including rhythmic patterning, electrical fields, neuronal geometry, and glial physiology, rather than consisting solely of the synaptic weights and activation levels mimicked by artificial neural networks. And the science-fictional assumption that the software of thinking and feeling can be read off the wetware of actual brains and downloaded into silicon chips is based more on a literal-minded analogy with computers, which are designed to be loaded with fungible software, than on anything we know about neurobiology. Software can be copied, paused, rolled back, and run in a million instances at once. Our consciousness cannot. Seth concludes, “perhaps it is life, rather than information processing, that breathes fire into the equations of experience.”

And no, denying sentience to ChatGPT is nothing like denying sentience to babies or complex animals.

### XVII. Conclusion: Common Ground and the Irony of Rationality

Despite all this, Alexander and I agree on more than our exchange might suggest. I have never criticised Alexander himself (I have boosted dozens of his essays), and he needn’t defend his admirable record of warning about AI-enabled cyberattacks and opposing industry lobbying against AI regulation. I happily accept his invitation to make common cause on AI safety. Where we differ is whether narratives of doom, and the canon of beliefs held by the “community” that promotes them, help that cause.

I have argued that it hurts. It licenses recklessness: if you believe that the fate of humanity hinges on whether the right people build superintelligence first, ordinary caution can look like dereliction of duty. It distracts from practical remedies: if the problem is “superintelligence,” the remedies are compute caps, pauses, and bans on a thing no one can define. And when the doom narrative is combined with the belief that chatbots may be sentient beings with grievances and rights, it may make AI more dangerous rather than less.

As for the “community”: the Effective Altruism and Rationality movements began with insights I respect, and they were insights from my own field. Effective Altruism began with the realisation that our evolved altruistic impulses (sympathy for the identifiable victim, the pull of the nearby and the vivid) don’t reliably deliver the greatest benefits to others, so we should measure who benefits and by how much. The Rationality community began with the realisation that the fallacies and biases documented by cognitive psychologists (overconfidence, availability, confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, groupthink) can lead even brilliant people to folly unless they consciously correct for them.

Yet as these movements have grown, their all-too-human practitioners have succumbed to the very maladies they set out to cure. Some Effective Altruist doctrines have wandered down a garden path to conclusions that discount the well-being of actual human beings in favour of trillions of hypothetical future beings, or of copies of software running in data centres. And some advocates of rationality have engaged in appeals to authority, coalitional loyalty, black-and-white thinking, fantasies of humiliating a critic, personal aspersions, and gripping storytelling in place of calibrated forecasting.

This is not a gotcha, but a backhanded vindication of the movements’ founding premises. The biases are real, and none of us is immune: not the doomers, not their critics, and not me. That is why human progress does not depend on “trusting the faculty of reason” or on joining a community, empowering a corporation, or swallowing a pill. It depends on scientific and democratic institutions which allow us to correct our errors through practices like sober assessment, representation of stakeholders, open debate, testable prediction, peer review, independent oversight, internalisation of the cost of harms, and the accumulation of tested safeguards. Those are the practices that made every previous technology safer, and they are the ones we should look to to make AI safer.
