I’m fascinated by the sex gurus of Cornell University’s Health office. I’m also learning a lot from them.

Take, for example, a [pamphlet posted online](https://health.cornell.edu/sites/health/files/pdf-library/self-exploration.pdf) titled simply “Self-Exploration.” It explains that

> As you explore your body and tune in to the pleasure of touch, you may notice your genitals responding. If you have a penis, it may become erect. Similarly, a vulva may become swollen or engorged and a vagina may become “wet” (lubricated). Such physiological responses are automatic (like increasing heart rate or blood pressure) and may or may not correlate with actual sexual desire. It’s okay to simply notice.

This was all news to me! Especially the part about penises.

[“Sexual Pleasure: Finding the ‘Sweet Spot’ ”](https://health.cornell.edu/sites/health/files/pdf-library/sexual-pleasure.pdf) was similarly educational. “There are many pleasurable benefits to sex,” explains that two-pager. “It can provide a release of ‘feel-good’ hormones, making it a great stress buster. It can also be a fun way to wake up in the morning, a tender way to connect during the day, or a terrific study break.” I’m going to give this “sex” thing a try! What really convinced me was an image of a vaguely humanoid silhouette with its erogenous zones marked with red and orange dots.

“oOh,” says the vaguely humanoid silhouette:

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GRCB!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd1e44dcc-642e-402c-9bd8-8a08376fcbbd_325x351.png)

This silhouette experiences pleasure if you stimulate its left ear (but not its right), its left breast (but not its right), or its right foot (but not its left). It appears to have no genitals, unfortunately.

I came across these materials because as the Cornell alleged frat-house rape scandal has percolated, so too have takes attempting to link the event to Cornell Health. [Wesley Yang](https://x.com/wesyang/status/2107470280985919974), for example, noted that “Cornell health educators, like health educators at colleges and high schools across America, promote pornography, group sex, open relationships, bondage, and S&M as normal and healthy in chipper instructional materials bearing the school’s official endorsement.”

Yang found out about these materials from Jared Lederman, a Cornell senior who argued against Cornell’s approach to sex education and advocacy from a traditionalist-ish perspective [on his Substack](https://jaredlederman.substack.com/p/cornell-has-a-sex-education-problem). “I have spent the last several years surrounded by the carefree sexual culture I am describing, and having watched what happens when sex is severed from commitment, I have come to believe that sexual intimacy belongs within a committed relationship, accompanied by loyalty, responsibility, and exclusivity,” he wrote. “I am not suggesting Cornell need adopt this outlook on sex; I am merely advocating it not espouse the polar opposite.”

I want to separate out the question of school-produced programming from the question of what sorts of events *students* at Cornell want to put on (Lederman does dip into the latter a bit). I think the latter is a silly thing to spend much energy worrying about. Student groups tend to have a set budget per year or semester, and if they want to put on some sort of pro-kink event, these are all legal adults (albeit young ones). As long as everything is safe and legal, and as long as the university doesn’t interfere when other students from different backgrounds want to put on, like, a pro-abstinence puppet show(?), who cares? Part of being a college student is figuring out what matters to you, whether and how you want to express yourself, and all that. I’d much rather colleges err on the side of laxity rather than nannying when it comes to student activities and speech, even on private campuses where schools can legally intervene more than they can on public ones.

It’s two other questions I’m seeing get more play online. The first is whether there’s a causal relationship between Cornell Health’s sex education offerings and what happened in that frat house. As the *New York Post* put it in a headline, “ [Cornell handed students raunchy sex checklist discussing ‘three partners at a time’ — which could’ve led to frat orgies: critics](https://nypost.com/2026/10/10/us-news/cornell-hosts-sex-checklist-discussing-intercourse-with-three-partners-at-a-time/).”

That doesn’t make any sense to me.

The reason it doesn’t make any sense to me gets us to the second question: Why is Cornell Health publishing this sort of material? By which I mean: On what planet is a circa 2026 college student going to their university health center, of all places, for either basic sex education or information on whether or not they should be interested in having sex? Like, who is this *for*?

I think it’s mostly for Cornell administrators, in the sense of providing them with work to do. Colleges have more administrators than ever before — perhaps, I would venture to say, more than they *need* to have, strictly speaking. “Obviously this stuff is easy to make fun of but there’s a more serious question here of why are university bureaucracies in charge of this shit?” [said](https://x.com/Tyler_A_Harper/status/2108225624296890465?s=20) *[Atlantic](https://x.com/Tyler_A_Harper/status/2108225624296890465?s=20)* [staff writer Tyler Austin Harper on X](https://x.com/Tyler_A_Harper/status/2108225624296890465?s=20). “Gigantic chunks of the \*very expensive\* administrative enterprise is busy with make-work slop that ranges from pointless to bizarre.”

Producing sex-ed materials that no one needs is a pretty good gig for the administrators involved. It would be interesting to know exactly how much money, in terms of employee pay, it took to produce that pamphlet letting everyone know that vaginas get wet, for example. Of course, back in the day, students might genuinely rely on a student health center for sex education, and such pamphlets might be useful. But we are no longer back in the day, and there are countless online resources available to college students who require this sort of information, including via [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/sexual-health/in-depth/sexual-health-basics/hlv-20049432), [Planned Parenthood](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/sex-pleasure-and-sexual-dysfunction), and so on.

[More from Harper](https://x.com/Tyler_A_Harper/status/2108366349374197913?s=20):

> One thing that’s always striking in these debates is how obviously bizarre policies—in this case, university bureaucrats providing fetish gear explainers for students—gets justified through vague science-washing, and framed as vital medical information about “sexual health.”... This is one way weird elements of the progressive academic bureaucracy routinely get legitimated: policies that are culturally contestable and politically debatable get reframed as neutral, quasi-scientific, pseudo-medical “best practices” that promote “wellness” and “health.”... And it’s maddening because most people look at things like university bureaucrats providing fetish explainers and think “well that’s kinda weird?” But a certain sort of academic calmly explains how it’s just in keeping with “health” that administrators provide fetish explainers.

Part of this is, yes, make-work. There is a well-oiled sub-industry here that constantly must justify its own existence. (Of course I’m not talking about, like, *doctors* or *nurses* at a student health center — of course we need those. I’m talking about the staffers who produce these sorts of pamphlets about the mechanics of arousal and fetish gear and so on.)

But another part of the occasion is that the health center inculcates in college students the “correct” ways to think and talk about sex, according to current custom among the sorts of people who go to Cornell or send their kids there. Now, I think this is pretty silly too, because (again) no college student is taking their cues from their local campus health center. But the messages imparted by these materials are just as much about a very idiosyncratic set of norms than they are about sexual health or education in the traditional senses.

One really interesting example of this is the strange mishmash of boundless open-mindedness mixed with profound neuroticism. A lot of people pointed to this [“Yes, No, Maybe So: A Sexual Inventory Stocklist”](https://health.cornell.edu/sites/health/files/docs/Bulletin%20Boards/Gender-Sexuality-Pleasure_yesnomaybe%20checklist.pdf) form hosted by Cornell, which actually comes from [Scarleteen.com](http://scarleteen.com/), “Queer sex ed for all since 1998,” in 2010.

Of course there’s no science or evidence or reasoning behind this or the rest of the form’s contents. [It’s like that “white supremacy culture” stuff from Tema Okun](https://www.slowboring.com/p/tema-okun) — it’s just sort of... made up. And I’m hard-pressed to imagine a better way to stoke neuroticism about sex in a certain type of young, inexperienced person than to have them take a microscope to just about any sort of sexual activity they might engage in and have them rate, on paper, how they feel about it. Just... why?

Of course I’m lumping together different pamphlets or instruments published at different times, but what I’m describing here does faithfully represent a certain type of neurotic faux-leftist “sex positivity” that is only selectively positive about sex. In this view, porn is by default good, fetishes shouldn’t be “stigmatized,” and even practices most of us find weird or off-putting or bound to give ammunition to sociopaths need to be considered with an open mind, lest we be tarred as Sex-Negative. On the other hand... are you *sure* you’re okay with your partner touching you affectionately without explicitly asking your consent? Have you considered whether eye contact might make you uncomfortable? Did you fill out the form indicating your level of comfort with this and other practices?

I think this must melt a certain type of young brain, and I think the mixed messages inherent to this ideology can explain some of the weirder meltdowns we’ve seen over the last decade.

There really are some parallels here to the weirdest anti-racist trainings. In both cases, because the underlying trainings and materials are based on basically no evidence, there’s no particular reason to think they make things better rather than worse. In both cases, there are [sound theoretical reasons](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/01/17/opinion/dei-trainings-effective.html) to think they *do* make things worse rather than better, and in both cases, that wouldn’t be the worst thing for the administrators and bureaucrats involved. If their efforts lead to a worse climate on racial or sexual matters within a given community, or lead to greatly expanded concepts of [harm](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2020.1796080) and victimhood — well, won’t that just prove that more training is needed, and therefore more of this type of “expert”?
