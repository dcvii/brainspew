LONDON—For years Jason Arday, who in 2023 became the youngest Black professor ever appointed by the University of Cambridge, was feted in the British media for his remarkable back story and account of his achievements.

Raised in south London in a family of Ghanaian origin, Arday says he was diagnosed with autism as a child, couldn’t speak until he was 11 and overcame illiteracy in his teens before becoming a sociology professor. When at the age of 37 he was appointed as a professor at the University of Cambridge, the prestigious institution said that he would help “address the underrepresentation of people from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds.”

Today, Arday is at the center of a plagiarism scandal that has cost him his job and raised questions about Cambridge’s decision to hire him in the first place.

In recent months, a handful of academics have cast doubt on the originality of Arday’s 2015 doctoral thesis and the veracity of claims about his past feats, including that he raised 5 million pounds, equivalent to around $6.7 million, for charity and once ran 600 miles in six days, completing the challenge with a hairline fracture in his leg.

Late Wednesday, Arday resigned, saying it was the only way to move on from the accusations, speculation and public commentary. “There comes a point when the personal cost becomes too great,” he said.

When the allegations first emerged, Cambridge stood by Arday, saying he was the victim of a racially motivated smear. On Wednesday, Cambridge said it had launched a formal investigation after receiving “new information” about Arday’s academic qualifications. Hours later, Arday quit his role at the university’s Jesus College. Arday didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment.

The tale of Arday’s rise and fall has dominated British headlines for weeks and become a lightning rod for debate over whether the professor was the victim of a racially motivated witch hunt or was overpromoted by a university eager to burnish a reputation for diversity. At the time of his appointment there were only 150 Black professors in the U.K. out of a total of around 23,000 people working in such posts.

Allegations about the veracity of Arday’s back story first gained traction in July following a Substack post by Nathan Cofnas, an academic who left Cambridge in 2024 after a disciplinary probe into a blog post in which he claimed that the number of Black professors at Harvard would “approach 0%” in a meritocracy. He argued that “Blacks would disappear from almost all high-profile positions outside of sports and entertainment” under such conditions. Cambridge concluded that the post didn’t break the law or the university’s regulations upholding free speech.

Cofnas ran Arday’s 2015 doctoral thesis, which he wrote while at a university in Liverpool, through plagiarism-detection software, and found numerous passages that appeared to be exact copies of another dissertation written by a different student in 2009. Cofnas also flagged several other pieces that Arday wrote that appeared to include plagiarism. “Everyone involved—including Arday himself—understood that he was being recruited to promote the ideology of DEI,” Cofnas wrote, using an acronym for diversity, equity and inclusion.

After the allegations were picked up in the press, there was a swift pushback. Cambridge called the claims against Arday a “vile smear campaign to undermine his credibility.” A journalist at the Times Higher Education magazine who probed the claims of plagiarism was reported to the police by Arday for alleged harassment. The Metropolitan Police investigated but closed the case.

A petition in defense of Arday put together by the progressive campaigning group, the Good Law Project, was signed by 16,000 people, including the leader of the Green Party. “When a distinguished Black academic is repeatedly subjected to baseless allegations after being fully exonerated, it signals there is an attempt to undermine Black people who hold positions of influence,” it said.

In a subsequent interview with the Times of London, Arday acknowledged past mistakes and said his autism had contributed to errors in his earlier academic work and that he relied “heavily on mimicry” to understand concepts. He said he was the victim of a campaign to smear his reputation. Arday clarified to the Guardian newspaper that the £5 million fundraising for charity was done by a group of people over two decades. He also clarified that he had run 600 miles in 12 days with rest days in between.

However, further inconsistencies in Arday’s claims emerged. In previous interviews, he detailed stories of abuse, including claiming that a severed pig’s head was once delivered in a cardboard box to his parents’ home. The police said there was no record of any such incident being reported to them. Two universities where Arday said he had been a visiting professor denied he ever held those roles, while Cambridge removed a reference to one of his degrees from his staff page.

As pressure grew, Arday decided to quit. “The years since my appointment have also been marked by an unrelenting level of public scrutiny and personal attack. While criticism is an inevitable part of academic life, what I have experienced has gone far beyond scholarly disagreement,” he wrote.

His memoir, “Great and Unfortunate Things,” is due to be released by Simon & Schuster later this month. The publisher said Wednesday it is proceeding with the book’s publication as planned.
