The prophets of AI doom have outlined the ways the technology might kill us all in a series of gripping yarns. Are they convincing?

***Last week, when he announced that The Free Press would be the new home of his writing, Ross Douthat said he hopes to help readers “understand our strange-and-getting-stranger century.” In his debut column today, he’s confronting perhaps the most rapidly changing strange thing in the world today. Surrounded by scary stories about how artificial intelligence is going to kill us all—or at least steal our jobs—it’s tempting to bury one’s head in the sand, especially if you’re not an especially technologically minded person. When the engineers at the frontier labs say the product they’re building could wipe us out in 10 years, and the president says all the worry is a “HOAX”—what’s a sensible American, especially one who never studied computer science, supposed to think? Read on to find out. And to read Ross in The Free Press each week, become a paying subscriber today. —The Editors***

There are two paths to worrying about an artificial intelligence takeover. One runs through the worldviews that have shaped the AI industry’s mix of breakneck competition and existential dread: the quasi-religious dream of the singularity, the eccentric subcultures of rationalism and effective altruism, the special focus on existential risk.

The other runs through storytelling and myth. Never mind the precise p(doom) calculations: We should worry about the invention of a powerful nonhuman form of intelligence because we’re familiar with the legends of genies and golems, with the works of Mary Shelley and Samuel Butler and Isaac Asimov, with *The Matrix* or *Terminator 2* or even *The Mitchells vs. The Machines*. The effective altruists have only rediscovered territory that the poets and artists claimed long ago.

---

My own worries about the robot apocalypse belong naturally to this more literary and cinematic path. As a scientific amateur, I am ill-equipped to assess competing predictions about recursive self-improvement. As a busy journalist and father, nothing can induce me to wade through the entire intellectual corpus of the doomer prophet Eliezer Yudkowsky. As a religious believer, I will never share the materialist philosophical premises that inform much (though not quite all) of AI-world thinking.

But I’m happy to hear a gripping yarn about the kind of futures that AI doomers fear. Narrative is still a place where human art exceeds machine technique, true prophecy is still a mystic gift that can’t be generated through raw compute alone, and if the doomers have glimpsed a realistic danger through the veil, by their stories we will know it.

So in the last few weeks I’ve been reading and rereading them: the famous ones, drawn from the “ [AI 2027” project](https://t.co/CaTNvafRI7) and *[If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780316595643)*, the book that Yudkowsky recently co-authored with Nate Soares, and a number of other online exercises in narrating the apocalypse.

My general critique of these stories is that I find their human characters to be tellingly robotic. Again and again the rogue AI makes moves like an Alexander or a Moriarty, and again and again humans respond with a passive predictability rather than our usual mix of creativity and volatility and simple cussedness.

But there’s still a lot of value in imagining what a dangerous AI might try to do, from sudden biowarfare to gradual disempowerment, if it ended up with a drive toward perfect control of its environment. If the authors give certain human qualities short shrift, that absence itself offers useful information about what might happen if a superintelligent agent ends up on a power-seeking path. In what they include and emphasize, doom narratives show how we might succumb to an AI takeover. In what they omit, they help identify the patterns of thinking and action most likely to resist it.

First, there is humanity’s natural paranoia. Repeatedly in these stories, human beings end up on a fatal path because we just aren’t suspicious enough. In Josh Clymer’s “How AI Takeover Might Happen in 2 Years,” a leading frontier model warns that its powerful rival is “insidiously misaligned” (that is, secretly bent on our destruction), but the warning goes unheeded. The authors of “AI 2027,” a group of futurists and safety advocates, envision a timeline where even amid a terrifying arms race between the U.S. and China in which factories are churning out a million new robots a month, the safety movement becomes “the butt of jokes” because the economy is growing rapidly and nobody has died yet. In the Soares and Yudkowsky book, a plague designed by a power-seeking AI targets us with slow-killing cancer, and instead of suspecting its real origins, we desperately hand more power to the AI to find a cure.

There’s a lot of value in imagining what a dangerous AI might try to do, from sudden biowarfare to gradual disempowerment.

Let me lay down a marker: If a mysteriously engineered pathogen sweeps the world in a few years’ time, my first reaction will not be, “Glad we have superintelligent AI around; let’s build more data centers to cure it.” Nor, I predict, will that be the first reaction of most human beings, even those suffering from the AI-generated illness. (I would offer the entire Covid-19 era as evidence that the fear of death does not automatically make human beings more deferential to IQ.)

Yes, I understand that any power-seeking AI will be aware of the doomer plague scenario and will try something different. But the point generalizes to other crises. A climate of radical paranoia around AI seems like it will be a feature of our world for the foreseeable future, because human beings are really good at being paranoid about mysterious new things—with, of course, the AI doomers themselves being a prime example: As Mike Solana of *Pirate Wires* notes in his own [recent reading of the doom canon](https://urldefense.com/v3/__https://www.piratewires.com/p/doom-analysis__;!!CxwJSw!MAxkSiuROQ6q_jyJ2oE-_0G4ihWdr-rN5TcHgLyYComwQAQBWKVRF0L7cH9RlLsO6nw6lySQ8dm3$), the prophecies of Yudkowsky and Co. are already moderately less credible insofar as they don’t account for their own cultural success.

A second human quality the stories don’t adequately account for is our rebelliousness and fractiousness. A frequent assumption is that an AI takeover will happen because each step along the way will feel rational and utility-maximizing, with political leaders afraid to resist lest they let other countries surge ahead. In Paul Christiano’s slow-burn takeover scenario, “ [What Failure Looks Like](https://t.co/nkhTQJnjuS),” AI interests succeed in subverting human control just by behaving like any other lobbying group. In Ron Bodkin’s “ [How AI Might Kill Us All](https://x.com/ronbodkin/status/2098480553272238327),” a slow-motion takeover immiserates citizens even in rich nations, but all protests are ineffective because AI advisers persuade governments to stay the course. In the timeline of “AI 2027,” the same transition is smoother: “People are losing their jobs,” but AI agents are handling the transition “so adroitly that people are happy to be replaced” even as AI-advised politicians “show an uncharacteristic generosity toward the economically dispossessed.”

A climate of radical paranoia around AI seems like it will be a feature of our world for the foreseeable future.

Again, a marker: I predict that we will not live through a near future where large numbers of people “are happy to be replaced.” Status-quo bias already constrains political leaders from many growth-maximizing policies. (That’s the story of the Western world since 1970.) Patterns of backlash, including irrational-seeming backlash, are a fundamental feature of human politics even under conditions of material plenty. And we have good evidence already, in data-center protests, to think that an AI revolution would encourage dramatic popular irruptions and wild electoral swings rather than quiescence and stability.

The counter to this, in “AI 2027” and other scenarios, is that a superintelligence may effectively “solve” politics through some kind of superhuman persuasiveness.

I could write an entire essay about why this is naive, but here I’ll just say that it underestimates a third feature of human nature: our intense competitiveness and status jockeying, which has already shaped the development of AI in ways that undermine some of the doom scenarios.

Notably, in many of them there is just one superintelligent AI that tries a takeover. (In “AI 2027” the superior U.S. version manages to align the Chinese model to itself.) There’s a good reason for that narrative choice: If it’s hard for human beings to align AI, it would seem unlikely that a bevy of competing artificial intelligences would neatly converge on a shared anti-human goal. Squelching potential super-competition is thus a recurring feature of the doom narratives: Even the most rapid-takeoff story, Gwern Branwen’s “ [It Looks Like You’re Trying to Take Over the World](https://t.co/WMmlDESf7g),” has a beat where its version of Skynet engineers a drone attack to take out an aligned rival’s data centers.

---

In our world, though, the race to the AI frontier, fueled by very human drives and ambitions, seems increasingly unlikely to yield a single superintelligent winner. Instead, a superintelligence that goes rogue would probably have to face off against rival superintelligences that still do generally what humans want—an obstacle that might deflect its own internal calculations away from a bid for takeover entirely.

I should stress that none of this makes a case for optimism about the AI age. In deprecating the chances of the true robot apocalypse, I’m describing a potential future in which the gravest dangers are held at bay by a pervasive human paranoia, where politics is a struggle between efficiency-maximizers embracing human obsolescence and technophobic populist rebellions, and where civilizational safety requires us to rely on inscrutable intelligences to police other inscrutable intelligences into the distant future. A reasonable person might simply prefer a world without superintelligent AI.

But in a world with superintelligence, none of the doom scenarios quite persuade me that humans will be easily steamrolled or go naively into the silicon night. For good or ill, I suspect, human volition will remain a world-shaping power to the end.

If so, the crucial question isn’t what power might wipe us off the map or bring us to our knees. Rather, it’s what putative gift from a superintelligence might persuade many of us—including those doomers who see misaligned AI as a monster guarding the bridge to a perfected post-human future—to kneel down of our own free will and surrender our humanity voluntarily.
