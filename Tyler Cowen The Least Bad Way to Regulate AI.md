“If the U.S. delays unduly in approving its new models, they will be superseded by Chinese models or, eventually, models from other parts of the globe,” writes Tyler Cowen. (Illustration by The Free Press )

AI safety has become too important to be left up to Washington’s whims. A partnership among the labs could reduce risk without stifling progress.

For how much longer will the fate of American artificial intelligence be steered from Silicon Valley rather than Washington? To watch the pace of AI progress, one might imagine that the answer to that question is rapidly shortening. Each milestone in the capability of AI increases the desire among lawmakers and much of the public to rein in the technology. That includes positive breakthroughs, such as the declaration last week by payments company Stripe that AI has achieved the long-awaited “singularity”: the ability to improve itself beyond what humans can contribute. It also includes worrying episodes, like the breach of the AI database Hugging Face last month by a powerful model gone rogue.

Yet for all of the risks that AI brings, the harm caused by overregulating it would likely be much worse for the public. That is why AI developers and experts are devising proposals to promote AI safety without putting too much control in the hands of the federal government.

I have some definite recommendations, which are drawn in part from ideas [put forward earlier by Dean Ball](https://www.hyperdimensional.co/p/putting-private-governance-into-action), now head of strategic futures at OpenAI but formerly a senior White House policy adviser for the Trump administration. (I have also done work for major AI labs.)

The key is to create some basic safeguards, but without stifling broader AI progress. To do so, we must defy the conventional wisdom about public oversight and instead trust the AI labs to be their own primary regulators.

---

My version of the proposal starts with defining a private not-for-profit body for AI regulation. An ideal body would draw some features from [FINRA (the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority)](https://www.finra.org/): a consortium of financial firms that examines the trade practices of each and makes recommendations, helping the federal Securities and Exchange Commission with oversight and regulation. The AI version would include the major labs and would be authorized and overseen by Washington, perhaps through the now-fledgling [Center for AI Standards and Innovation](https://www.nist.gov/caisi).

This body would periodically audit major AI companies and their models, judging their conduct and safety. In the short run at least, much of this would be focused on issues of cybersecurity, and whether the new models created more cyber risk than they help to solve. If a company passed the audit, it would be exempted from standard liability law, at least provided that it had shown basic, reasonable care, as opposed to extreme or deliberate negligence. That would free the AI labs from the fear that courts might derail their business by granting huge awards to plaintiffs for ill-defined harms that could not reasonably have been prevented. And it would give the labs a strong incentive to meet the safety standards of this body.

It is reasonable to wonder whether such a body, composed of industry players, would issue fair and equitable judgments of safety. Maybe not. Yet there are many upsides and no better alternative.

For one thing, each company knows that a dangerous model from another company could cause a harmful incident and damage the prospects for the entire industry. Consider the Three Mile Island meltdown in 1979, which contributed significantly to the mothballing of the entire U.S. nuclear industry. Few people can name the company (Metropolitan Edison) behind the malfunctioning plant; the reputational penalty attached to the industry as a whole.

Another incentive for safety is that the top companies do not want too much competition from lower-price, lower-quality upstarts. That too will induce those companies to support fairly tough standards, perhaps excessively tough in some cases. Still, we are choosing from imperfect alternatives. The concrete truth, whether we like it or not, is that there is far more expertise within the companies for judging AI safety than we can expect to find in the federal government anytime soon.

Some risk is coming no matter what, and we can manage it better if it originates from certified American companies with enduring ties to this country.

The companies are also positioned to balance the pursuit of safety as laid out above with an urgent desire not to shut off progress altogether, or slow it down unduly. Compared with the government, they would have a much stronger incentive to ensure that good models are approved and allowed into the market.

Note also that governmental agents would oversee and participate in this body, and they would have the option of partially offsetting any undue corporate bias, whether in the direction of too much or too little safety.

Very small and very new companies would not have to participate in this system of regulation. For one thing, there are too many of those small companies to keep track of, and what counts as a full or limited “model release” may not be well-defined.

That leaves open the possibility of a dangerous incident caused by a small developer using an unreleased model. But start-ups would still be subject to standard liability law. We might over time wish to amend or rewrite those laws to take account of special features of AI, but in the meantime we have to use the laws currently on the books. Again, that seems like the best of a series of imperfect alternatives.

There are two main alternatives to the private regulation framework. One is offered by libertarians, who sometimes propose a “no regulation” scenario. Yet that wish is an illusion, even if one has broadly libertarian sympathies. If we do nothing, all AI models would be regulated by current liability law. To the extent that harms, or perceived harms, resulted from AI models, the major AI companies would be sued repeatedly, just as Meta currently is facing multiple and possibly quite expensive lawsuits related to its social media services.

Our courts do not currently have the expertise to judge complex issues of AI liability, and so this de facto regulation by the courts is unlikely to go well. The AI companies would rather have a more predictable participatory process staffed by experts.

The second alternative would be to set up a governmental regulatory body, broadly akin to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), but covering AI. A new AI model would have to receive approval from this agency before release.

But that approach could not work, at least not at the moment. The FDA, for instance, requires a yearslong, systematized process of clinical trials to approve a new drug. In contrast, right now we are living in a world where major AI model improvements come every few months, if not more frequently. We need something less risk-averse, less process-oriented, and more seat-of-the-pants.

If the U.S. delays unduly in approving its new models, they will be superseded by Chinese models or, eventually, models from other parts of the globe. That too would militate against safety. Some risk is coming no matter what, and we can manage it better if it originates from certified American companies with enduring ties to this country.

The status quo for AI regulation is also untenable. The Pentagon and national security agencies are increasingly [taking the lead](https://www.thefp.com/p/in-the-pentagon-battle-with-anthropic) and making policy on the fly that affects the whole industry, such as banning Anthropic from government contracts before the courts stepped in to allow it, or barring the release of one of the lab’s models, only to partially reverse that decision even more quickly. This informal, discretionary approach cannot go on forever; sooner or later it must be regularized.

There is no really good way to do that, but this is my best guess as to how we should inch forward.
