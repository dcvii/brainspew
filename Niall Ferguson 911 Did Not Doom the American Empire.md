I predicted its decline 25 years ago. Today, the U.S. has serious problems, but our rivals have even bigger ones.

Is America an empire? That was that question on the minds of many U.S. citizens 25 years ago, in the wake of the September 11, 2001, terrorist attacks on the United States. And it is a question being asked again today, as the war with Iran drags into its seventh month, and Donald Trump continues to fantasize about annexing Canada and Greenland—to say nothing of the application of the “Donroe Doctrine” and the “Trump Corollary” in Venezuela and (coming soon) Cuba.

In the years after 9/11, a standard left-wing interpretation was that Osama bin Laden and his suicidal hijackers had struck a blow against American imperialism in the Middle East. Surprisingly, some neoconservatives within the George W. Bush administration agreed. “We’re an empire now,” an unnamed “senior adviser” to President Bush told journalist Ron Suskind in 2004, “and when we act, we create our own reality. And while you’re studying that reality—judiciously, as you will—we’ll act again, creating other new realities, which you can study too, and that’s how things will sort out. We’re history’s actors... and you, all of you, will be left to just study what we do.”

This was not the official line, of course. “We’re not an imperial power,” said President Bush in April 2005. “We’re a liberating power.” In his [brilliant new history](https://www.thefp.com/p/simon-sebag-montefiore-middle-east) of the modern Middle East, *[The Cauldron](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780593805053)*, Simon Sebag Montefiore shows the ambivalence Americans have always felt about their imperial role in the region. At the height of the 1973–74 Arab oil embargo, Henry Kissinger threatened to seize the Saudi oil fields. He was bluffing. Nearly 30 years later, partly on Kissinger’s recommendation, Paul Bremer found himself installed as Grand Poo-Bah in charge of the Coalition Provisional Authority in Baghdad. It was May 2003. Within seven months he had to admit, in an [email to his wife](https://www.thetimes.com/world/middle-east/article/paul-bremer-iraq-emails-diplomat-2fwd70g8j): “We are already halfway out of \[here\].... We are not ready for empire!”

And yet, and yet. There’s just something about unleashing a storm of steel against the Persians, as the United States has this year, that does seem kind of empire-y.

“A republic, if you can keep it,” was Benjamin Franklin’s wry definition of what he and the other participants at the Constitutional Convention had created, after winning their war against the British Empire. Two hundred and fifty years after the United States first declared its independence, the imperial scenario seems closer than ever. Just reflect on the scene at the White House back in June, when gladiators fought in a temporary Colosseum for the amusement of President Donald J. Trump. To quote the British author Robert Harris—author of *[Agrippa](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780385552387)*, his new novel set in the time of Octavian/Augustus—“You can really see \[Trump\] in a toga with a gold laurel wreath on his head.... He is an imperial kind of president.” It’s not a new analogy. The German magazine *Der Spiegel* splashed a cartoon Trump-Caesar on its cover in January last year with the headline: “ [Der Imperator](https://www.google.com/imgres?q=Der%20Spiegel%20Der%20Imperator%20Trump&imgurl=https%3A%2F%2Fcdn.prod.www.spiegel.de%2Fimages%2F872f0ba3-1770-4b4c-8555-1dca924374e4_w335_r0.7502857142857143_fpx50_fpy23.jpg&imgrefurl=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.spiegel.de%2Fausland%2Fdonald-trump-und-seine-rueckkehr-ins-weisse-haus-der-imperator-a-54242521-3022-49aa-b4ea-97e72bc81b87&docid=Sfp6cRmiT01kZM&tbnid=ddVRai7TUjJZXM&vet=12ahUKEwjL_e2xq9KWAxWUJTQIHaeZELcQnPAOegQIOBAA..i&w=335&h=446&hcb=2&ved=2ahUKEwjL_e2xq9KWAxWUJTQIHaeZELcQnPAOegQIOBAA).”

I have long been skeptical about this kind of thing. To my eyes, Trump has always seemed a quintessentially 19th-century American figure: a composite of Andrew Jackson, William McKinley, Teddy Roosevelt, and P.T. Barnum. And going to war in the Persian Gulf is, in many ways, the most conventional thing he has done as president.

---

George W. Bush overthrew the ruler of Iraq. Trump overthrew the ruler of Iran. In both cases, the justification was that these leaders possessed, or were seeking to possess, weapons of mass destruction. Neither war was popular at home. Both wars strained relations with allies. The only difference is that Bush committed ground troops to Iraq, whereas Trump has made war on Iran in much the same way as Bill Clinton made war on Serbia in the 1990s—from the air—and for the same reasons.

Of course, in 2016, Trump claimed he had always opposed the Iraq War, which was not strictly true. “Frankly, I think the son, being loyal to the father, I think he really wanted to go into Iraq,” Trump said in February of that year, “even if it wasn’t the right thing to do.” He blamed the invasion for the persistent instability of the Middle East. “It started ISIS, it started Libya, it started Syria,” Trump declared flatly. “Everything that’s happening started with us stupidly going into the war in Iraq... and people talk about me with the button. I’m the one that doesn’t want to do this, okay?”

And yet here we are. If Trump’s actions in Iran constitute American Empire, it’s not new. Indeed, if you buy the argument that the imperial presidency was Franklin Roosevelt’s creation, it will celebrate its centenary in seven years’ time. On that basis, Trump isn’t just the 47th U.S. president; he’s the 16th American emperor.

Let’s rewind the tape 25 years. In the wake of the 9/11 attacks, which catalyzed my decision to leave Oxford for the United States, I wrote a book with the title *[Colossus](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780143034797)*. I had wanted to call it *Blind Colossus*, but my editor thought this too negative for the zeitgeist. The agreed subtitle in the first edition was *The Price of America’s Empire*, whereas I had wanted *The Rise and Fall of the American Empire*. The book was a hurriedly written sequel to *[Empire](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780465023295)*, a history of the British imperial era published the year before. It’s illuminating to revisit the argument of *Colossus* a quarter-century later.

“The events of September 11, 2001,” I wrote, “represented the culmination of well-established historical trends: the contradictions of American policy in the Middle East, the growing dependence of Western economies on oil from the Persian Gulf, and the adoption and development of terrorism as a tactic by Arabs hostile to the United States and its allies.” Since 1990, I argued, U.S. foreign policy had been “in large measure improvised in response to the failures of the UN and, in particular, to the failures of the European powers represented on the UN Security Council.” (I gave the examples of Bosnia and Kosovo.)

Unlike the left at that time—and for this, woke academia will never cease to hate me—I had no objection to American imperialism in principle. “A liberal imperial model,” I dared to say, “offers the best prospects for economic growth by guaranteeing not just economic openness but, more important, the institutional foundations for successful development.” The problem was that in practice, three deficits guaranteed that the American empire would be less successful and durable than its British predecessor: “its economic deficit, its manpower deficit, and—the most serious of the three—its attention deficit.”

1. “The United States is an unusual empire in its dependence on foreign capital to finance both private consumption and government borrowing. Yet its twin deficits are not the result of too many foreign military interventions. In fact, it is the domestic fiscal commitments of the federal government that seem likely to overstretch it in the years ahead. The true feet of clay of the American Colossus are the impending fiscal crises of the systems of Medicare and Social Security.”
2. “There are simply not enough Americans out there to make nation-building work.... When American service personnel are posted abroad they generally do not stay for very long.... Unlike the United Kingdom a century ago, the United States is an importer of people.... Moreover, when Americans do opt to reside abroad, they tend to stick to the developed world.... This, in other words, is an empire without settlers, or rather the settlers come to the metropolis rather than leave it for distant lands.”
3. “The American electorate is averse to the kind of long-term commitment that history strongly suggests is necessary to achieve a successful transition to a market economy and representative government \[in a country such as Iraq\].... The United States has acquired an empire, but Americans themselves lack the imperial cast of mind... the threat to America’s empire does not come from embryonic rival empires to the west or to the east. I regret to say that it may come from... within.”

In some ways, this analytical framework still holds. The United States’ fiscal position has only deteriorated since 2001. Its debt-to-GDP ratio has risen threefold from 32 to 99 percent; its current account deficit is up from 3.3 to 4 percent; its net international investment position is an order of magnitude larger (on net, we owe the rest of the world $22 trillion, compared with $2.3 trillion 25 years ago).

Trump isn’t just the 47th U.S. president; he’s the 16th American emperor.

As was already obvious in 2001, the 20th-century welfare state is unaffordable with 21st-century demographics. Aside from the anomalous Depression year of 1934, the United States broke “ [Ferguson’s Law](https://www.hoover.org/research/fergusons-law-debt-service-military-spending-and-fiscal-limits-power) ” (if debt interest exceeds defense spending, your empire is in trouble) for the first time in 2024, and it’s only going to get worse. I wish Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent the best of luck: They are going to need to figure out a very smart, new [Treasury-Fed Accord](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/treasury-fed-accord) to manage these wartime levels of debt without either a bond market crisis or a dollar crisis.

The left’s old argument about Gulf wars is that they’re always about oil. If the goal was to keep oil cheap, it didn’t work out. The price of a barrel of oil is four times higher than it was on the eve of 9/11. The price of gasoline is nearly three times higher. And the dollar’s purchasing power in terms of gold has sunk even further: The dollar price of an ounce of the yellow metal is up by a factor of 16.

The American attention deficit disorder has also worsened since 2001. The “War on Terror” at least started popular; the war against Iran was unpopular from the outset and has sunk even faster in public esteem. Support is down to [35.6 percent](https://www.natesilver.net/p/iran-war-polls-popularity-approval); it took until August 2007 for the Iraq War to get there. Affordability isn’t just about the price of gas, of course, but high gas prices do help explain the public opposition to the war. So does the big shift in public attitudes toward Israel in recent years.

In other ways, however, *Colossus* needs to be updated. The manpower deficit has been significantly reduced in Trump’s second term. Net migration peaked at 2.7 million in 2024, according to the [Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/blogs/random-samplings/2026/01/historic-decline-in-net-international-migration.html). Not only has the flow across the southern border all but ceased, but Americans are leaving home in numbers not seen since the Depression. A recent Brookings report estimates that there was net negative migration last year. *[The](https://www.wsj.com/us-news/americans-leaving-the-us-migration-a5795bfa)* *[Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/us-news/americans-leaving-the-us-migration-a5795bfa)* found 180,000 U.S. citizens who had “voted with their feet” by leaving the country. But Americans are not emigrating to colonize the developing world, as the British once did. A great many are going to Western Europe or Canada just to get away from Trump. A Gallup poll last year found that 40 percent of American women aged 15–44 would like to move overseas for keeps.

What hasn’t changed significantly is the scale and deployment of U.S. military personnel. Compared with 25 years ago, the American armed forces are somewhat smaller (down 9 percent). As in 2001, however, the vast majority—87 percent—are stationed at home.

There are other and more important ways my account of American empire needs updating. As Bush’s legions were deployed to the Hindu Kush and Mesopotamia, I foresaw decline and fall. In economic terms, at least, that simply has not materialized. In the past quarter-century, the U.S. economy has outperformed its G7 peers (or “near-peers,” as we now say) and stayed ahead of the BRICs—Brazil, Russia, India, and China—which Jim O’Neill of Goldman Sachs assured us back in 2001 would dominate the 21st-century world economy. To be precise, in 2001 the United States accounted for 48 percent of combined G7 GDP in current U.S. dollars. This year, according to the latest International Monetary Fund data, the share is 59 percent. China, by far the largest of the BRIC economies, surged from 13 percent of U.S. GDP in 2001 to 77 percent 20 years later. But that was Peak China. This year, it has slipped back to 64 percent.

In the same 25-year time frame, the U.S. stock market has crushed nearly all the competition. If you had invested all your savings in just one country on September 10, 2001, only long India would have been a better trade than long USA—and you would have needed *gotey* (गोटे) of steel (look it up in a Hindi dictionary) to bet the house on India back then. According to MSCI, U.S. stocks are up 7.2 times since September 2001. The equivalent multiple for Brazil is 4, Germany 3, China 2, and the UK 1.9. If you had asked me for 25-year equity market predictions in 2001, I doubt this would have been my answer.

What hasn’t changed significantly is the scale and deployment of U.S. military personnel.

And Wall Street shows no sign of fatigue as the party rocks on. This is not just about artificial intelligence, though it is now a key driver of the U.S. economy’s current vigor. Second-quarter results have seen the strongest earnings revisions this century, according to *[The Wall Street](https://www.wsj.com/business/earnings/corporate-profit-america-44836dc6?mod=hp_lead_pos1)* *[Journal](https://www.wsj.com/business/earnings/corporate-profit-america-44836dc6?mod=hp_lead_pos1)*. Across S&P 500 companies, per-share earnings soared 53 percent year over year, while sales rose nearly 16 percent.

In my pessimistic way, I keep bracing myself for some kind of AI bust. Last week, however, Frank Flight of Citadel published an [impressive note](https://www.citadelsecurities.com/news-and-insights/global-macro-strategy/elastic-expectations/) in answer to the question: “Does making intelligence cheaper result in a large expansion of demand?” His answer: “Yes.” Although the usage-weighted price of tokens has declined 40 percent since the end of June, the month of July saw a 49 percent month-over-month increase in AI spend per employee among the top 1 percent of enterprises, and 25 percent among the top 10 percent. Meanwhile, advanced semiconductor (GPU) rental markets are up since June, while the cloud revenues of the big tech companies (also known as the hyperscalers) are also up. In short, “cheaper intelligence appears to be generating enough additional usage to sustain demand for the underlying compute.” That strongly suggests that the hyperscalers’ massive investments in data centers will pay off—at least until some rogue frontier model perpetrates a cyber disaster that confirms the American public’s forebodings about AI.

What’s more, the U.S. has surged back as a hydrocarbon power, something I certainly didn’t see coming in 2001. Thanks to the shale revolution that took hold around 2008–2012, U.S. oil production is up 130 percent, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration’s Monthly Energy Review. Natural gas is up 102 percent. This helps explain why, relative to other currencies, the dollar hasn’t weakened in 25 years, and the 10-year Treasury is still the preferred “safe asset” in world financial markets. (The real effective exchange rate is pretty much where it was in 2001, as is the yield on the 10-year, between 4 and 5 percent.)

Writing in the wake of 9/11, I could dimly foresee the end of what was then known as “the unipolar moment”—the post–Cold War world with only one “hyperpower.” China, I wrote, “seems a more plausible candidate for the role of counterweight to the United States than the European Union.” That proved to be an understatement. It has been clear to me since 2018 that China is the most formidable economic and strategic rival the U.S. has ever faced. At the time of 9/11, U.S. manufacturing value-added was more than 2.5 times that of China; these days, the roles have almost reversed, with China’s manufacturing value-added now 1.6 times that of the U.S.

In *[The Grand Chessboard](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780465094356)* (1997), Zbigniew Brzezinski envisioned a worst-case scenario for the period after the Soviet collapse: “\[a\] grand coalition of China, Russia, and perhaps Iran, an ‘antihegemonic’ coalition united not by ideology but by complementary grievances.” That is exactly what we face today, with North Korea a bonus fourth member of the Axis of Authoritarians. Unlike Bush’s “Axis of Evil”—Iran, Iraq, and North Korea—this Axis is a real one, with ever higher levels of military and economic cooperation. The Axis leaders meet regularly and appear to have a game plan to challenge American primacy. By contrast, Trump has shaken up the U.S. system of alliances—not necessarily a bad thing in itself, but a strategy that makes it difficult for Washington to elicit assistance when it needs it.

---

The Trump administration’s hope this year was that Iran would prove the weakest link in the Axis. At first, in the opening phase of the war, the Iranian regime looked like it had been disarmed and defeated. But, as regime change turned out to mean an Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps dictatorship in place of a senescent theocracy, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz confronted Trump with [a stark choice](https://www.thefp.com/p/niall-ferguson-how-great-powers-lose) between Gallipoli (a much larger military effort involving ground forces) or Suez (quietly conceding control of the strait to Iran).

One reason his administration has not been forced to make that choice is that the economic consequences of the closure of the Strait of Hormuz have turned out (thus far) to be less severe than the energy experts expected. Although traffic in the strait remains depressed, overall Gulf crude exports have recovered to around 60 percent of prewar levels. Roughly 1.9 million barrels per day (MMBD) are being loaded at Fujairah in the United Arab Emirates, south of the Gulf, and somewhere in the 3 MMBD range at Yanbu on the Red Sea. But the key variable in keeping a lid on oil prices has been Chinese demand destruction. Though they have recovered slightly, Chinese crude imports are running at between 6 and 7 MMBD, compared to the prewar level of around 11 MMBD, thanks in part to massive investment in alternative petrochemical feedstock (coal-to-chemicals) and vehicle electrification.

Xi Jinping did not do this to help Donald Trump, of course. His goal has been to reduce China’s vulnerability to a blockade in case of a future confrontation with the U.S. But the short-run effect has been to reduce the pressure on Trump simply to fold in the gulf, as he might have been forced to if the energy shock had been of 1973–74 proportions. As a result, we are in a holding pattern: inconclusive air strikes and counterstrikes punctuated by equally inconclusive diplomacy.

No one would now call the United States a hyperpower, to be sure. But my prophetic vision of decline and fall 25 years ago clearly has not been fulfilled. In relative terms, America is still Number One by any measure except maybe numbers of robots—a topic for another day. The three deficits I identified in 2003 may still exist, but what matters in the empire game is relative, not absolute, power. On this basis, I would far rather have America’s problems than those of the Axis powers.

I, for one, do not dismiss Secretary Bessent’s threat to inflict “ [an economic D-Day](https://www.ft.com/content/cb865200-1a06-40be-86a3-10ee2bb05804) ” on Iran. Its currency has plummeted past 2 million to the dollar, down from 1.4 million at the start of the year. On August 28, Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei’s Telegram account issued a statement warning Iranian officials against using “disappointing speech” and false dichotomies such as “war or negotiation”—a rebuke to President Masoud Pezeshkian and Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, both of whom had warned a week earlier about Iran’s deteriorating economic situation.

True, Russia is offering Iran assistance. But the costs of Vladimir Putin’s war in Ukraine just keep on rising. Russia lost more than 42,000 soldiers in both July and August, a staggering figure, bringing its total casualties to 1.3–1.45 million. Meanwhile, Ukrainian deep strikes on Russia’s oil infrastructure are inflicting rising economic costs. This doesn’t necessarily make Putin more likely to settle. Indeed, he may be more likely to escalate, perhaps by ramping up his drone probes against European NATO members. But no one could credibly argue that Russian power has been enhanced by four and half years of bloody attrition.

The paymaster general of the Axis is, to state the obvious, China. And we are accustomed to marveling at that country’s technological prowess (cue [dancing robots](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mUmlv814aJo) and statistics about the latest Kimi AI model). But the Chinese economy also shows signs of faltering. On July 11, in a talk at Renmin University of China, an old friend of mine, economist David Daokui Li, gave a [damning assessment](https://www.yuzhehe.com/p/chinas-economy-faces-an-overall-cooling?r=24uoq2&triedRedirect=true&hide_intro_popup=true). China’s real unemployment rate, he said, was 10.2 percent, with a growing share of Chinese workers reliant on the [gig economy](https://www.ft.com/content/a3803e70-cb4d-444f-a31e-05be2f2c44f6?syn-25a6b1a6=1). From January to May, cumulative fixed-asset investment fell by 4.1 percent, a contraction previously experienced only in 1961 and 1967. China’s growth from 2001 to 2020 was propelled by local government infrastructure investment and residential construction, but both have now stalled. According to Li, total local government debt now exceeds 100 percent of GDP, forcing drastic cuts in spending (from 41 percent to 35 percent of GDP). The government lacks the will or means to resuscitate a real estate market weighed down by debt and countless “tower blocks for nobody.” No economy will be more dramatically affected by the global fertility collapse, the speed of which very few demographers foresaw 25 years ago. Last year, China’s population fell by 3.4 million, the fourth consecutive year of contraction. Births fell 17 percent to 7.9 million, the lowest since records began in 1949, against about 11.3 million deaths. The most plausible projections of China’s population in the year 2100 range from 525 million to 732 million, implying a decline of 48–63 percent.

---

In short, China’s economic miracle is fading fast. This is insufficiently appreciated in the West. We may indeed live to see an empire decline and fall. But I suspect it will not be America’s.

It is a curious feature of our culture that we are always worrying about decline, even when the underlying trends are going in the opposite direction. The one thing that stubbornly refuses to decline is decline-ism. I am reminded of the mood not 25 but 50 years ago. In his memoir, *On Watch* (1976), Admiral Elmo R. Zumwalt recalled a conversation with Henry Kissinger during Zumwalt’s time as chief of naval operations:

> K. feels that U.S. has passed its historic high point like so many earlier civilizations. He believes U.S. is on downhill and cannot be roused by political challenge. He states that his job is to persuade the Russians to give us the best deal we can get, recognizing that the historical forces favor them. He says that he realizes that in the light of history he will be recognized as one of those who negotiated terms favorable to the Soviets, but that the American people have only themselves to blame because they lack stamina to stay the course against the Russians who are “Sparta to our Athens.”

Within a decade, the great unraveling of Soviet power had begun, symbolized by the Chernobyl disaster.

What will be the Chinese equivalent, I wonder? Or was it Covid?
