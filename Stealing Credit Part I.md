*Credit theft can be damaging to individual STEM investigators. However, it can have much further reaching consequences in the case of intellectual property theft.*

*This is explored in detail in the following. Parts II and III describe a series of anecdotes where credit attribution became an issue. Some methods for reducing the incidence of these problems is suggested in the final segment, Part IV, along with some related topics.*

### Misattribution and Its Damage to Innovation

Attribution can make or break a STEM career.\[1\]

In addition, this issue is associated with the allocation of scarce human talent. However, potentially massive amounts of other resources can also be involved.

Failing to uphold reasonable standards in acknowledging intellectual property contributions leads to waste of human potential and other assets.

In the future, theft of intellectual property might be of much greater import and have more impact than any other kind of theft.\[2\]

### Honesty in STEM

STEM relies on fairly strict codes of conduct. Investigators must be scrupulously honest in their struggles against the challenges that nature presents.\[4\]

Also, pondering and solving mathematical conundrums requires impeccable integrity. One small slip, and everything is lost.

In addition, it is important to preserve the comity of STEM culture. To do this, it is critical that each individual in STEM must maintain the highest degree of probity in dealing with their STEM colleagues.

For example, if someone in STEM engages in fraud or other forms of mendacity or duplicity, the entire STEM effort suffers. The repercussions can reverberate for decades.

A renowned illustration of this principle involves the famous and influential American physiologist Ancel Keys (1904-2004).\[5\] Keys appears to have lied about the relationship between fats, cholesterol and cardiovascular issues. Critics argue that Keys selectively and dishonestly edited his data to produce nice, clean linear plots.

This chicanery was not discovered until decades after Keys’ death. By then, billions if not trillions of dollars had been wasted. In addition, many had sickened and died because of this fraud. This reflected poorly not only on Keys, but the entire biomedical establishment. By extension, the reputation of STEM itself suffered.\[6\]

This was not a good thing. We are still dealing with the fallout now, decades later.

Keys’ form of misrepresentation of Research and Development (R&D) results is still with us. Currently, it appears in seemingly ever-increasing quantities. Many are trying to grapple with this problematic blight on STEM.

However, there are several other forms of dishonesty in STEM that are also troubling. This essay is about one of those. That is, *credit misassignment*.

A relevant quote from an eminent Harvard faculty member on this topic is:

> Bestow credit where it is deserved, and expect the same from others. Honest credit carefully given matters enormously.
> 
> \-- E.O. Wilson, from “Letters to a Young Scientist”

### Contribution Acknowledgement

One of the more concerning forms of deceit that is common in STEM is the so-called “theft of credit” or “misattribution of results.”\[7\] This might seem fairly innocuous at first glance. However, the STEM professional’s position relies on the proper ascription of their intellectual property contributions.\[8\]

In other words, who discovered this thing first? Who had this idea? Who invented this device? Who offered useful advice along the way? Who was the first person to solve this problem? When did it happen? Where did this method originate?

The answers to these sorts of questions are very important to the occupation of someone in STEM. They are also very interesting as we try to understand the creative process. Knowing where innovation comes from is valuable information.

Confusing the issue is the fact that several times in history, different individuals have surfaced that claim “priority” in some discovery or the development of some technique. For example, both Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) and Sir Isaac Newton (1643-1727) appear to have created calculus at more or less the same time, independently. Charles Darwin (1809-1882) and Alfred Russel Wallace (1823-1913) each seem to have come up with the concept of evolution by natural selection on their own.

There are lots of other prominent examples, like the ‘reinvention’ of the ‘Gauss telegraph’ by Samuel Morse (1791-1872) a few years later.\[9\] Another salient instance that comes to mind is the so-called “Cooley-Tukey” algorithm, which has apparently been rediscovered many times over. Perhaps the first person to have recognized and used this technique was the German mathematician and physicist Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855).

This sort of thing is so common and so annoying to STEM professionals that the German polymath, geographer, naturalist, and explorer Alexander von Humboldt (1769-1859) referred to it when he outlined the ‘three stages of scientific discovery’. First, people deny it is true; second, they deny it is important; and third, they credit the wrong person.\[10\]

There are a number of “folk theorems” along these lines. For example, Russian mathematician Vladimir Arnold (1937-2010) has outlined the so-called “Arnold Principle”. That is, “Discoveries are rarely attributed to the correct person.”

There is also an Arnold Corollary to this statement, “If a discovery is named after someone, it is highly likely that they did not discover it.” This is very similar to the “Stigler Law of Eponymy” in sociology; “No scientific discovery is named after its original thinker.”\[11\]

### Historical Examples

One of the more obvious situations of credit misattribution is that of women whose contributions were overlooked in favor of their male colleagues and counterparts. This is a well-known phenomenon known as the “Matilda Effect.”

For example, Rosalind Franklin worked incredibly hard on determining the structure of DNA. She shared her data with her boss Maurice Wilkins and two competing scientists, Francis Crick and James Watson. Franklin died prematurely of cancer, but was not really recognized for her massive efforts until much later. Crick, Watson and Wilkins all shared a Nobel Prize in 1962 for the discovery of the double helix structure after Franklin died in 1956.

Jocelyn Bell Burnell discovered the first evidence of pulsars in 1967 as a graduate student. Her male supervisor Antony Hewish was awarded the Nobel Prize for her work in 1974, in a move which was fairly controversial at the time.

Lise Meitner provided the mathematical and physical evidence for nuclear fission. However, Meitner was Jewish and had to flee Berlin for Sweden in 1938. Her coworker Otto Hahn published her work and left her name off the paper out of fear that he would be attacked for working with a Jewish woman. As a result, Hahn won the Nobel Prize for this accomplishment in 1944 and Meitner was ignored, along with her nephew Otto Robert Frisch. Frisch and Meitner apparently actually coined the term “nuclear fission.”

Another similar prominent example involves the so-called “Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle” associated with carbon fixation. Although Melvin Calvin won the 1961 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this discovery, his collaborators at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory Andrew Benson and James Bassham were neglected and unmentioned. Benson and Bashom were co-discoverers of the intermediate compounds and used carbon-14 tracing to illuminate the carbon path.

Zoologist Dr. Charles Henry Turner (1867-1923) was blocked from pursuing an academic career because of his skin color. He was relegated to teaching high school instead. Nevertheless, Turner published over 70 ground-breaking papers on insect cognition that were ignored until white scientists later replicated and confirmed his results.

Sir Jagadish Chandra Bose actually discovered wireless signaling in Calcutta in 1895, about 6 years prior to Guglielmo Marconi’s commercial work. They both used a “mercury coherer” as a radio wave detector. Marconi obtained a patent and was later awarded a Nobel Prize for his work. Bose at the time was opposed to patents as a matter of principle, and did not seek out a patent as a result. However, Bose was eventually far more successful and widely recognized for his numerous contributions to solid state electronics, radio and other technical fields.

A recent and more tragic event associated with improper assignment of credit is the apparent suicide of Cambridge sociology professor Jason Arday in August of 2026. By effectively lying about the credentials and accomplishments of this individual, assorted well-meaning virtue signalers in academia set this individual up for failure. This entire episode casts aspersions on the institutions involved. Once again, this sort of activity is not always without negative consequences.

### Summary

As banal, innoxious and commonplace as misattribution of credit might appear, it is probably a serious matter that is worthy of some consideration. As technology evolves, these issues might become more prevalent in the future.

It is necessary for human fulfillment for people to feel they have a stake in society. Recognition of human worth is a noble goal. Credit allocation can form an important component of this.
