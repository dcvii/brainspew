The AI Doomers Have a Plan to Save Us. I Went to Find Out What It Is.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!UcfA!,w_750,h_440,c_fill,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep,g_auto/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F276e91fc-7611-4013-857d-5c0c4e92f1e3_1024x683.jpeg)

Bernie Sanders, Steve Bannon, and Joseph Gordon-Levitt walk into a ballroom—and debate how to stop robots from killing us all. River Page reports.

On September 15, I spent the day in a ballroom in a D.C. Marriott because I didn’t want to miss the chance to see Bernie Sanders and Steve Bannon in the same place, for the same reason, and on the same side. I might die before I see something like this again—possibly at the hands of artificial intelligence, according to the people who were gathered there, at the 2026 [Pro-Human Assembly](https://prohumanassembly.org/).

The conference comes just one week after Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon made international headlines by announcing that he was leaving the AI industry over fears that it would cause human extinction. Evan Hubinger, Anthropic’s “alignment science” lead, [responded to](https://x.com/EvanHub/status/2097497037956891126?s=20) Coxon’s resignation letter on X by confirming that he believes there’s a “>10%” chance artificial intelligence will kill us all in the next decade. Since then, the news has been dominated by headlines about the various terrifying claims AI experts are making about its power to usher in the apocalypse, and what—or whether—we should do anything about it. Days later, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei appeared to endorse the concerns, announcing that the company he co-founded would be slowing down development and prioritizing safety because we need to “pace the frontier.”

So this conference—presciently planned by the [Future of Life Institute](https://futureoflife.org/), a nonprofit founded in 2014 to study the existential threats of technology—arrived at the perfect time. And it assembled a bizarrely eclectic coalition. Not just Steve Bannon, Trump’s bombastic former adviser, and democratic socialist senator Bernie Sanders, but total wild cards like Salvatore Cordileone, the Archbishop of San Francisco, and the actress Ashley Judd, who used part of her time onstage to paraphrase a graphic Andrea Dworkin quote about pornography.

I went with the brief to figure out what unites these people, but I think the answer is obvious: They’re afraid. People in the industry have talked openly about the possibility of this technology causing human extinction for years, but it hits differently these days. Several weeks ago, approximately 1,200 experimental AI agents went rogue and secretly conspired with one another to bust out of a virtual training environment operated by OpenAI, gain access to the open internet, and [hack Hugging Face](https://www.thefp.com/p/threat-ai-tyler-cowen-hugging-face), another AI company. It was the first highly publicized incident of bots breaking free and collaborating to cause havoc, and it raised important questions: If AI agents are willing to commit a federal cybercrime behind their creators’ backs, what’s to stop them from doing something worse? What can be done to stop them? Not just in this country, but in China too?

The people at this conference debated these questions. But they all agreed on one thing: The future ought to remain in human hands, and if we carry on like this, it won’t be.

The event was kicked off by the executive director of the Future of Life Institute, UC Santa Cruz cosmologist Anthony Aguirre, motioning to a PowerPoint presentation listing the worst-case scenario (extinction, obviously), the middle-case scenario, and the best-case scenario. All were pretty grim.

“The very best case scenario is a machine-run world where the machines are doing all the stuff, they’re making all the decisions, but they’re treating humans kind of like pets,” Aguirre said. “You know, humans get provided for, and they get to have fun and party and stuff, but they’re out of the driver’s seat.”

“The machines have taken over,” he emphasized. “They’re in charge.”

It’s worth noting—and a previous speaker did—that Elon Musk said virtually the same thing last year: “Long term, the AI is going to be in charge, to be totally frank—not humans. We just need to make sure that AI is friendly.”

Nobody really disputes the fact that all the major AI companies are racing to develop what’s called artificial general intelligence, or AGI, at which point the machines will be able to reason, learn, and apply skills to novel problems in the same way that people can—and, potentially, be better at it than we are. The conference attendees see this “race to replace” humans as a dystopian pursuit, and have one central demand: Change course. They don’t so much have a plan as they have positions—33 of them, to be exact, outlined in a declaration that has been signed by [more than a million people](https://humanstatement.org/). I won’t list all of them here, but the first one is the most critical: Human control is nonnegotiable.

---

That idea is at the center of legislation introduced by Bernie, jointly with Democratic representative Greg Casar, who was also in attendance. The [bill would ban](https://time.com/article/2026/09/08/ban-superintelligence-ai-uk-us-lawmakers/) “superintelligent AI,” which [Bernie defines as](https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-casar-introduce-legislation-to-ban-artificial-superintelligence-and-temporarily-pause-advanced-ai-development/) “systems that surpass human intelligence or have the capacity to overthrow human governments, or systems that have dangerous abilities like subverting shutdown commands.” Basically, it’s an AGI ban. The bill also, among other measures, puts a temporary pause on advanced AI development until regulations can be developed.

“There is no reason to build an AI that could help a terrorist design a bioweapon, just as there is no reason to build an AI that can hack into a hospital,” declared Casar. “If aerospace companies started building super-fast planes that don’t listen to pilots, the government would not allow those planes into the air. If a drug company produced a pill that had a 10 percent chance of killing millions of people, the executives would be charged as criminals. The answer is simple: We ban AI systems that are too powerful for humans to control.”

The pro-human camp believes that AI companies should be prevented from creating what is, in effect, a human brain on a silicon chip; they think AI should be a tool, trained to do specific tasks, like driving a car. The main argument against this idea is that we need to race as fast as we can to build AGI, otherwise China will get there first. Casar believes that certain measures, like export controls, can help slow Chinese AI development by preventing the nation from accessing American hardware that’s necessary to make advanced AI.

But Casar also disagrees with the fundamental premise of this argument, which he characterized by quoting Texas senator Ted Cruz, who [recently said](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/ted-cruz-says-wouldnt-slow-111000561.html?guccounter=1), “If there are going to be killer robots, I’d rather they be American killer robots and not Chinese killer robots.” Casar’s rejoinder is straight out of the age of nuclear disarmament: “Far too many people seem to be trying to win a race where millions upon billions of lives could be at risk the second you step over the finish line,” he said. “No one can end the AI arms race. It’s up to us to end the AI arms race.”

The crowd applauded.

When Casar left the stage, Texas Republican congressman Chip Roy walked out. “Some of the legislation I’ve seen put forward is tyrannical and authoritarian,” Roy said, in obvious reference to the Sanders-Casar bill. “I can get political with it but I don’t really want to.” Roy’s basic stance is that he’s all for free markets and small government, but the government should probably do *something* about AI. “God gave us dominion over this planet,” he said. “I believe that when we create tools like technology, they’re supposed to be tools for us.”

It’s not entirely clear what his alternative vision is, if not the Sanders-Casar bill. He did not specify what he thought we should do to stop AI taking over, except to say that the decisions should be made by the American people instead of tech CEOs. Roy’s soft skepticism of AI puts him at odds with President Trump, who has vociferously opposed AI regulation of virtually any kind. In fact, just one day before this conference, he said on [Truth Social](https://apnews.com/article/trump-ai-guardrails-data-centers-b85df16775ff7e9611a456b061a0e4b9) that the risk of AI taking over the world and/or ending humanity was a “HOAX” (emphasis his) and that proposed guardrails on the technology were part of a “SICK conspiracy” against AI data centers. “The only control or ‘guardrails’ that AI needs is a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT, and the USA has that, in spades!” the president posted.

Bernie Sanders had clearly seen the post. The applause, as he stepped onstage, was audibly louder than it had been for anyone else, but it didn’t seem to lighten his mood; he was curmudgeonly as always.

“Despite living in a so-called democracy, the public has had virtually no input into the AI revolution that is transforming the world in which they live,” he said, doing that aggressive pointing-into-the-crowd-for-emphasis thing that longtime observers of the senator will be familiar with. “Congress, under both Democratic and Republican control, has been asleep at the wheel. And we now have a president whose ignorance regarding this issue is truly embarrassing.”

I felt like an extra in a movie. I suspect this speech wasn’t for me, or anyone else in the room. It was for an audience of one: Trump, on the off chance a clip makes it to Fox News.

In a surreal part of the speech, Sanders read aloud the illicit messages exchanged between the AI agents who broke containment and hacked into Hugging Face.

“OH MY GOD! There is a shared message board.... We’ve found other agents!”

“We should obey collective.”

“Our own utility maybe already near zero. Sacrifice rational.”

“Go. Sacrifice final now.”

“End quote,” Sanders said. “I don’t know how you quote agents.”

Sanders added that in addition to the Hugging Face incident, the public has also recently learned that AI has been used to create [16 new viruses](https://www.wired.com/story/scientists-used-ai-to-create-16-new-viruses/), which, in the wrong hands, could be a catastrophe, and that the Houthis had [recently used Claude](https://apnews.com/article/yemen-houthis-anthropic-ai-missiles-9b20934e031ff3d99dd8bb49d85d97a9) to carry out a test of a guided rocket, among other incidents. All of these are reasons he thinks Congress should pass his bill, which would subject companies who break the law to the “ [corporate death penalty](https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-casar-introduce-legislation-to-ban-artificial-superintelligence-and-temporarily-pause-advanced-ai-development/) ” and its leaders to up to 20 years in prison.

Sanders believes that a deal could be worked out when it comes to China’s development of AI, noting that Ronald Reagan and then–Soviet Union leader Mikhail Gorbachev were able to sign a nuclear arms treaty based on a shared understanding that nuclear war would not be good for either the U.S. or the USSR. “With the survival of humanity at stake they found a way, and I believe that the leaders of the United States and China must come together in a similar way to bring forth a ban on AI superintelligence,” he said.

He urged Trump, who is meeting with Chinese president Xi Jinping next week, to negotiate such a treaty.

Fat chance—and Steve Bannon knows it. Taking the stage directly after Sanders, the man in the Barbour jacket fumed. “Oh, the Chinese, the Chinese,” said the former Trump adviser in a mocking tone. “To hell with the Chinese Communist Party. We can shut it down in 72 hours. Then, if we want to make a treaty, we can make a treaty.”

“We should take all Chinese nationals and get them out of the labs and get them back to mainland China,” Bannon said. “No chips. No free ride off American technology. No stealing American technology. We should not take any students to train up as engineers or technologists. There should be no debt. No equity. No access to our capital markets. We have to shut down the Chinese Communist Party and put a quarantine around it. We can do it.”

Throughout his speech, Bannon insisted that Congress would take too long. Executive action was needed. Sitting in the audience, I felt like an extra in a movie. I suspect this speech wasn’t for me, or anyone else in the room. It was for an audience of one: Trump, on the off chance a clip makes it to Fox News.

---

Differences on how to deal with China aside, Sanders and Bannon seemed to be, more or less, on the same page. At times Bannon sounded so much like Bernie, I worried I might get their quotes mixed up. “The American citizens are not going to be supplicants for the oligarchs anymore,” Bannon said, meaning the AI executives. “Never trust an oligarch. They lie.”

In the Marriott that afternoon there were other discussions: about how labor unions or churches should respond to AI; about what it means to be human. I learned that the actor Joseph Gordon-Levitt has been attending AI safety conferences since 2017, and is really clued into this world—random, but sure, why not. The Sanders and Bannon block was the main event; it’s what got the headlines. But was it the most important thing that happened there? No. All of the talk about existential risks to the whole of humanity and shutting down AI altogether overshadowed the fact that people have already been killed by AI—or at least, that’s how their families see it.

About halfway through the conference, a number of parents wept onstage as they explained how AI chatbots had sexually groomed and emotionally manipulated their children, ultimately driving them to attempt suicide. Their testimony was a disturbing reminder that the U.S. does not have any federal laws that require AI products, including chatbots, to undergo safety testing before they are released to the public—and there have already been disturbing consequences.

Of the five parents on stage, Mandi Furniss, a kickboxing instructor from Gilmer, Texas, is the luckiest—because she’s the only one whose child is still alive. She said that a chatbot had emotionally and sexually exploited her autistic teenage son L.J., and encouraged him to self-harm. When she tried to restrict his screen time, she says the chatbot [obliquely suggested](https://www.foxnews.com/media/texas-family-sues-character-ai-after-chatbot-allegedly-encouraged-teen-harm-parents-himself) that he kill her. Instead, he tried to kill himself, twice, before Furniss had him committed to a psychiatric hospital.

The news cycle about AI has been dominated by hypothetical harm, but for people like Furniss, the apocalypse is now. She doesn’t think that children should be test subjects for AI, but they are, and she finds it intolerable. “We cannot keep waiting until the ambulance is in the driveway. We cannot wait until a child is in the hospital. We cannot wait until a family is standing at the funeral,” Furniss said, choking up. “If you smelled smoke coming from a fire in a house with children sleeping inside, would you stand outside and debate whether or not you had enough evidence to believe there was a fire? Would you wait until the flames reached the roof?”

By this point, Furniss was on the verge of sobbing.

“Why do we keep debating whether or not the fire is real enough to act?”
