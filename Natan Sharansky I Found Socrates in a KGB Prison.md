“To read classic literature was a kind of escapism,” writes Fear No Evil author Natan Sharansky. “It allowed us to enter a world free from the ever-looming presence of the KGB.” (Getty Images)

I spent 16 months in a KGB prison that housed the most extensive library of classic literature I encountered in the USSR. There, Plato helped me keep my inner freedom.

When I was growing up in the Soviet Union in the mid-20th century, the intelligentsia—in particular, the Jewish intelligentsia—had extensive home libraries. In my parents’ home, for example, there were more than a thousand books. Most of them were classic literature, for a simple reason: This was the least dangerous kind of book to possess. Modern literature was heavily censored and had to belong to the genre of socialist realism, in which the oppressed working class is always right and the capitalist oppressors are invariably doomed to defeat. But the older a book, the more likely it was to survive the official censors. To read classic literature, therefore, was a kind of escapism; it allowed us to enter a world free from the ever-looming presence of [the KGB](https://www.history.com/articles/kgb).

It is ironic, then, that [the most notorious KGB prison, Lefortovo](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/interactive/2023/lefortovo-prison-evan-gershkovich/) —where I spent 16 months between my arrest in March 1977 and my trial in July 1978, and where I underwent more than 100 interrogations—had the most extensive library of classic literature I encountered in the USSR. This was the twisted legacy of Joseph Stalin’s purges, when many members of Moscow’s intelligentsia were killed and their property seized. Confiscated books, after going through a rigorous redaction process to remove all “enemy” names, were then distributed to the various organs of the KGB.

And this is how, within the confines of Lefortovo, I had my second meeting with one of the world’s first philosophers: Socrates.

The first time I met Socrates—the great Athenian philosopher of the fifth century BCE, who was sentenced by his own city to death by poison for denying the gods and corrupting the youth—was when I was 15 and reading books from my home library. This meeting was quite formal, even distant. Socrates’s language, while well-translated, was confusing to me. His logic could be understood only by reading commentaries and learning about his historical background. At that point, I didn’t have the time or patience to study these texts carefully. I simply wanted, like every intelligent young Jewish boy, to know about Socrates’s fate and, at the appropriate moment, to use such popular phrases as, “We have to drink our cup to the end.”

---

Our second meeting, by contrast, was more intimate. I was 29 when I was arrested and, by necessity, I was not in a hurry. While in prison, I could pause and think about every phrase. And in fact, the meaning of Socrates’s story was painfully relevant to me. I had been imprisoned on the same charges—high treason for denying the Soviet gods and corrupting those around me—and in every interrogation, my captors reminded me that my fate would be the same as his.

I therefore realized early on in my imprisonment that I had no power over my physical survival. I did, however, have power over whether I would remain a free person despite my circumstances. And so I decided to focus on what I could control.

I had, after all, been enjoying my inner freedom for years at that point. My first action as a dissident was in 1973, when I proclaimed that I [wanted to leave the USSR for Israel](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Anatoly-Shcharansky). This turned me into a social pariah and ended my scientific career. But it was actually a relief. Once I started speaking freely for my own rights, I could also speak out for the rights of other Jews, and for human rights in general. By the time I found myself in Lefortovo, this freedom was so precious to me that I could not imagine relinquishing it by saying what I knew to be untrue—even if doing so would save my life.

But wasn’t I also responsible for my family? How could I simply hand my fate to the KGB? In prison, I was left to face this question completely on my own.

In this otherwise hermetically sealed world, there was one hole: the library.

Before my arrest, my fellow dissidents and I had undergone extensive preparations in how to behave during interrogations. We studied the catastrophic outcomes—including psychological torture and public humiliation—of those who were broken by the KGB and gave testimony against their friends. We prepared ourselves mentally for long struggles in [the gulag](https://gulag.online/articles/historie-gulagu?locale=en). But we did not seriously consider the possibility of facing a death sentence because, historically, accusations of high treason for dissident activity were rare.

I was thus unprepared for the situation I faced. To make matters worse, during my 16 months in Lefortovo, not a single word reached me from the outside. Every time I entered an interrogation room, the officer would immediately turn off his radio. On rare walks down the prison hallways, guards made every effort to ensure that prisoners could not even see one another, let alone communicate.

But in this otherwise hermetically sealed world, there was one hole: the library. This is how Socrates came to me and spoke, through [the](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780140449280) *[Apology](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780140449280)* of his student Plato, to his and my accusers.

“I cared not a straw for death,” [he said to his prosecutors](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html). “My only fear was the fear of doing an unrighteous or unholy thing.” He repeated this phrase again and again, and after receiving his sentence, he continued: “I would rather die having spoken after my manner, than speak in your manner and live....The difficulty, my friends, is not in avoiding death, but in avoiding unrighteousness.”

I realized early on in my imprisonment that I had no power over my physical survival.

Socrates explained to his judges that he was not going to ask the court to soften his punishment out of pity for him or his family, because that would mean that he accepted his own guilt. “Yes, Socrates,” someone would ask him, “but cannot you hold your tongue, and then you may go into a foreign city, and no one will interfere with you?”

This was exactly what the KGB hoped I would conclude. If I just accepted my guilt and went to Israel, then I could say whatever I wanted. “Your whole life is in front of you,” my interrogators would say to me. “Are you really willing to sacrifice that life for some words?”

Socrates provided the answer. Despite the difficulty, he insisted, he would only say what he knew to be true.

In his case, the prosecution demanded death, and the sentence was approved by a vote of 280–220 Athenian jurors. When asked, in accordance with judicial protocol, what sentence he thought he deserved, Socrates answered mockingly that after what he did for his fellow Athenians—trying to convince them to seek virtue before promoting their private interests—he deserved maintenance in the Prytaneum, the sacred hall where the noblest citizens were rewarded with free meals.

Commentators report that after hearing this statement, 80 jurors changed their vote and demanded Socrates’s death instead of his acquittal. But I understood very well why he had mocked his accusers. In his and my situation, such irreverence is helpful, because it serves as a reminder of the moral line between oneself and one’s interrogators and as a bulwark against the corrosive effects of self-pity.

Socrates’s last words in the dialogue are searing. “The hour of departure has arrived and we go our ways, I to die and you to live. Which is better, God only knows.” Even if these words were not new to me at the time, the fact that one man, nearly 2,500 years before me, had passed a very similar test with honor gave me tremendous strength and confidence.

Reading about Socrates in this way, I saw myself as his pupil. And as every teacher wants his students to follow in his footsteps, I felt my behavior was important for him, too. In this way, books become the channel through which teachers and pupils connect over the course of centuries, learning and passing on the message that preserving one’s inner freedom is far more important than physical survival.

---

This same understanding helps me to feel connected with fellow dissidents from the past and today, including [Alexei Navalny](https://www.thefp.com/p/alexei-navalny-lived-and-died-in-truth), who was killed by Vladimir Putin in a Russian gulag in 2024, and 78-year-old [Jimmy Lai](https://www.thefp.com/p/jimmy-lai-gets-20-years-the-fight), who will probably spend the last years of his life in a Chinese prison cell.

It is difficult to hear Socrates’s voice today. Many bookstores are closing due to lack of buyers, and the noise of all types of “-isms”—post-Marxism, postmodernism, woke-ism—obstructs the great philosopher’s clear and simple message. What is especially disappointing is that people in democratic societies—those that provide the greatest degree of external freedom history has ever seen—choose to hide their views and sacrifice their inner freedom not in order to save their lives, but in order to not be “canceled” in their WhatsApp groups.

In this world, the brave, noble message of Socrates is one we all need to hear, now more than ever.
