The centre of gravity in the Arday Affair has shifted. Public discussion no longer focuses on evidence that Cambridge University’s youngest black holder of a professorial chair plagiarised considerable portions of his PhD, embellished his c.v. with three academic appointments at institutions that now deny it, and claimed to be the author of a book that was never written. The focus has moved on to the question of why he was appointed in the first place, and what that tells us about the state of academe.

This is good. For Jason Arday himself I feel somewhat sorry. Yes, he’s been serially dishonest. And yet, from what I’ve observed, he really believes his own lies. In brushing aside critics as “racially motivated”, he sounds as if he sincerely means what he says. He’s trapped in a world of fantasy. Whatever his personal guilt, there’s a tragic quality to his predicament. For, when reality does finally break through, Nemesis will enter, too. A terrible reckoning will cause the self he’s constructed to disintegrate. I fear for his life.

And that makes me angry—but not with his critics. It makes me angry at all the adults who failed to call him out. Who indulged him. Who lowered the threshold so he could clamber over it. Who encouraged him to dismiss criticism as expressions of ‘eurocentric’, colonialist, racist bad faith. Who aided and abetted the elaboration of his fantasy, year after year.

Latest among them are the eight professors and the vice-chancellor’s deputy on the committee that unanimously recommended his Cambridge appointment. Evidently, they failed to scrutinise him. But why? One leading candidate for the answer is the identity of the applicant: an autistic man of African heritage, possessing double membership of historically marginalised and victimised groups.

I find this explanation entirely plausible. I’ve witnessed at first-hand how some professors, even at our most prestigious universities, use their victim-identity as a get-out-of-gaol card. How they deploy it to trump intellectual standards and by-pass accountability before the bar of reason. And how their anti-rational behaviour prevented neither their appointment nor their promotion.

Several years ago, I was invited to take part in a panel discussion about empire in Oxford. One of my fellow panellists was a female professor of international relations. At one point in our discussion, I turned to her and said, “A few moments ago, you said X. Now you’ve just said Y. They’re not consistent”. Waving her hand imperiously, she swept my complaint off the table, exclaiming, “Enough of your donnish, tutorial tactics!”

I was struck dumb, feeling—and I use the word advisedly—unmanned. But, later, I reflected, “Are there no rules to this game any longer? And if not, what on earth is the point?” Clearly, our discussion had not been a searching out of the truth together by common submission to the disciplines of logic and evidence. It was merely the occasion for the declamation of a preferred narrative and silencing criticism by the insinuation of bad faith. Hence, ‘tactics’.

But there was more. For, my tactics had also been described as ‘donnish’, which connotes fusty white males in tweed jackets, reeking of pipe smoke circa 1950. My fellow panellist was also exploiting her political power as a member of a group commonly stereotyped as victims (women) over a member of a group commonly stereotyped as victimisers (men). I was aware that, in response to the dismissal of my criticism, my tongue was tied, partly because the one doing the dismissing was a woman and I was a man. And her choice of words took care to remind me of that. Rather than a forum for finding out the truth, our discussion had been an arena for the exercise of power.

This was no isolated incident. In my recent book, *The New Dark Age: Why Liberals must Win the Culture Wars*, I report one of many similar cases on the far side of the Atlantic. After serving as a professor of political science at Alberta’s Mount Royal University for fourteen years, Frances Widdowson was fired in 2021. Why? Because she challenged the university’s policy of requiring its members to ‘respect’ and ‘value’ indigenous ways of knowing. When she disagreed that traditional native American ‘star knowledge’ should be viewed in the same way as astronomical science, indigenous studies professors accused her of racist harassment, making the campus ‘unsafe’ for them. Widdowson sees her particular experience as an instance of a wider phenomenon in universities, where differential treatment on the basis of one’s identity “is becoming the norm”.

The silver lining of the Arday Affair’s dark cloud is that it has lifted the lid on this problem in academe, just as the murder of Henry Nowak exposed it among the police. Now it’s up to us to get the measure of it. A local investigation by the University of Cambridge isn’t enough. We need a national inquiry into the corrupting effect of identity politics on our universities.
