Like most Americans, I was raised to believe that education was the path to a better life. And for me, it certainly was. However, it turned out that the elements I believed were most important about education (cultivated knowledge, skills and theories) — the things I viewed as *the point* of education — these mattered far less than the name at the top of my degree.

Before I attended Columbia University, I had an AA, two BAs and a MA. I’d published articles in academic journals. I’d done a lot of public engagement. However, when I would pitch articles to outlets like the New York Times and The Atlantic, I wouldn’t even get a rejection. I was below wasting time on a “no thanks.” However, after I arrived to Columbia, editors for the New York Times and other outlets began *reaching out to me,* cold, asking if I’d be interested in writing for them. I didn’t suddenly become a better writer in the time it took to relocate from Sierra Vista, AZ to the Upper West Side of Manhattan. The plane trip didn’t magically imbue me with important insights that I hadn’t been trying to elevate before. Instead, my rise to [media prominence](https://musaalgharbi.com/musa-al-gharbi/public-impact/) was almost purely a function of network effects and institution effects.

A similar reality holds for my academic success. I have a tenure-line job at an R1 research university. I can thank Columbia for that. Nationwide, 80 percent of tenure-line faculty [come from](https://musaalgharbi.com/2023/01/24/college-professors-representation-disparities/) just 20 percent of schools, and if you look at *elite school* faculty, it’s an even higher percentage of faculty from an even narrower set of programs. It’s an open secret that if you’re graduating from a non-elite PhD program and you haven’t published in a top disciplinary journal, almost no one will even read your file (and it’s much harder to publish in a top disciplinary journals if you aren’t part of the “right” PhD program). Across the board, the measures used to represent “merit” in academia [largely reflect and reproduce](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/being-a-professor-is-a-product-and) antecedent advantages.

I now have a blockbuster book with Princeton University Press. I put that book under contract as a grad student. This, too, was largely a product of the name attached to my (then, forthcoming) credentials. I have no doubt that if I’d pitched publishers *the exact same proposal* but I was writing as an instructor at Cochise College or as a grad student at University of North Dakota, the proposal likely would have gone straight into the trash bin. There certainly wouldn’t have been an auction for the rights to the book, really favorable terms for promotion and distribution, an [international book tour](https://musaalgharbi.com/book-tour-we-have-never-been-woke/), and so on.[^1]

For my part, I targeted Princeton University Press as my publisher because I understood that other [symbolic capitalists](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/meet-the-symbolic-capitalists) would take my findings and arguments much more seriously compared to if I published them in a non-academic press or with a less-prestigious academic publisher. For instance, I could have published the exact same book with University of New Mexico Press, but no one would have ever heard about it. Journalists and academics viewed the book as “important” in large part because of who published it (and the institutional pedigreee of the author).

Although *[We Have Never Been Woke](https://musaalgharbi.com/paperback-edition-we-have-never-been-woke/)* criticizes the use of elite credentials to decide whose perspectives matter it is, itself, a direct product of these very tendencies.

Whenever I talk about things like this, people tell me I’m selling myself short and being too modest or, if they’re uncharitable, they assume I must be engaging in *false* modesty. God help me, I am not a particularly modest person. I recognize that I’m smart.[^2] I do the work I’m doing precisely [because](https://musaalgharbi.com/musa-al-gharbi/biography/) I think I have something important to contribute. My point is that I was just as “smart” when I was working as a shoe salesman, adjunct instructor, and freelancer. I recognized I had something to contribute *then* too. But almost no one really cared what I thought, what I knew, or what I was capable of. And if I hadn’t become entangled with elite institutions, few would have known or cared about my perspectives on anything – developed capacities and acquired knowledge be damned. I could have produced the exact same work, and I would’ve had a much harder time publishing it, and if I managed to get it placed somewhere (likely, less prestigious), not many would have read it, let alone celebrated it.

Whoever finds me impressive should know that there are likely “Musa al-Gharbis” all around this country, some of them even with advanced degrees (i.e. who have “an education” albeit from the “wrong” schools), but they will live, work and perish in obscurity because they never managed to get the “correct” credentials with the “correct” brand name attached to them. I only ended up becoming an Ivy League intellectual myself as a result of genuinely bizarre circumstances. And far from my rise to the Ivies being the result of a well-functioning meritocratic order, I have no doubt that I was attractive to Columbia University in large part due to [my non-academic qualities](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/unfortunate-things).

I’m deeply grateful for the ways my Ivy League education changed my life. But the reality is, Columbia University didn’t make me smarter than I was when I arrived (in my mid-to-late 30s). And as it relates to the knowledge and skills I’ve become known for, they’ve been cultivated primarily outside the classroom for the entirety of my academic career. The main way my PhD from Columbia University put me on a different trajectory was by marking me, especially to (other) elites, as someone who should be taken *very* seriously. It wasn’t the education itself that mattered, but the symbolic power of the educational institution I graduated from.

Had I produced the same work without these credentials, I’d have been screaming into a vacuum. Again, this is not speculation, it’s literally my life story. Reckoning with my improbable success and its unsettling sources pushed me to rethink what education does and does not do. Researching and writing my book further challenged my views on what mass education can (and cannot) do, who it (dis)serves, and how.

## The (Ig)Noble Origins of Mass Education

The history of public education in the U.S. is riddled with cultural contradictions.

On the one hand, advocates tied mass education to goals contemporary Americans would still view as noble: developing everyone’s capacities to their fullest, increasing overall economic productivity, supporting meritocracy and social mobility, fostering social cohesion and civic participation. Marginalized and disadvantaged Americans were projected to benefit from this project more than anyone.

On the other hand, many of these same advocates, sometimes in the same paragraph, stressed that public education was important because most poor people and ethnic or religious minorities were basically garbage human beings. Their children, the argument ran, were [destined](https://yalelawjournal.org/essay/the-once-and-future-promise-of-religious-schools-for-poor-and-minority-students) to be trash people too – a drain on society – unless they could be coerced or incentivized into spending most of their waking hours for most days outside of their parents’ homes. They must be [placed under the tutelage](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-credential-society/9780231192354/) of white Anglo-Saxon Protestants (WASPs) who, with God’s help and sufficient patience, time and resources, *may* be able to turn them into decent citizens worth keeping around. Of course, should they fail in that project, deportation, sterilization, imprisonment and other forms of containment were explicitly identified as [fallback plans](https://www.google.com/books/edition/We_Have_Never_Been_Woke/P6kBEQAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&pg=PA58&printsec=frontcover).

That is, sentiments about uplifting the wretched of the earth and integrating women and minorities more fully into society were paired seamlessly with classism, ethnoreligious chauvinism and xenophobia. They were two sides of the same coin. They were both baked into the pie together. This cultural contradiction [continues to define](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/the-biggest-bias-in-higher-education) the project of mass education.

As Agustina Paglayan [demonstrates](https://bookshop.org/a/103354/9780691261270), expansions in mass education in the United States, Europe (and [Latin America](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/education-or-indoctrination-the-violent-origins-of-public-school-systems-in-an-era-of-statebuilding/C72BC036898996925583051B4430F1BF)) over the past 200 years have taken place in moments when elites grow particularly anxious about perceived deficits, pathologies on unruliness of the masses. The fairly explicit and empirically evident purpose of these expansions has been to transform the poor and minorities into more docile subjects who more readily embraced the preferred worldviews of their “betters” (ostensibly for their own good). Efforts to unlock human capital, foster social mobility, and so on have, in practice, been secondary considerations at best.

If *this* is what public schools have primarily been about, it might seem self-evident that parents should send their kids to private schools instead. However, elite schools are largely indoctrination factories too, just with a slightly different set of ends (achieved more successfully). Elite education, in practice is primarily a privilege laundering scheme aimed at transforming antecedent advantages into perceptions of merit.

## Mystification of Social Inequalities

Where to send one’s kids to school is often perceived to be a high-stakes matter. Many notice that elite schools have higher test scores, graduation rates and average GPAs. They notice graduates of these schools are more likely to go to college (and get into “better” colleges) and earn more money than everyone else. Many impute causation to these correlations: the positive observed outcomes *must be* a product of the superior education students receive at these schools. They must be learning more information, cultivating more skills, and employers must be responding to these gains in favoring graduates of elite schools over others.

This is a very optimistic story that jives well with the American sensibility that we’re all basically the same, everyone is capable of anything given the right support, and allocations of opportunity turn primarily on merit. Implicitly, you could take *any* kid and give them the same resources and teaching and they’d enjoy similar outcomes as other elite school graduates or, at the very least, see a vast improvement over their antecedent life prospects. People therefore assume that if they can get *their own* kid enrolled in these schools, their life prospects will be radically improved due to the increased academic knowledge and skills they’ll purportedly accrue.

It is very useful for elites and elite institutions that these perceptions are so widely held.

These assumptions allow elite private schools to charge outlandish tuition ([$80-90k per student per year](https://www.privateschoolreview.com/tuition-stats/private-school-cost-by-state) for top schools in symbolic economy hubs) because the investments and sacrifices will allegedly more than pay for themselves.

This assumption helps elites build capital if they own property near these institutions: families are willing to [pay a premium](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2587017) to be zoned into top public K-12 schools [^3] (or to be within easy commuting distance of elite private schools) because they [believe](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.3982/ECTA10266) the education their kids stand to receive will radically elevate their life-prospects over and above the cost to live there.

More to the point, these perceptions help wealthy and successful people convince others that themselves and their children “deserve” what they have.

They allow top-ranked colleges and universities to select for children of well-off families (who can pay full tuition, make generous endowment gifts, and whose progeny will be well-positioned to make alumni donations), but on ostensibly “ [meritocratic](https://www.politico.com/interactives/2017/top-college-rankings-list-2017-us-news-investigation/) ” grounds – i.e. on the basis of the supposedly “superior” K-12 education these affluent students received at their fancy schools.

Elite college or university credentials enable employers to subsequently perform the same sleight of hand: through their preference for graduates of top universities, elite firms likewise overwhelmingly select [candidates from affluent backgrounds](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691155623/pedigree), albeit on formally “meritocratic” grounds (again, on the basis of the “superior” education they are purported to have received, this time in elite colleges).

But here’s the thing: there’s little evidence that these schools do, in fact, impart more useful knowledge or skills or cultivate students’ capabilities better. The differences in test scores between more and less elite schools seems to be almost purely [a function of selection effects](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/spanish-journal-of-psychology/article/abs/education-and-intelligence-pity-the-poor-teacher-because-student-characteristics-are-more-significant-than-teachers-or-schools/F73D9839D7E1398C33A117CB37E084E8): these schools only admit people who have a high demonstrated level of ability and conscientiousness to begin with, who don’t have bad disciplinary records, and who *do* have [very involved families](https://bookshop.org/a/103354/9780520271425) (who work aggressively to jump through all sorts of logistical, administrative and financial hoops to get their children through the admissions process and support their attendance). These are kids who are *already* likely to show up to class regularly and on-time, complete all their assignments promptly and in accordance with the expectations of their teachers, perform well on tests (as a product of their higher abilities, study habits, access to tutoring, and additional parental support and accountability mechanisms) and stay out of trouble. They were poised for academic success regardless of which school they happened to be placed in. And they would have tested the same regardless of what school they went to.

Don’t take my word for it: research consistently finds that it makes [little-to-no practical difference](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272775719300901) whether children of relatively well-off families attend elite K-12 schools or not. The fancy classes students take at these schools do not have a significant impact on [standardized test scores](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.6.3.30) or [any collegiate outcomes](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17286). Being surrounded by other affluent, white, and high-achieving peers has no measurable impact on [one’s own standardized test scores](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.3982/ECTA10266) or [college enrollment or completion, or the quality of college](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.6.3.58) one gets into. Kids already poised for success in life enjoy roughly the same success regardless of whether they attend a high-performing K-12 school or not.

For students of less privileged backgrounds who get folded into these schools, there is a [more substantial](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.6.3.20) benefit to attending, but it’s almost purely [non-academic](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/724568) in nature. Test scores and related intellectual indicators [don’t change much](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0162373716672039) for these students and their grades often [decline](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20170599). Yet, although there are few-to-no observed *academic* benefits for these students, they often report [better climate](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0162373716672039) than the institutions they hailed from. As a result, they enjoy their time in school more, even if little changes about their intellectual aptitude or academic performance. And so, for kids who are otherwise more likely to drop out or decline to proceed to college, attending an elite school helps with persistence. But for kids from supportive families who planned on going to college anyway, these benefits are moot.

Non-traditional students who end up in elite schools also face much lower risk of [arrests](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/126/4/2063/1922935) and [disciplinary incidents](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2006.00702.x) than they otherwise might. Wealthier kids, of course, are generally unlikely to face arrest or disciplinary action irrespective of where they attend school.

Nonelite pupils also gain access to social networks and cultural capital that they would not have been exposed to (which more affluent peers *would* have irrespective of where they attend school). And as a consequence, the “ [privileged poor](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/catalog.php?isbn=9780674976894) ” become much more legible and appealing to elite gatekeepers when they attend elite k-12 schools, irrespective of their academic “merits.”

But for everyone else, there is no empirical difference. The literature on this is robust and unambiguous. But it’s incredibly hard to get the “trust the science” crowd to actually process and act on this information in their own lives.

## The Sham of Elite “Education”

For rich and poor kids alike, the actual education students receive at elite schools is, broadly, unrelated to their life outcomes. They don’t actually learn much more than they would at a different school. More critically, no one really cares what they learned or didn’t learn in these institutions anyway. Conventional elite students understand this, and it shapes how they approach their education.

In his [ethnographic study](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691229201/privilege) of a prestigious boarding school, sociologist Shamus Khan found that elite students don’t tend to work very hard or very long at their studies and, what’s more, they often disdain those who do. They don’t typically strive to master the material from their courses. Instead, they rely heavily on SparkNotes to avoid doing the readings (today, LLMs). They virtually never do optional readings or ungraded assignments (they’re not motivated to push themselves intellectually or to learn as much as they can; they do what they must to check required boxes and nothing more). Yet, none of this matters for their life trajectories because more important than the formal curriculum that students broadly neglect is the ‘hidden curriculum’ that elite students generally *do* come to master at these schools: how to present oneself as knowledgeable, competent, poised and “naturally” talented even in the absence of substantive understanding or skill; how to tell a compelling story to elite gatekeepers in order to pique their interest and tug at their heartstrings; how to navigate institutional bureaucracies and rules and turn them to one’s advantage; how to take tests well (and how to aggressively [game](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12207-021-09429-7) disability policies to [juice one’s scores](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/07/30/us/extra-time-504-sat-act.html)); how to effectively socialize and network with other elites.

The formal instruction at elite schools is far from rigorous. As I detail in *[We Have Never Been Woke](https://musaalgharbi.com/paperback-edition-we-have-never-been-woke/)*, all the “social justice” educational fads are far more prominent in elite schools than elsewhere. Moreover, there is a lot more pressure in elite spaces to master these ways of talking, thinking, and presenting oneself in order to demonstrate that you’re one of the “good” elites, a person who belongs in the Google, NY Times, and McKinsey & Co. crowd.

Schools in Mississippi can aggressively follow the “ [science of reading](https://publichealthpolicyjournal.com/mississippi-reading-their-reading-proficiency-surge-isnt-a-fluke-its-a-national-mandate/) ” all they’d like. Insofar as the education reforms do open doors for students, they’ll [probably end up relocating](https://mississippitoday.org/2025/07/15/faq-mississippi-brain-drain-crisis/#bd-large) to symbolic economy hubs like Houston or Atlanta – benefiting those young people, perhaps, but doing little for their communities of origin. Irrespective of how well or poorly Mississippi teaches it’s students, the state is still likely to remain [poor](https://worldpopulationreview.com/state-rankings/poorest-states) for the foreseeable future. Elite schools wouldn’t deign to emulate *[those](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/book-announcement-those-people)* [people](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/book-announcement-those-people).

In any case, it’s broadly recognized that kids at elite schools will be fine no matter how they’re taught. What’s more, imparting the “correct” cultural capital and habitus is far more important for elites than their mastery of literature, history, mathematics and so on.  
  
Indeed, these affluent students will eventually get into elite colleges largely [as a result](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/141/1/51/8306880) of their *non-* academic credentials, their social connections and familial wealth. As someone who was both a student and grader at an Ivy League school, you can tell who arrived at these institutions from elite k-12 schools and who didn’t… and not in a good way.

Indeed, studies have [found](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/137/2/845/6449025) that graduates of elite secondary schools who get into elite colleges tend to be worse than average on the academic front. That is, they tend to be relatively weak students, not prodigious ones. Nonetheless, they tend to perform particularly well socioeconomically after graduation, especially relative to peers from less advantaged backgrounds who graduated from the same university and with higher GPAs. Why? Because while students from non-elite backgrounds tend to prioritize their academics when they get into top colleges, students from elite backgrounds spend much more time [networking and socializing](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/soc4.12068) — and these latter behaviors tend to yield much higher career dividends in our ostensibly “meritocratic” system than cultivated knowledge and skills.  
  
Consider: the typical full-time college student in the U.S. today spends about [three hours per day](https://www.slowboring.com/p/college-students-should-study-more) on classes, studying and homework combined -- down from 40 hours in the 1960s, and 27 hours in 2003. They spend significantly more time socializing than doing anything academic.

At elite schools, the expectations are lower still, because if wealthy families are paying [upwards of $90k per student per year in tuition](https://www.businessinsider.com/most-expensive-colleges-tuition-in-us), they expect a pleasant student experience. And, again, one of the main life enhancing benefits of being at these schools is to form relationships with current and future elites, not leaning Plato more effectively. Students are being pragmatic in putting socializing above their studies. But parents expect their kids to graduate with stellar GPAs anyway. And the customer is boss.

Far from being more rigorous or demanding, grades at elite schools are something of a joke. At both the K-12 and collegiate level, studies have found that educational institutions that cater to elites tend to [grade far](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016146811211400707) *[less](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016146811211400707)* [harshly](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016146811211400707) than most other schools. And the divide has only grown: in recent decades, grades at high schools attended by more affluent students have [shot up significantly](https://fordhaminstitute.org/sites/default/files/20180919-grade-inflation-high-schools-2005-2016_0.pdf) (far more than at other institutions), even though scores on [standardized tests](https://hechingerreport.org/newest-advantage-rich-america-higher-grades/) and other [proficiency exams](https://www.educationnext.org/lower-bars-higher-college-gpas-how-grade-inflation-boosting-college-graduation-rates/) have remained stagnant or declined. That is, elite students aren’t getting any better in terms of measured knowledge or skill, but their grades rapidly inflating anyway*.* As a consequence, graduates of elite programs often look like meritocratically superior students on paper. Yet this appearance is largely an artifact of the reality that less privileged students tend to be graded more closely according to their “merits,” while wealthy kids feel more-or-less entitled to good grades irrespective of their actual performance.

At contemporary elite colleges and universities, an A has become more-or-less [the default grade](https://gradeinflation.com/). As reported in *[The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2025/08/harvard-college-grade-inflation/684021/), “* In 2011, 60 percent of all grades at Harvard were in the A range (up from 33 percent in 1985). By the 2020–21 academic year, that share had risen to 79 percent.” A committee at Harvard [recently found](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/06/us/harvard-students-absenteeism.html) that students regularly get A’s at the university despite rarely doing the readings and regularly skipping class.

In contexts like these, Bs are reserved for significantly worse-than-average students (i.e. students who show they have a particularly poor grasp of the material) who seem to be at least making an effort. Cs or below are assigned almost exclusively to students aren’t meeting the minimum course requirements and competencies at all, and with the foreknowledge that parents and academic counselors (and sometimes higher-ups) will likely intercede to revise the grade upwards anyway, irrespective of what the student may actually “deserve” (most typically, in these cases, they actually deserve an “F” or, at best, a “D”). Implicit in these “ [grade-grubbing](https://slate.com/human-interest/2014/05/why-professors-inflate-grades-because-their-jobs-depend-on-it.html) ” interventions is the message that professors are not really there to decide who should and should not receive elite credentials — admissions has already done that. The job of instructors is simply to launder students’ socioeconomic privilege into academic “merit” by assigning grades that match the luster of the elite credentials that students’ families (or [future selves](https://www.wsj.com/articles/financially-hobbled-for-life-the-elite-masters-degrees-that-dont-pay-off-11625752773)) are paying such immense sums to obtain.

Wherever students ultimately end up attending, they tend to [learn very little in college](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/A/bo10327226.html). They take classes in math and get good grades but show no growth in their actual mathematical knowledge or abilities when comparing pre and post assessments. They take writing classes and got As on their papers but their writing does not demonstrably improve. They take classes in their majors that were supposed to impart subject-matter expertise but retain very little. This was the case *before* students were using LLMs to complete their assignments (when elite students [heavily relied on Kenyans](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/05/technology/kenya-college-essays-ai.html) to complete their work instead). It’s surely worse today.

But in the end, it doesn’t really matter if most learn anything in college or not. [Roughly half](https://www.strada.org/reports/talent-disrupted) of all grads end up working in a job that doesn’t require a college degree at all. For those whose position *does* require a degree, it’s usually just a generic requirement for *any* BA+ credential. Symbolic profession employers generally care far more about the name brand at the top of the degree than what applicants majored in, which specific courses they took, and what grades they earned therein. Indeed, nearly half of those who work at jobs requiring a college degree are working in a field [unrelated to their major](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2013/05/do-big-cities-help-college-graduates-find-better-jobs/). No one outside the university cares very much what you learned in college. Most employers assume (correctly) that entry-level hires know practically nothing, fancy piece of paper notwithstanding.

The degree requirements for most white-collar jobs are [superfluous](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/dismissed-by-degrees/). Most people learn [how to do their jobs](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/15/12/learning-learn-work) *on* the job, [not in college](https://bookshop.org/a/103354/9780226197289). In practice, one doesn’t need to have previously completed four years of postsecondary schooling to master the specific tasks and responsibilities that graduates typically learn to perform at work.

At bottom, employers don’t prefer degree holders because they are hungry for the specialized knowledge and skills they (typically fail to) cultivate while pursuing their specific majors. They’re hungry for capable and conscientious conformists and, as It turns out, college degrees, [especially](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/i/173022238/degrees-of-separation) [from elite schools](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/i/173022238/degrees-of-separation), are a great way to screen for folks like *that*.

However, every aspect of elite education is designed to obscure these realities, including and especially from elites themselves.

## You Can’t Educate Your Way to “Social Justice”

To recap: the education system in the U.S. has long been defined by a deep cultural contradiction. On the one hand, advocates for mass education hoped to use the classroom to integrate the marginalized and uplift the disadvantaged. On the other hand, their conception of these “others” was bigoted and condescending and elites’ desire to empower others was tempered by their greater desire to preserve and enhance *their* *own* social position and to keep the poor and minorities “in line.”

In practice, irrespective of intent, mass and elite education work synergistically to reinforce and legitimize social inequalities. When this [mystification](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences/applied-and-social-sciences-magazines/mystification#:~:text=When%20reference%20is%20made%20to%20mystification%20in%20Marx%2C,the%20relativization%20of%20ideological%20positions%20over%20material%20reality.) system is functioning well, inequalities derived from differences in educational attainment and educational “quality” come to be viewed as [natural and appropriate](https://us.sagepub.com/en-us/nam/reproduction-in-education-society-and-culture/book203162). They come to be seen as reflecting, first and foremost, the superior grit, discipline, hard work and cultivated talent of those who manage to get degrees (and especially elite degrees) as compared to those who don’t.

As beneficiaries proceed from elite pre-K, to elite primary and secondary schools, to elite colleges, and into elite jobs at elite firms, they grow increasingly confident they “deserve” or are entitled to the comfortable jobs, above-average (dual) incomes, and relatively stable homelives that likely await them. Over time, an [implicit](https://www.chronicle.com/article/the-insufferable-hubris-of-the-well-credentialed) (and often [explicit](https://musaalgharbi.com/2019/08/27/unearned-smugness-partisan-diploma-divide/)) conviction will grow that those with fewer or lower-status credentials are reciprocally *[un](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/i/152460419/symbolic-capitalists-may-be-especially-prone-to-bias-and-motivated-reasoning)* [worthy](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/i/152460419/symbolic-capitalists-may-be-especially-prone-to-bias-and-motivated-reasoning) of working the same jobs, enjoying the same lifestyles, and being afforded the same respect or deference as people like themselves.

The elitist side of this cultural contradiction has been a very successful project. The egalitarian side of the contradiction has been much less effective. Basically, nothing works to reduce observed disparities. Virtually all efforts have been great disappointments.

For instance, you might think the big difference between rich and poor students is the funding available to each. You’d be wrong: the schools with the most poor and minority students generally [receive more per capita](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/school-spending-equity-since-1976/) than those catering to the richest and whitest students. More importantly, there just [isn’t a strong connection](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/a-state-level-perspective-on-school-spending-and-educational-outcomes/) between spending, educational outcomes, and long-term life prospects.

Governments in the U.S. spends an average of [$17.6k per student](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026/school-system-finances.html) per year in public schools. The [average private school tuition](https://www.privateschoolreview.com/tuition-stats/private-school-cost-by-state) nationwide is $15k per student per year (i.e. 15 percent *less* than public school allocations). The latter tend to have higher test scores, graduation rates, college completion, and lifetime earnings. Throwing still *more* money at public schools is unlikely to erase observed differences in outcomes.

One might think that the most important difference between the good schools and the bad schools isn’t money per se, but teacher quality. However, this isn’t well-supported empirically either. Research has [found](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/spanish-journal-of-psychology/article/abs/education-and-intelligence-pity-the-poor-teacher-because-student-characteristics-are-more-significant-than-teachers-or-schools/F73D9839D7E1398C33A117CB37E084E8) that teachers are responsible for only about 1- 7% percent of total variance in student academic achievement. In other words, you could give all the least advantaged people the very best teachers in America while all of the affluent kids received the very worst teachers in America, and distressingly little would change in terms of standardized test scores, knowledge and skill acquisition, grades and so on.

The economist Raj Chetty argues that there are *other* ways that teacher quality can matter ([part 1](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.9.2593), [part 2](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.9.2633)): how instructors manage the classroom, how well they support students having difficulties, how pleasant they are to interact with… these factors can make a difference in ways that may be consequential for lifetime prospects but might not show up in academic indicators.

Yet, even here, it seems the main benefit of good teachers is bottom skewed: trading a mediocre teacher for an excellent teacher makes a much smaller difference than replacing a bad teacher with a mediocre one. And this difference matters most for kids who might otherwise drop out or decline to go to college (i.e. teacher quality matters less for those whom high school graduation and college attendance are virtually guaranteed anyway).

The issue isn’t that elites have especially *good* teachers, it’s that many poor students have especially *bad* teachers (and, as a result, they come to hate school and become less likely to do their work, graduate or go on to college while becoming more likely to get into trouble, and they lack support structures outside of school to get them to push through). However, it’s hard to actually cut bad instructors in low-performing public schools because teachers’ unions aggressively resist most accountability measures.[^4] Moreover, while there are many other reasons to replace these bad teachers, the *academic* effects of these efforts would be fairly small, and the gaps between more and less elite students wouldn’t shrink that much even if all the worst teachers in the country were purged and replaced.

Here, one might think that the biggest problem isn’t in the classroom, it’s all the other stuff that happens in the school: weak disciplinary policies, a culture of low expectations, institutional pressures to force students through regardless of whether they meet key benchmarks or not, etc. If we changed these things, the gaps would surely close, right? It turns out, not so much.

Massive experiments across dozens of schools in multiple states have [found](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/129/3/1355/1817328) that even if districts spent massive money and dedicated incredible effort to ensuring low-performing schools operated just like elite schools (increased instructional time, hiring more effective teachers and administrators while cutting poor performers, high-dosage tutoring, data-driven instruction, a culture of high expectations), the effects on educational outcomes are modest: no improvement in reading, some improvement in math (one sixth of one standard deviation per year), no change in attendance. Test scores, on average, remained lower at these schools than others in their states. And this modest result was generated with levels of resources that could not plausibly scale or be sustained over the long term (as the scholars themselves acknowledge in their research).

Faced with these findings, one might think the solution isn’t to try to make the public schools more like private schools, but to just make it easier for parents to put their kids into other schools by allowing them to apply the tax dollars allocated for their kid’s public education towards a different institution. However, school choice seems unlikely to change much about the broad distribution of outcomes either.

Voucher experiments tried out in a number of states [have been disastrous](https://azmirror.com/2023/12/11/one-year-in-arizonas-universal-school-vouchers-are-a-cautionary-tale-for-the-rest-of-the-nation/). They basically served as subsidies for wealthy families who already send their kids to private schools to now do so on the taxpayer dime (instead of spending their own ample resources) while creating lots of dysfunction for everyone else. Many states who’ve tried these experiments have rolled them back because of the results are so consistently terrible.

More broadly, charter schools are not systematically better than public options. Massive studies looking at a range of academic outcomes have long found that they’re inferior, on average. More recent data has shown charters edge ahead a bit, yet even *this* relative performance is wildly uneven. Only 1 in 3 charters have better outcomes than public counterparts. 1 in 5 actually have worse outcomes, and [the rest are statistically indistinguishable](https://www.edweek.org/policy-politics/charter-schools-now-outperform-traditional-public-schools-sweeping-study-finds/2023/06). That is, despite charters doing ever-so slightly better at the macro level, families have a two-thirds chance of being in the same academic situation or a worse one if they move their kid out of their zoned public school and into a charter.[^5] Most families [do not have](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-barriers-that-make-charter-schools-inaccessible-to-disadvantaged-families/) an easy way of identifying which of these schools are more or less effective in general or for their own children, so moving their kids would be a gamble they’d fail to gain from most of the time.

And the ROI for increasing school choice is pretty small in any event: studies have [found](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272723001974) that even a massive change like increasing the charter market share by a full 10 percentage points only raises test scores by a single hundredth of a standard deviation and increases graduation rates by just 1-2 percentage points.

You can give families the ability to change schools, but the big problem for school choice and virtually all other approaches to addressing disparities through education is that most of the gaps observed in the education space are not, themselves, a product of educational content, delivery or quality. They are downstream of [more fundamental differences](https://freddiedeboer.substack.com/p/education-doesnt-work-30) in ability and life circumstances (supportive, involved and academically-inclined parents, safe communities, [stable homelives](https://www.theatlantic.com/education/2026/01/texas-education-community-schools/685703/)) that schools cannot plausibly erase.

The wealth and life prospects of the rich kids is not, in fact, a product of what happens in the classroom at their elite K-12 schools. Nor are the life prospects of poor people shaped primarily by what happens in *their* K-12 classrooms. The main reasons these schools have different outcomes is they just pull from different pools of people: the elite schools only admit those already poised for success and then take credit for that success. Low-income public schools are often saddled with students with disabilities, deep behavioral issues, difficult home lives, and lower antecedent levels of measured ability, but then they’re blamed for underperforming elite counterparts. This credit and blame is largely misallocated across the board because it’s overwhelmingly selection effects driving the differences all the way down.

This is why, no matter how aggressive or ambitious the treatment is, the effects tend to be fairly modest. Changing teachers, disciplinary policies, resources, or even schools changes very little for most people’s life prospects. Even the slight benefits observed tend to hold for very narrow subset of students. For instance, some efforts to more comprehensively identify and cultivate merit can help exceptional students from less privileged backgrounds achieve social mobility – albeit mostly as a result of *non* -academic improvements, as we’ve seen. In the process of providing a leg up for high-ability but underperforming kids, it’s possible to [diversity society’s elites](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/diversity-meritocracy-arose-together) at the margins (while intensifying competition for a place among them). But this won’t do *anything* to help people of average or below average abilities.[^6] In fact, the more genuinely meritocratic the system the harder it is on *those* folks (i.e. the vast majority of the population). As former Harvard University president Larry Summers (in)famously [put it](https://direct.mit.edu/ajle/article/doi/10.1162/ajle_a_00024/107212/HOW-MERITOCRACY-FUELS-INEQUALITY-PART-I-The):

> “One of the challenges in our society is that the truth is kind of a disequalizer. One of the reasons that inequality has probably gone up in our society is that people are being treated closer to the way that they’re \[meritocratically\] supposed to be treated.”

It should be no surprise that the transition to the “ [knowledge economy](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/introduction-to-the-symbolic-economy) ” has been accompanied by growing inequality, rising affective polarization and increased institutional dysfunction and mistrust: to the extent that our society allocates socioeconomic opportunities and sociocultural prestige on the basis of things like measured intellectual abilities and educational attainment, these divides and tensions are likely to persist and expand.

All said, education is a great way to identify, cultivate and confer legitimacy upon what DuBois called the “ [Talented Tenth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talented_tenth).” However, it cannot close inequalities between ordinary people and elites. Quite the opposite.[^7]

This does not mean we should burn down or defund all public schools. There are other laudable functions that mass education can more plausibly serve (ensuring functional literacy and numeracy; providing fundamental information about history, culture and society – one’s own and others’; helping people get an accurate understanding of their own abilities and the life paths plausibly open to them; preparing people for vocations suited to their interests and abilities; serving as sites of socialization and community bonding; providing a haven for children in difficult circumstances). We should try to help institutions fulfill *those* purposes better and more consistently instead of trying to force these institutions to perform roles they are ill-suited for.

## Putting My Kids Where My Mouth Is

These questions around education are front of mind because of a recent viral article by Nicole Hannah Jones (NHJ).

For those unfamiliar, the New York Times author wrote [an essay](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/06/12/magazine/choosing-a-school-for-my-daughter-in-a-segregated-city.html) in 2016 discussing how she chose to send her own daughter to a school full of poor and low-income families. Her explicit goal was to resist segregation in New York City schools (among the [most segregated](https://civilrightsproject.ucla.edu/press-releases/report-shows-school-segregation-in-new-york-remains-worst-in-nation/) in the entire U.S.) and to to use her resources and influence to give other students opportunities they would not otherwise have. On September 20 <sup>th</sup> of this year she published a [follow-up](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/magazine/ny-public-schools-black-students-segregation.html) essay, 10 years on, explaining that her daughter, now in high-school, feels like she was ill-served by this decision. And NHJ agrees, excoriating herself for putting moral principles above her own kid’s welfare while accomplishing almost nothing (in her view) for less affluent families in the process.

A lot of what follows will be extremely critical of NHJ, so I should start by giving some props. Taken at face value, the experiment is an attempt to put her principles into practice, by investing her own child into institutions and communities, and trying to control what she can control. I think this is [really important](https://osf.io/preprints/socarxiv/wd54z_v1) for people to do. Although, as we’ll see, it’s a little unclear what NHJ’s ultimate goals and values *are* here. Moreover, her approach to living her values was not remotely pragmatic and, as a consequence, will likely deter others from even trying to emulate her.

But I digress. This is supposed to be the “nice words” portion. In that spirit, I think it’s great NHJ publicly acknowledged her perceived mistakes. I’ve spent [much of this year](https://musaalgharbi.substack.com/p/daniel-bell-and-me), and a lot of this essay, detailing things that I’ve changed my mind about. More public figures [should do the same](https://www.vox.com/science-and-health/2019/1/4/17989224/intellectual-humility-explained-psychology-replication). Although, as we’ll see, the more one reflects on the essay in question, the less honest and transparent it reads.

The text itself is evocative and well-written. But I must admit that I found myself frustrated throughout by the failure to grapple with the actual stakes of her experiment which were, in fact, minimal.

Very little changed about her daughters’ life prospects as a result of being sent to these schools. It’s not like there’s a plausible scenario wherein NHJ’s kid is going to be unable to get into an elite school if she wants to go to college. She isn’t going to end up poor or working class (or even conventionally middle class) because her parents didn’t send her to elite K-12 schools.

Najya, her daughter, will be just fine no mater where she goes or what she learns (or fails to learn) in school. Her parents are [multimillionaires](https://richestlifestyle.com/nikole-hannah-jones-net-worth/). They’re prolific and influential people that many elite institutions are eager to have deeper relationships with. Also, as the article takes pains to note, she’s black. Schools are not supposed to consider race and gender anymore, but it’s an open secret that elite schools still do. As such, being able to help “diversify” the school is an admissions asset. Moreover, despite being rich, Najya can honestly claim to have grown up in poor and segregated schools in Brooklyn before climbing her way to more selective institutions. As I detail in my [first book](https://musaalgharbi.com/paperback-edition-we-have-never-been-woke/) (pp. 251-3), this sort of poverty LARPing is extremely common throughout the symbolic professions. Long story short, Ivy League schools will eat. her. up. Had she completed her K-12 education at these poor and minority-majority schools she would have been, if anything, even *more* attractive to elite school selection committees.

Moreover, all else failing, Najya’s mom is a [tenured chair](https://profiles.howard.edu/nikole-hannah-jones) at Howard, where she’d *definitely* be able to gain admission (in virtue of being the kid of a faculty member) and where she’d even get a [tuition discount](https://hr.howard.edu/working-hu/tuition-benefits) (although it’s completely unnecessary because, again, her parents have more than enough money to pay full tuition out of pocket anywhere in the world). And regardless of where or whether she goes to college, her mom will have no problem setting her up with a media job. Indeed, her recent essay will go a long way towards making Najya familiar to everyone in the business.

Incidentally, the essay vividly demonstrated how much NHJ’s daughter had already internalized the culture and aspirations of elite symbolic capitalists by, among other things, the fact that she had looked up her school’s rankings and complained about how low they were.[^8] This is something that would be unlikely to occur to my own kids because they haven’t been enculturated to think everything is a competition and to obsess over rankings. And if it ever did occur to them to look up their schools, they’d certainly be embarrassed to complain about them, let alone demand to go to a higher ranked institution. I didn’t raise them to think in terms of institutional prestige or to be laser focused on getting into Harvard because I recognize that, in fact, they’ll be just fine regardless of where they go to school (both in K-12 *or* college).[^9]

Incidentally, although I didn’t try to write a viral NY Times essay about it,[^10] I also sent my own kids to their zoned public school for the six school years [we lived in Manhattan](https://musaalgharbi.com/2025/01/01/how-we-lived-in-nyc/) (2016-2023). I am myself, a product of public schools from kindergarten through high school, into community college, and the local public land grant school where I got my BA and MA degrees. I now teach at a public university.

My wife and I never felt the pull of coming up with tens-of-thousands of dollars per kid per year on private schools to keep our children away from the darks and the poors, nor did we have any desire to spend comparable money to live in a place where “those people” would not be included in the schools we sent our own kids to. Instead, we just yielded to the default and sent our kids to their zoned public school. Although PS165 is mere blocks from Columbia University (and our apartment), very few Columbia affiliates sent their children there. Our kids received a fine primary education nonetheless.

PS165 is an institution with some notable alums: towards the end of the period when Harlem was a [Jewish enclave](https://archive.org/details/whenharlemwasjew0000guro/page/n247/mode/2up), the school was [attended by J.D. Salinger](https://short-stories.co/@j.d.salinger/the-laughing-man-e0kl1kqkjzxp). These days, the school is mostly (61 percent) black and Hispanic and most (58 percent of) families have incomes that qualify them as “ [economically disadvantaged](https://data.nysed.gov/enrollment.php?year=2025&instid=800000047461).” In terms of academic measures, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, it [ranked](https://tools.nycenet.edu/dashboard/?utm_source=schools.nyc.gov&utm_medium=Public_Page&utm_campaign=School_Page#dbn=03M165&report_type=EMS&view=City) well below the NYC median in English and math. However, its relative position increased greatly throughout the pandemic – less a matter of genuine improvements in learning than in minimizing the [learning loss](https://www.governing.com/education/nyc-schools-struggle-to-regain-covids-lost-generation) that plagued so many other schools (school leadership deserves credit for this stability, of course, but it’s an importantly different accomplishment than actually raising scores). In more recent years, as others began recovering a bit from the pandemic, the school returned to being a below-median institution.

I didn’t know any of these details at the time my wife and I were making our decisions about schooling (I looked up these stats for essays I wrote after I’d already moved out of the city). I didn’t care about stuff like this very much. Unlike NHJ, I wasn’t *trying* to send my kids to a minority-majority, majority low-income, or academically below-average school. I just sent them to the school we were zoned for. And things worked out just fine at PS165.

For middle school, things were a little different because you *do* have to make choices about where you send your kids. Literally, [every single family](https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article/?kanumber=KA-02865) must apply to 12 middle schools and then an algorithm pairs students to educational institutions based on a range of factors.[^11] We chose the schools closest to our apartment. My son was ultimately slotted into Columbia Secondary (my daughter only got through elementary school in the city and never went through this process).

Columbia Secondary has very different characteristics than PS 165 or the NYC system as a whole. A plurality of the students were still Hispanic, and there were more black and Hispanic students than other group. But there were a lot more white and Asian students than in other public schools, and a significantly higher number of affluent families. In terms of academics, Columbia Secondary ranked [among the best in the city](https://tools.nycenet.edu/dashboard/#dbn=05M362&report_type=EMS&view=City).[^12] My son received a great education there too but, to be honest, it wasn’t really *that* different from PS 165.

In any event, upon transitioning to Long Island for my current job, once again we simply enrolled our children in their zoned public schools. My son is in high school now like Najya. He is thriving. So is my (now) middle-school aged daughter.

Sending our kids to their zoned schools in Manhattan may not have dramatically changed the entire school or community, but we definitely *did* make a difference at the margins. My wife was the president of the PTA and helped organize all sorts of opportunities for students. My kids were both in dual-language programs for the entirety of elementary school.[^13] Consequently, most of their friends were lower-income black and Hispanic kids. They are many respects in which these peers were observably influenced in a positive way through interaction with our kids and our family over the six years our lives were intwined. Did we radically change those kids life prospects 10 years from now? Maybe not. Did we make things a little better for families in the neighborhood for the time we lived there? Absolutely. And that’s not nothing.

Granted, the effects would have been compounded if more Columbia University families had followed our path. But it’s not like a thing is only worth doing if that action, in itself, radically remakes the entire system. NHJ seems to believe she was a failure because she didn’t single-handedly transform the school. That’s the wrong way to think about the issue altogether. Almost nothing one does will change the entire system or institution. That isn’t really a good reason to avoid doing what you can, and controlling what you can control, to concretely help others.

That said, in Nicole Hannah-Jones’ case, she clearly tried to control more than she possibly could or should have. It seemed to be *really* important to her that she turn things around single-handedly. In fact, when the city was going to adjust zoning in a manner that would’ve led to a significant number of additional affluent families being pulled into her kid’s elementary school – the exact thing she was ostensibly advocating for (more affluent New Yorkers sending their kids to schools like PS 307) – rather than celebrating the move, she threw her weight around to prevent a “ [yuppie invasion](https://nypost.com/2026/09/25/us-news/nikole-hannah-jones-campaigned-against-a-yuppie-invasion-of-kids-from-whiter-areas-to-failing-school-they-sent-daughter/) ” of the school.

There is tons of research showing that if those kids *had* been folded into her daughter’s school it would have probably improved test scores, parental involvement and community investment. It also likely would’ve helped less affluent students achieve social mobility through access to cultural capital and the formation of bridging ties ([here](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04996-4), [here](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04997-3)). It could have rendered the affluent students [more likely](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/702626) to build bridging ties across class and ethnic lines throughout their life course. In short, the policy had potential to advance all of the goals NHJ ostensibly set out to achieve. But, of course, the credit for any positive developments stemming from that change would have redounded to the city’s zoning decisions, not to the heroic individual efforts of the author of the 1619 Project who, to the detriment of all students who were not already rich, worked to kill the measure… an episode that goes conveniently unmentioned in her essay. But I digress:

Another strong proponent of [living one’s values](https://www.slowboring.com/p/live-your-values), journalist Matt Yglesias also sent his kid to his zoned public school in Washington D.C. His son also ended up attending a low-income, urban, minority-majority school. His family had a [similar experience](https://www.slowboring.com/p/why-i-send-my-kid-to-low-income-urban) to my own: mostly positive. Our stories are not outliers. In fact, it is the Hannah-Jones family experience that is not remotely typical of public schools in NYC or in general.

NHJ did *not* simply send her kid to her zoned public school (like Matt and I did). She instead chose to send her daughter to basically the worst schools she could find — schools that were not particularly close to her home. What was subsequently chronicled in her essay was not representative of what her daughter would have experienced if Nayja had just attended the school she was zoned for — a school that likely would still have had lots of poor and minority students her family could have helped, but wouldn’t have been as gripping for promotional purposes.

The elementary school Najya ultimately attended through her mom’s intervention, [PS 307](https://tools.nycenet.edu/dashboard/#dbn=13K307&report_type=EMS&view=City), was one of the city’s lowest performing schools (bottom quartile) *before* the COVID-19 pandemic and went into a tailspin after, ranking as one of the very worst schools in the city for 2024 and 2025 (although her kid was already in middle school by then). The dysfunction at the school was so severe that many parents referred to it as “ [Little Riker’s Island](https://www.change.org/p/please-help-us-save-our-school-and-community-before-it-s-too-late-6896902a-32dd-4b84-b34d-c40d25f1b84d).”

The New York Times essay repeatedly tries to displace blame for this situation onto everyone other than the teachers and leadership. NHJ repeatedly asserts that schools serving minorities and poor people are underfunded (citing outdated data) and understaffed. In reality, PS307 received [82% more funding per student](https://apps.schools.nyc/dsbpo/sbag/default.aspx?DDBSSS_INPUT=K307&fy=2025) from state and local coffers than the typical NYC school, topped off with additional dollars from the federal government and other sources, totaling [$52k allocated per student](https://www.districtfacts.com/ny/school/ps-307-daniel-hale-williams/). The student-teacher ratio is as good as it can possibly be: [1 teacher for every 10 students](https://www.districtfacts.com/ny/school/ps-307-daniel-hale-williams/). The school isn’t hurting for resources, it’s just ineffective at translating these investments into positive outcomes for the students. Again, there isn’t really a strong empirical relationship between school funding and educational outcomes. PS 307 is a clear illustration of how the two can come apart.

In any case, after Najya graduated from elementary school, she was enrolled in the [Urban Assembly Unison School](https://tools.nycenet.edu/dashboard/#dbn=13K351&report_type=EMS&view=City) for her middle years. Once again, NHJ simply declined to send her kid to any of the (better) public schools nearer to her home in order to send her to an institution that would make for more compelling storytelling. During the time her daughter attended this middle school (2021-2024), it was improving for English (albeit still in the bottom quartile for the city) but declining in math (ending up around the bottom decile for the city) — this despite the fact that it was *also* very well-funded and staffed. Urban Assembly Unison was allocated [36% more funding per student](https://apps.schools.nyc/dsbpo/sbag/default.aspx?DDBSSS_INPUT=K351&fy=2026) than the typical NYC school, totaling nearly [$44k per student](https://www.districtfacts.com/ny/school/urban-assembly-unison-school/) per year from all sources. The teacher-to-student ratio was still a very favorable [15:1](https://www.districtfacts.com/ny/school/urban-assembly-unison-school/).

Neither one of these institutions are remotely typical NYC schools. In terms of resources and staffing, they’re major positive outliers. In terms of academic performance, they’re both extreme negative outliers. NHJ chose them precisely *because of* their poor reputation (explicitly!). As such, her daughter’s experience should *not* be held up as the norm for what it would be like to send your kids to a family’s zoned public school. But that’s precisely what has transpired anyway.

The lesson that many seem to have learned from her essay is, “the most moral course of action is to keep your kids away from the darks, the poors and public schools wholecloth or they’ll be doomed! DOOMED!”

In truth, the children of symbolic capitalists will generally be fine, even in urban, minority-majority, low-income, and academically unimpressive institutions. Again, I speak from experience here. And there’s lots of data backing me up too. As we have seen, the ultimate educational outcomes of attending public schools are unlikely to be much different than if one’s kids attended selective private schools. Additionally, there are lots of non-academic virtues (for your own family and your community) to raising one’s kids alongside normies instead of other symbolic capitalist children. Relatively affluent families could also spare themselves all the wealth destruction entailed by burning thousands of dollars per kid per year on something that makes basically no material difference for the life prospects of their children.

If there’s one lesson that should be learned from the piece, it’d be “don’t actively seek out the very worst schools you can find and then send your kid to those schools in a narcissistic bid to enhance your own profile while loosely pointing towards social justice goals.” Each of the essays in this series succeeded in boosting the profile of NHJ and her daughter. She’ll probably win another Pulitzer for her latest. Her daughter was on *[This American Life](https://www.thisamericanlife.org/562/the-problem-we-all-live-with-part-one)* and will likely be blitzing other prestige media in the coming weeks. But for all the good the piece has done for *them,* it’s been an absolute disaster for the causes NHJ claims to support — not because it’s an example of a high-profile liberal getting “mugged by reality,” but precisely because the piece declined to confront some basic (inconvenient) realities.

The truth is, despite not loving her school, Najya had an *extremely* comfortable life. She lives in a $1.5 million flat in Bedstuy, the child of prominent and affluent professionals. She’ll continue to have a comfortable and successful life, regardless of where she went to primary and secondary school, and irrespective of whether or where she goes to college. She was born inches from the home plate and was virtually certain to crawl across one way or another. It’s be healthy for all parties to simply acknowledge this.

[^16]: In addition to the other issues highlighted throughout this essay, there are two other factors that severely limit the ability of mass education to eliminate disparities.

First, the reason education has social mobility value insofar as it is relatively rare. When most people didn’t have high school diplomas, possessing one set you apart and gave you an advantage. Today, when nearly all Americans have high school degrees, lacking one imposes a *cost* but attaining one doesn’t give you any meaningful advantage over most others. If BA degrees become as ubiquitous as high school diplomas, there relative value would likewise plummet. That is, the more people succeed in bringing people up to a certain level of credentialing, the less value that credential actually has. Beyond a certain threshold, mass education for the sake of social mobility becomes a self-defeating project.

A second constraint on the ability of public education to close gaps between elites and the masses is that, in a world where some educational intervention *did* prove to be actually effective — it *did* improve test scores, enhance life prospects, and so on in a dramatic way — this innovation would be adopted by elites too. And in virtue of having more resources and power at their disposal, they’d be able to implement this innovation more widely, rapidly, intensively and effectively than less affluent counterparts (while better managing any risks, tradeoffs or complications associated with these developments). And as a result, innovations that show promise to close inequalities would likely end up exacerbating them instead. It’s virtually impossible to innovate one’s way to equality for this reason. One would have to come up with an educational approach that helps dramatically improve prospects for less advantaged people that could *not* be adapted or implemented by elites. That’s a tall order even at the conceptual level, let alone in practice.
