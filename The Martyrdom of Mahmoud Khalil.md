Until he was detained by ICE, the anti-Israel activist was nothing more than a scoundrel. His new memoir confirms his status as a secular saint for progressives.

For Americans perpetually enraged about Israel, the ordeal of Mahmoud Khalil has become an obsession. On March 8, 2025, this Columbia graduate and Palestinian activist was snatched from his home by federal agents and whisked off to a series of detention centers. Since then he has fought his deportation tooth and nail. Khalil’s lawyers are asking the [Supreme Court](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/70611756/mahmoud-khalil-v-president-united-states-of-america/#minute-entry-478992699) to reverse the deportation order from last year on constitutional grounds.

Khalil first gained notoriety in the aftermath of the October 7, 2023, massacre. In the campus turmoil that followed, he emerged as a spokesman—one of the few who was unmasked—for students at Columbia using the occasion of the Hamas-led slaughter to demand the university cut ties with and divest from the state Hamas attacked, Israel.

Today, he has emerged as a secular saint for New York’s progressive literati. His legal battle to remain in the United States is being fought by a team of more than 20 lawyers representing the Center for Constitutional Rights, the American Civil Liberties Union, and other progressive groups. New York’s socialist mayor, [Zohran Mamdani](https://www.fox5ny.com/news/mamdani-hosts-palestinian-activist-mahmoud-khalil-ramadan-dinner-gracie-mansion), hosted Khalil for a Ramadan dinner in March, paying tribute to his “profound courage.” *[The New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/08/24/how-far-will-the-trump-administration-go-to-deport-mahmoud-khalil)* compared the appearance of his 6-month-old-son, Deen, to “a Rubens portrait of the infant Jesus, if the Flemish had been more realistic about a Nazarene’s complexion.” This month he spoke at a [Yom Kippur rally](https://www.jpost.com/diaspora/antisemitism/article-909235) with anti-Zionist Jews in Brooklyn at a podium draped in a sign reading, “For the sin of genocide, we atone.”

Khalil’s journey from Syria to Lebanon to Columbia and through the federal government’s immigration and deportation system is the grist for his new memoir published this week, *[No Land to Stand On](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9781250462008)*. As one might expect, the blurbs include high praise from the anti-Zionist left. Peter Beinart calls it “a defining document of our age.” Beinart is correct but for different reasons than he imagines.

Khalil’s memoir is a case study in what the British writer [Julie Burchill](https://spectator.com/article/meet-the-cry-bully-a-hideous-hybrid-of-victim-and-victor/?edition=us) has called the age of the “crybully,” when the aggressor plays the vanquished. He asks the reader to sympathize with angry campus mobs that singled out Zionists and sided with terrorists. And at the same time the memoir also tells the tale of a crybully turned into a genuine victim. After his rabble-rousing on campus, Khalil was taken from his pregnant wife and plunged into a maze of detention centers and immigration hearings.

All of that said, Khalil’s central claims about the movement he helped lead are hard to credit. He claims that the pro-Palestinian activists who were allowed to disrupt campus life were the real victims. The Zionist students on the other hand were crying wolf. “When this subset of pro-­Israel students said they felt unsafe, the university issued statements within hours, convened task forces, hired consultants, and reorganized its disciplinary apparatus,” he writes. “When Palestinian students said they felt unsafe, the university paid only lip service to our fears. Both made claims of safety. Only one claim moved the institution.” It’s not surprising that Khalil has joined other alumni and students in a [lawsuit against Columbia](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/14/nyregion/mahmoud-khalil-columbia-lawsuit.html) for endangering students who tried to shut down its campus.

There was an academic intifada in the months that led up to the encampments and building takeovers for Gaza. And Columbia’s administrators allowed this uprising to fester through most of the 2023-2024 academic year. It was not until April 2024, when masked students and outside agitators with zip ties and duct tape stormed Hamilton Hall and [trapped a janitor in the building](https://www.thefp.com/p/exclusive-columbia-custodian-trapped), that Columbia called in the [New York police](https://www.npr.org/2024/04/30/1248012619/columbia-university-gaza-protests) to clear the building and the quads.

Throughout this period, Jewish students endured slurs, threats, and harassment. The university convened a special task force on antisemitism at Columbia which [documented](https://www.columbia.edu/content/sites/www.columbia.edu.content/files/content/about/Task%20Force%20on%20Antisemitism/Report-2-Task-Force-on-Antisemitism.pdf) the environment. “Students have reported having necklaces ripped off their necks and being pinned against walls,” the report said. In dormitories, students found jokes about Hitler on communal whiteboards. Campus extracurricular clubs enacted new policies banning any Zionists. Popular slogans at the encampments included: “Al-Qassam, you make us proud, kill another soldier now”; “Yes, Hamas, we love you, we support your rockets too”; and “We say justice, you say how, burn Tel Aviv to the ground.” This side of the movement at Columbia was captured in the social media posts of undergraduate [Khymani James](https://www.cnn.com/2024/10/09/us/columbia-pro-palestine-group-apology), who said, “Zionists don’t deserve to live.” He initially apologized for his words but then revoked his apology.

It’s true that some pro-Israel students had filmed the encampments and demonstrations. There was at one point a truck that drove by the campus displaying the names and photos of Columbia students that had joined the anti-Israel demonstrations. But the Zionists were severely outnumbered at Columbia. Many faculty were openly supporting the other side of the should-Israel-exist debate. Indeed, the task force report details how some professors would single out Columbia’s Jewish benefactors for giving the school “blood money,” or would teach the Israel-Palestine conflict as a binary contest between oppressor and oppressed.

Khalil, though, would have his readers believe that this movement, which banned students who believed Israel had a right to exist, was a model community of tolerance and freedom. “Students who arrived often knowing little about Palestine sat in reading circles and learned,” he writes. “The movement built mutual aid networks for families in Gaza through bake sales, art sales, and fundraisers held in church basements and student housing.”

Khalil’s problem is that he was the face of and negotiator for a nonviolent movement in support of hyper-violent fanatics.

Khalil’s memoir oozes with chutzpah, a combination of nerve and intended offense. Here is how he describes the first messages from the administration following October 7, 2023: “The emails began immediately. An unceasing cascade of communications from then-­president Minouche Shafik and the deans of every school. Most expressed grief on behalf of Israeli victims, condemned Hamas in unambiguous terms, named the dead only if they were Israeli, shallowly evoked the trauma of Jewish students, and pledged the university’s commitment to Jewish students’ safety.”

Khalil is admitting he is offended that the president of his university “evoked the trauma of Jewish students” after 1,200 Jews were slaughtered by Palestinian terrorists.

Khalil was also mortified that Keren Yarhi-Milo, the dean of Columbia’s School of International and Public Affairs, issued a message to students that claimed the phrase “from the river to the sea” is a call for Jews living in Israel to be eliminated. “This was not the dean offering one possible interpretation,” Khalil writes. “This appeared as the dean asserting, as fact, a dishonest reading of a Palestinian slogan, one that scholars of the region have broadly rejected.”

Khalil claims that the slogan is “a call for freedom, justice, and equality for every person living between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean.” And in English-speaking demonstrations, the full sentence may suggest that, because the following phrase is “Palestine will be free.” The Arabic version of the slogan, though, is quite different. In that one, the last words translate to “Palestine will be Arab,” exactly what Yarhi-Milo interpreted it to mean.

Khalil’s problem is that he was the face of and negotiator for a nonviolent movement in support of hyper-violent fanatics. Remember that the disruptions on campus followed October 7, the worst mass killing of Jews since the Holocaust. Families were burned alive in their safe rooms. Women were violated and mutilated at a peace concert. And the raiders kept more than 200 people prisoner after the slaughter.

Khalil never acknowledges the sheer barbarity of October 7 or, for that matter, of the other campaigns of so-called Palestinian resistance. Instead he bemoans that many outside journalists framed his movement as antisemitic. “The conflation of anti-Zionism with antisemitism was a strategy to stifle righteous criticism of Israel,” he writes. “Such weaponization of antisemitism was designed to place a state and its crimes beyond criticism by wrapping them in the protections owed to a faith and its people.”

Khalil’s formulation in practice, though, posits a distinction without a difference. The anti-Zionism of the movement led the students to blame the Jewish victims of October 7 for the barbarity of Hamas. Whether motivated by enmity for Jews or the Jewish state, the result was a celebration of Jewish bloodshed. Both of Khalil’s organizations—Dar and the Palestine Working Group— [signed onto a statement](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSeHe5p0in9g4h9xURoTx6NPn-v2rb-m7k6ZIm4YNGqOPRVsFQ/viewform) two days after the atrocities that said, “The weight of responsibility for the war and casualties undeniably lies with the Israeli extremist government and other Western governments, including the U.S. government, which fund and staunchly support Israeli aggression, apartheid, and settler-colonization.”

When he was asked whether he supported Hamas, on *New York Times* columnist [Ezra Klein](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/08/05/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-mahmoud-khalil.html) ’s podcast last year, Khalil bobbed and weaved. “If I condemn Hamas, then I am a Palestinian worthy of listening to. If I don’t, then I’m not,” he said. “And this is what gets Palestinians angry with this line of questioning. Because as I said, Palestinians are the ones now being starved and genocided. Because even if Hamas does not exist tomorrow, the Israeli occupation and supremacy would continue against the Palestinians. So it’s not about Hamas.”

Khalil’s umbrage is a smoke screen. The reason Palestinian activists are asked about Hamas is because Hamas bears responsibility for the misery that followed the war it started. It fired rockets and missiles from mosques and schools and hid beneath and between the civilians the group was supposed to defend. Anyone who would justify the acts of these sadists should be pushed outside the margins of respectable discourse.

Given all of that, one might expect that I would favor Khalil’s eventual deportation. But I cannot, for reasons that have nothing to do with his low character and foolish activism. Rather, Khalil should not be deported because the Donald Trump administration’s reasons for deporting him would set a dreadful precedent.

The main justification, as put forth in a March 7, 2025, [memorandum](https://s3.documentcloud.org/documents/25894225/dhs-documents-mahmoud-khalil.pdf) from Secretary of State Marco Rubio, is that Khalil’s presence and activities in America undermine U.S. foreign policy and specifically the government’s policy to combat antisemitism. This derives from the rarely-used Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, a piece of Joseph McCarthy-era legislation that many scholars believe is unconstitutional.

---

“With regards to the law, it’s extremely clear that noncitizens in the U.S. legally have First Amendment rights,” Greg Lukianoff, the president and CEO of the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression, told me. “Even in the unlikely case the Supreme Court found the law facially constitutional, it would still find this use of it for people on student visas to violate the spirit of the original law.”

In other words, the Trump administration effectively seeks to deport Khalil for a speech crime. As offensive, deceptive, and slippery as Khalil’s speech is, foreign students studying in America enjoy the same First Amendment rights as American citizens. This is how it has been for most of U.S. history. If the secretary of state can now pick and choose what foreign students to deport based on their speech, then what is to stop a Democratic administration from targeting foreign students protesting gender ideology?

Later in the process, the U.S. government added the post hoc charge that Khalil failed to mention his internship with the United Nations and his membership in the Columbia University Apartheid Divest coalition. These are more serious infractions, but they also appear pretextual in light of the initial justification for his deportation.

Regardless, Khalil was treated as a criminal. He was never in physical danger, but the monotony of his life in detention was grueling. “Some mornings, I would wake up and lie in bed for hours before I could convince myself to move,” he writes. “What was the point? The day would be the same as yesterday. The same as tomorrow. The clock would tick. The meals would come. The names would be called. And I would still be here, waiting for something that might never arrive.”

Detaining and deporting Khalil will not make a dent against the monoculture at Columbia. The university had already been captured by an ideology that inverts morality and basic decency when it comes to the Jewish state. The rot can be traced back more than a half-century, to the rise of Columbia’s own Professor [Edward Said](https://www.thefp.com/p/the-godfather-of-the-campus-intifada) and his work to enlist the academic study of history and literature into a campaign to replace the State of Israel with Palestine.

Revoking Khalil’s green card because his speech displeased Marco Rubio does nothing to address that problem. Instead, it makes America less free and turns a scoundrel into a martyr.
