### Most of the money in the creative economy goes to corporations and intermediaries. It doesn’t need to work that way.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!D9mj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F42f077d3-6431-476e-83fd-b7a6e48f7d70_2400x800.jpeg)

Long before Apple Music, there was another kind of Apple music.

In January 1968, the four Beatles decided to create their own co-operative music business under the name Apple Corps. (The ‘p’ is silent, by the way, making it a play on words.) The move was a *no-brainer*, as we’d call it today.

As captains of their own ship (or let’s call it a Yellow Submarine), the Fab Four could set up their own recording studio, make music on their own terms, without any corporate interference, and sell albums and singles directly to fans.

Not only would they help themselves, but they would also support other deserving creators. The goal was a totally new model for the music business, with all decisions made by artists for the benefit of artists.

At a press conference, the Beatles laid out the scope of their ambitions—and it’s extraordinary how much their description resembles a different Apple company, the one later launched by Steve Jobs.

“It’s a business concerning records, films, and electronics,” explains John Lennon—and hence the whole notion of Apple devices came out of Liverpool, not Cupertino. Then Lennon continues, painting an inspiring picture of 1960s rebellion and business disruption:

> We want to set up a system whereby people who just want to make a film about anything don’t have to go on their knees in somebody’s office—probably yours….
> 
> All we have to do now is set up it and get it on its feet. And then what follows will be an actual production….

It’s hard to figure out what John is describing here. But he realizes that too, and admits as much in response to a question:

> We decided not to be very specific, because I don’t know what I’m doing, do I?

Paul isn’t any clearer, claiming that if they “give it away” it will pay for itself. At times, their business model sounds like YouTube—people making videos without permission from gatekeepers, and giving them away for free. But how could the Beatles do that before the Internet?

Then Paul gets crazier, insisting that they don’t even need to worry about profits:

> If you come and see me and say ‘I’ve had such and such a dream,’ I’ll say ‘Here’s so much money. Go away and do it.’”

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lC5bUE2d_c0)

It’s easy to laugh at the utopian views expressed here. But the Beatles clearly wanted to open up their cooperative arrangement to everybody—”people who just want to make a film about anything.”

In all fairness, there’s an actual economic justification for this kind of initiative—middlemen and gatekeepers keep most of the money made by music for themselves. That’s why former Spotify CEO Daniel Ek is richer than Paul McCartney. In fact, he has accumulated more wealth than any musician in history. That doesn’t seem fair. The people who make the songs deserve a larger cut than the provider of a crappy user interface—one that could easily be replicated by an artists cooperative.

That’s precisely the kind of artists cooperative I’ve been hoping for in our own time—something open-ended, that allows for everybody to participate. We’ve never really had that in music. Even ASCAP and the musicians union were launched with strictures to prevent too many people from joining.

For a while, the Beatles provided a breath of fresh air with Apple Corps. They signed a wide range of deserving artists—recording everyone from Ravi Shankar to the Modern Jazz Quartet. The label also helped launch new acts, including James Taylor and Badfinger. As Lennon promised, Apple also took chances in filmmaking—releasing, for example, the psychedelic Beatles TV movie *Magical Mystery Tour*.

I’m tempted to say, at this point: *But it couldn’t last.*

Yet the reality is that it did last. Apple Corps still exists today, owned by the surviving Beatles and the estates of the departed band members. But the energy behind the company collapsed after the band broke up. In the aftermath, Apple canceled all its visionary projects for transforming culture, and exists today as a manager of intellectual property.

There’s a long history of artist cooperatives falling apart. So it’s likely that, even under better circumstances, Apple Corps would have eventually turned sour.

Consider the case of United Artists. This movie studio really was, at its inception, a coalition of united artists. Back in 1919, four of the most famous celebrities in Hollywood decided to launch their own studio. The instigators were Charlie Chaplin, D. W. Griffith, Mary Pickford and Douglas Fairbanks, and (like the Beatles) they had dreams of reinventing the entertainment business.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2xob!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fee983b3b-c98b-43f3-953e-f13f46cecac2_1305x1000.jpeg)

The founders of United Artists: Douglas Fairbanks, Mary Pickford, Charlie Chaplin and D.W. Griffith.

It fell apart, for all the usual reasons—personal disagreements, cash flow problems, litigation, poor management, and the rest. But like Apple Corps, United Artists never disappeared. Amazon owns it today,

That’s the typical fate of artist-run businesses. They eventually sell out to the system.

The launch of the Tidal streaming platform in 2015 was United Artists all over again. But if anything, the cast of participants was even more powerful this time. At the launch event, some of “owners” were introduced—they included Jay-Z, Beyoncé, Madonna, Rihanna, Kanye West, Nicki Minaj, Daft Punk, Alicia Keys, Usher, and Jack White, among others.

But the main owner of Tidal today is Jack Dorsey’s company Stripe, which acquired majority control in 2021. Like the other companies profiled here, this streaming platform will probably survive, but never deliver on its inspiring dream of artists controlling their own destiny—and cash flows!

Does this mean that an artist co-op will never triumph over Spotify and the other entrenched tech interests in music? I don’t reach that conclusion. I believe Tidal could have won out, if the artists behind it had agreed to give it exclusivity for new releases—and especially if it had enlisted a few more superstars (for example, Taylor Swift).

The music business is built entirely on access to songs. At the end of the day, the artists decide who gets that access. If they play hardball, they can tilt the balance in their favor.

Creating the software to stream music via apps is the easy part. Spotify’s real power is its network of subscribers. But they are only there for the songs. If the songs go elsewhere, fans will have to move too.

Spotify realizes this, of course. That’s why the company works so hard to promote passive listening. The less listeners care about the specific artists, the more power Spotify has over the industry.

That’s the number one reason why music feels so stagnant right now. The most powerful company in music wants it that way—and thus encourages passive listening and a world in which songs are generic content.

If you love music, you should hope for a day when other values are dominant in the culture—innovation, vitality, excitement, creativity, etc. But don’t expect tech platforms to play any role in that. They want business-as-usual, not cultural rebirth.

Our best hope is for a more ambitious artists cooperative built on principles that are truly scalable. The co-op needs to make it easy for every musician to join, and offer fair, transparent terms for all participants. To encourage solidarity and long term thinking, royalty payments ought to include shares in the co-op—so that musicians share in each others’ success.

Record labels would participate too, if they’re smart. That’s because Spotify has hurt them already, and will do more damage over the long run. I don’t shed many tears over this. The labels helped launch Spotify, and are thus responsible for this huge shift in cash from creators to streamers. But in the current moment, labels would do better if they worked cooperatively with musicians in taking power back from tech platforms.

I doubt this will happen anytime soon. But sometimes transformative change can occur unexpectedly—and simply because a few smart people had a bold vision. Ten years ago, nobody even dreamed of a company like Substack, an enterprise that gives writers 90% of revenues and thus manages to attract many of the leading creators in media and publishing. And who anticipated the rise of Bandcamp or Patreon or other pro-artist platforms of that sort?

We really can do something like this in music. All the problems that the Beatles identified long ago are still out there, but today we have technologies that can help us solve them. We really can empower creators AND give music away for free (or almost) AND remove middlemen AND get paid fairly.

The business model works. The tech tools exist. The culture wants and needs it.

It just takes vision, leadership, and a little patience. A good time to start is right now.
