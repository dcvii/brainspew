## Who’s Lying and Why?

Naomi Kanakia is the creator behind the “Woman of Letters” publication on Substack and the writer behind the lightly viral “ [Ross Barkan got screwed](https://www.woman-of-letters.com/p/ross-barkan-vs-the-machine) “ article from August 25th. She details a personal friendship with Barkan, crafts a ridiculous narrative about how his plagiarism being exposed was a right-wing hit job, and misrepresents some of the sources she cited to write her own piece—most notably, she cites the Columbia Journalism Review. Let’s check that, shall we?

Kanakia’s own words, from the third paragraph in the section entitled “ **What does Naomi think?**“:

> “while Ross might’ve violated these standards, it was an error made in innocence, and it does not necessarily constitute plagiarism. That’s what the Columbia Journalism Review [said](https://www.cjr.org/laurels-and-darts/abortion-coverage-built-trust-jessica-valenti-kylie-cheung-ross-barkan-plagiarism-data-center.php) back in May…“

Kanakia then misleadingly quotes the linked CJR article, written by Susie Banikarim, out of context:

> “The question is whether this is plagiarism or something more like sloppy attribution or aggregation. Barkan seems genuinely confused about the distinction. In the Shapiro piece, he did link to Harwell’s story in the following paragraph—presumably not something he would do if he were trying to be deceptive about his source material.“

But what does the CJR actually say *in context?* (Emphasis mine)

> “The question is whether this is plagiarism or something more like sloppy attribution or aggregation. Barkan seems genuinely confused about the distinction. In the Shapiro piece, he did link to Harwell’s story in the following paragraph—presumably not something he would do if he were trying to be deceptive about his source material. ***But confusion isn’t really a defense in journalism.*** **If you are going to use another writer’s prose—not a stray fact or publicly known information, but a sequence of details arranged in the same structure and pattern—that requires a quote or a clear attribution, not a hyperlink.**“

Why lie? Why mislead? Why do it in such a blatant and dishonest fashion? Only Kanakia can know the answer to that question, but the errie similarity to Barkan’s own plagiarism, and then his initially dismissal attitude when exposed, is quite something.

I won’t spend unnecessary time rehashing what’s wrong with “Ross Barkan got screwed“ from a purely journalistic standpoint. Read it, and compare with my Monday article “ **[Barkan’s Writing Ethics Problem, By the Numbers](https://edokwin.substack.com/p/barkans-writing-ethics-problem-by)** “ for yourself. Suffice to say, I think these people believe you’re all stupid, because they don’t even cover their tracks very well. Which brings us to “Zona Motel” and Jarett Kobek…

Ross Barkan had been accused of plagiarism while writing freelance columns for *New York* magazine’s website. … Like any ghoul granted a sip of the sweet stuff, I wanted more blood. I kept waiting for someone to tell me what Barkan had done. No one did. It was 2026.“

“No one” told Kobek or his collaborates at Zona “what Barkan had done“? Bullshit. Complete and utter. I’ve encountered this JAQ-off tactic in discussions of Barkan’s scandal before. ### Weaponizing Privacy of Process

I freely granted in my own piece that the internal investigations by NYMag, Gothamist, et al were not fully transparent. I said this for completeness, not to cast aspersions on any institution’s credibility. In fact, I’d been in conversation with journalists over the week before release, explaining that I would take pains not to through NYMag or anyone else under the bus. But that bus throwing is exactly the move Kanakia and Kobek insist on pulling. It’s lazy, dishonest, immature, and conspiratorial.

No one is responsible for Barkan’s plagiarism except Barkan. He is a grown adult. He committed transgressions that other people go their entire careers never engaging in, not even ”by accident.” And he committed them at least 67 times at one publicayion alone, with another two or more more pieces implicated at another publication, and who knows how many elsewhere throughout the net. Stop sloughing off blame.

The fact is, processes like these are private. And they often stay so, for good and bad reasons. On the good side, people have a right to some measure of professional tact and discretion, even as contractors, even when summarily pronounced as, in effect, damaged goods. NYMag did not want to violate Barkan’s privacy or destroy him. Nor “screw” him, contra Kanakia. They wanted to conduct a fair review, find the truth that other journalists at NPR and elsewhere did, or find proof exonerating him, if possible.

Once the review completed, they rendered their verdict and cut ties. They saw no need to excoriate him or to be especially detailed in their accounting of what they found. Perhaps especially because it only corroborated what others had already uncovered. If Kobek thinks they did a bad job, he makes a lousy argument for why. If he *doesn’t* think the verdict was substantially in error, why publish the piece and keep soft-pedaling, or even sometimes outright saying, the idea that Barkan was somehow done dirty? Again, Kobek wants to have it both ways. Just asking questions. As if.

### “Everybody Does It” — Hit Dogs Hollerin’!

A big contention throughout this saga has been the dubious notion that Barkan did something which is routine, and thus is a victim of, at most, selective outrage over commonplace practices and standard “errors.” Kobek spends paragraphs leaning into that very narrative, and impugns the journalism of other writers to make his case (even as he claims he’s not). Including one of Barkan’s plagiarism victims!

> “And then, as mentioned earlier, NPR’s Bobby Allyn [tweeted](https://x.com/BobbyAllyn/status/2055384515733061668) that he’d … discovered similarities between the 24 September column with a [February 2025 article](https://theintercept.com/2025/02/18/oracle-tiktok-israel-palestine-gaza/) written by Georgia Gee and published in *The Intercept.*

So Gee was plagiarized, and Sobek’s next move with that in mind is to say…she did the same thing as Barkan in her own writing. Unbelievable.

> *New York* found fault in Barkan’s textually unattributed compression and borrowing of language from Gee’s article and, to correct the issue, quoted Gee offering textually unattributed compression of other articles while borrowing their language.
> 
> I offer this with no disrespect to Georgia Gee. I am not accusing her of a single thing. I find her to be a *very good* reporter.
> 
> But when an institution corrects someone, it’s *weird* if that institution inserts a blockquote of someone else doing a remarkably similar thing to the original offense.“

It’s a cute trick he’s trying to pull, but it’s false. The specific accusation against Barkan regarding this Gee overlap wasn't that he paraphrased instead of quoting, it's that Susie Banikarim at CJR directly checked Barkan's claim that Barkan linked to The Intercept in the passage in question and found there is, in fact, no hyperlink. The absence of any hyperlink at all is something that Gee did, every time, in all three passages Kobek quotes as his "gotcha."

Furthermore, Gee's three examples are compressing already-public facts (quotes an Israeli outlet, Haaretz, and Times of Israel had already published), while the actual complaint against Barkan concerns compression of Gee's own *original investigative reporting* (the Oracle employee interviews, the internal Slack messages she obtained). Aggregating public record with a link is a different journalistic act than compressing someone's exclusive reporting. Kobek tries to equivocate on this. Don’t fall for it.

Kobek blathers on, in his forced, ironic style:

It’s almost as if he’s the *very* *best* writer at the *Times*.

Which might themselves mirror language from other articles. (I stopped wandering the labyrinth after I established that Gee’s language mirrors her linked sources.)“

## Barkan’s “Talent” Demands Exemption?

“Barkan is talented enough that he produced pretty clean copy. So you can see it,“ Kobek says. I will firstly say I have read a non-exhaustive but highly representative sampling of Barkan’s prose, across fiction and nonfiction.
