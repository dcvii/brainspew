Back in 2024 I published an article headlined “ [Why Is The Same Misleading Language About Youth Gender Medicine Copied And Pasted Into Dozens Of CNN.com Articles?](https://jessesingal.substack.com/p/why-is-the-same-misleading-language)” The sentence in question was “Gender-affirming care is medically necessary, evidence-based care that uses a multidisciplinary approach to help a person transition from their assigned gender — the one the person was designated at birth — to their affirmed gender — the gender by which one wants to be known.”

As I wrote:

> But of course, whether youth gender medicine is medically necessary and evidence-based is exactly the thing being debated, and anyone who has been following this debate closely knows that every national health system that has examined this question closely, including the NHS, has come to the same conclusion: the evidence is paltry. That’s why so many countries, including Sweden, Finland, the UK, and Norway, have significantly scaled back access to these treatments for youth. So it’s very strange to see this sentence, which reads as though it comes from an activist press release, published in a news article in CNN, an outlet that generally adheres to the old-school divide between news and opinion. \[footnote omitted\]

That article included a list of 35 instances in which that sentence, or very close variants of it, had appeared in CNN.com articles between May 2022 and March 2024. I reached out to CNN but the network gave me no substantive comment — someone there told me on background that it’s standard for outlets to provide reporters with guidance about the appropriate language to use, but this didn’t seem like a credible explanation. Sure, outlets have style guides when it comes to “illegal alien” versus “undocumented immigrant,” or “biological sex” versus “sex assigned at birth,” but come on — this was clearly different:

> You do *not* generally see the same complex sentence pasted over and over and over into news stories written by different authors and published in different sections. I asked CNN if it could provide me any other examples of CNN.com publishing the same sentence in multiple stories by different authors, and posed the same question in an email to Virginia Moseley, the CNN executive editor who, according to the website, “oversee\[s\] international and domestic news operations across platforms.” I didn’t hear back about this.

One thing I didn’t do when I published that first piece, but which I should have done, was confidently lock down the *very first* instance of this sentence appearing on CNN. Last week I finally did, and... this looks really bad for CNN. Whether or not you want to call it “plagiarism” will probably depend on how charitable you’re feeling, but either way it reflects genuinely sloppy editorial practices. It’s also a cringeworthy example of how mainstream media outlets have let themselves be steamrolled by activists on this issue.

The very first time the sentence appeared on CNN was in an [April 21, 2022 primer on youth gender medicine](https://www.cnn.com/2022/04/21/health/gender-affirming-care) published by reporter Jen Christensen pegged to the rising tide of state laws banning the practice. In her piece, Christensen wrote — well, you probably know the sentence by now. Even by the standards of puff pieces, it’s a puff piece, effectively a 2,000-word advertorial for youth gender medicine and the doctors who provide it, complete with lengthy quotes from those doctors about how super careful they are and about how little genuine controversy there is in their field.

Anyway, what matters more for our purposes is that the first part of the sentence — “Gender-affirming care is medically necessary, evidence-based care that... ” actually comes from elsewhere.

On June 15, 2021, [the American Medical Association published a press release](https://www.ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/ama-reinforces-opposition-restrictions-transgender-medical-care) announcing that in response to the growing number of state laws banning youth gender medicine that were being proposed or passed, “physicians and medical students at the AMA’s House of Delegates meeting voted to meaningfully expand the organization’s strong opposition” to such measures.

Here’s the third paragraph in the press release, bolding mine:

> “The AMA opposes the dangerous intrusion of government into the practice of medicine and the criminalization of health care decision-making,” said AMA Board Member Michael Suk, MD, JD, MPH, MBA. “ **Gender-affirming care is medically-necessary, evidence-based care that** improves the physical and mental health of transgender and gender-diverse people.”

Dr. Suk might have an astonishing number of letters after his name, but in this context he is clearly engaging in advocacy. He’s expressing an *opinion* on a hot-button issue, and an ill-informed one, at that (at least in my humble opinion, as someone whose name is trailed by only 30% as many letters as Dr. Suk’s). There’s nothing wrong with printing his quote, of course, or reporting that the AMA’s House of Delegates passed this measure, but that’s not what Christensen did: Rather, she lifted part of his advocacy quote, removed the quotes from it, and presented it as her own voice. While it’s impossible to know for sure — CNN did not respond to a pair of emails I sent to Moseley and two of the network’s comms higher-ups — if I had to guess, Christensen probably didn’t do this on purpose. Or at least my money would be on a mix of sloppiness and ideological certainty, not malice. But still, it’s not good!

What’s even more not-good is what happened next: This advocacy language, disguised as CNN’s considered news judgment, propagated across dozens of articles spanning years of coverage. I’m not blaming these reporters for this or accusing them of anything — my point is that CNN put them in a difficult situation by inserting arguably plagiarized language into their writing via some sort of house policy of disseminating the rosiest imaginable gloss on youth gender medicine. The reporters don’t deserve scorn or scrutiny for this, but CNN itself certainly does.

I suppose there’s a chance Christensen, too, was victimized by an editor insisting she include this language. What makes this a bit less likely-seeming is the fact that her original article actually includes other examples of her lifting language almost verbatim from professional guidance about youth gender medicine without proper attribution.

The one that stood out most to me, as a guy deep in the mire of [writing a book about all this](https://jessesingal.substack.com/p/im-writing-another-book), was when Christensen wrote that:

> Some critics of \[youth gender medicine\] suggest that children should wait until adulthood to transition, but the American Academy of Pediatrics says this is an outdated approach. It assumes that gender identity is fixed at a certain age, but research shows that it’s healthier to value a child for who they are rather than for what they will become.

This is all pulled from a [very sloppy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011888/) and controversial 2018 AAP document known colloquially as [the Rafferty statement](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/142/4/e20182162/37381/Ensuring-Comprehensive-Care-and-Support-for) after its lead author, Jason Rafferty, which Christensen links to elsewhere in her piece.

Now, she mangles the details here — the section in question criticizes the [Dutch approach](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22455322/) to childhood gender dysphoria, specifically its ‘watchful waiting’ recommendation, which does *not* call for kids to wait until adulthood before they transition. In any case, Christensen stitches together her own summary of the AAP’s stance from verbatim or lightly altered language straight from the Rafferty statement.

Here’s the relevant part of that latter document, bolding mine:

> This **outdated approach** does not serve the child because critical support is withheld. Watchful waiting is based on binary notions of gender in which gender diversity and fluidity is pathologized; in watchful waiting, it is also **assumed that** notions of **gender identity** become **fixed at a certain age**. The approach is also influenced by a group of early studies with validity concerns, methodologic flaws, and limited follow-up on children who identified as TGD and, by adolescence, did not seek further treatment (“desisters”). More robust and current **research suggests** \[Christensen actually upgrades this to ‘shows’!\] **that, rather than focusing on who a child will become, valuing them for who they are**, even at a young age, fosters secure attachment and resilience, not only for the child but also for the whole family.

There’s also this (again, bolding mine):

Christensen: **“** [In 2015](https://www.ustranssurvey.org/reports), **1 in 4 adults who identified as transgender avoided a** needed **doctor’s visit because** they **fear** ed **being mistreated**, according to the [US Transgender Survey](https://transequality.org/sites/default/files/docs/usts/USTS-Full-Report-Dec17.pdf) from the National Center for Transgender Equality.”

Rafferty statement: **“** In the past year, **1 in 4 adults who identified as transgender avoided a** necessary **doctor’s visit because** of fear of **being mistreated**,” with a citation pointing to the USTS. (Note that in her sentence cribbing the Rafferty statement, neither of her links is to that statement.)

Plagiarism is a [spectrum](https://academic-integrity.uregina.ca/?page_id=59) and these are perhaps closer to misdemeanors than felonies. But still: That’s a lot of very specific language to have lifted; there’s obviously not enough daylight between Christensen and the organizations she is so enthusiastically deferring to.

Which has kind of been a problem for years now.
