Is Wall Street right to be celebrating the Nvidia CEO’s return? Joe Nocera investigates. Plus: Aaron MacLean asks whether the move will make America less safe.

***Earlier this morning, Nvidia’s board unanimously reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO—an extraordinary turn of events just 11 days after Huang resigned amid revelations of a series of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman suspected by U.S. intelligence of being a Chinese spy. President Trump claimed credit for the comeback. “I believe my intervention was very important,” he said today.***

***The decision has enormous implications for the AI revolution, the global economy, and U.S. national security. For a closer look at the national security stakes, read [Aaron MacLean’s piece](https://www.thefp.com/p/will-jensen-huangs-return-make-america-nvidia-ai-business-chips) on whether Huang’s reinstatement will make America less safe. And scroll down for Joe Nocera, who takes on the market euphoria surrounding Huang’s return—and explains why he thinks it will be short-lived. —The Editors***

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lUBU!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F041bce28-ab09-4290-bb95-ee5d8cccd35e_1320x30.png)

So now we know: Jensen Huang really is too big to fail.

This morning, just 11 days after Huang resigned his position as the CEO of Nvidia, the company’s board of directors reinstated him. It was a stunning turnabout—indeed, just last Tuesday, during the company’s third-quarter earnings call, board chairman Harvey Jones [had said](https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/nvidia-chair-harvey-jones-jensen-huang-comeback-earnings-call-analysts-china-singapore/) that the company “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO.” Tech investors were ecstatic about the sudden reversal, and they proved it by driving up [Nvidia’s stock](https://www.google.com/search?q=nvidia+stock&oq=nvidia+stock&gs_lcrp=EgZjaHJvbWUqBggAEEUYOzIGCAAQRRg7MhIIARAAGEMYgwEYsQMYgAQYigUyDQgCEAAYgwEYsQMYgAQyDQgDEAAYgwEYsQMYgAQyDQgEEAAYgwEYsQMYgAQyDQgFEAAYgwEYsQMYgAQyDQgGEAAYgwEYsQMYgAQyDQgHEAAYgwEYsQMYgAQyEggIEAAYQxiDARixAxiABBiKBTIQCAkQABiDARixAxiABBiKBdIBCDIzMjBqMGo5qAIAsAIB&sourceid=chrome&ie=UTF-8) by 30 percent soon after the opening bell.

The tech bros, meanwhile, who had been pushing hard for Huang’s return were equally pleased. “The mistake has been corrected,” Elon Musk [posted on X](https://x.com/elonmusk/status/2124875939133751684). Brad Gerstner of Altimeter Capital, a tech hedge fund that had been leading the charge to bring Huang back, wrote one word on X when he learned the news: “[unanimous](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/smart-people-saying-jensens-resurrection-164306786.html),” referring to the board’s vote. President Donald Trump went on CNBC and essentially took credit for bringing Huang back, claiming he had been in touch with the board as it was making its decision. “We have to protect our geniuses,” [he said](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/cnbc-transcript-president-donald-trump-speaks-with-cnbcs-eamon-javers-and-squawk-on-the-street-today.html). “We don’t have enough of them.” (He later posted [an AI image](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/23/trump-jensen-huang-nvidia-sex-sells-meme) on Truth Social of Huang in Trump’s famous pose after the Butler, Pennsylvania assassination attempt, holding a red bra in his raised fist.)

Nvidia’s importance—to the stock market, to the AI build-out, and for that matter, to the U.S. economy—was reflected in the broader market. The tech-heavy Nasdaq was up more than 7 percent by noon. The S&P 500 index was up by nearly 6 percent. CoreWeave, a deeply indebted company that builds data centers—and is largely reliant on funding from Nvidia—was up 22 percent. Another so-called “neocloud,” Nebius, was up 24 percent. Indeed, the only tech stock that wasn’t drafting off Nvidia today was Anthropic, which has been largely flat.

The reason for all the cheering, [as I’ve noted before](https://www.thefp.com/p/artificial-intelligence-bubble-finance-perfect-storm), is that Huang is seen as someone who is not only indispensable to Nvidia and the AI universe but the entire U.S. economy. His company makes the chips that drive the AI revolution. And a big chunk of the billions that Nvidia earns from selling those chips get recirculated as debt to the neoclouds who can then buy yet more Nvidia chips for the data centers they are building. The clear impression one gets from the excited reaction to Huang’s return is that tech investors on both Wall Street and in Silicon Valley are convinced that all will go back to the way it was, with Jensen Huang serving once again as the man throwing hot coals into a raging fire. Except, of course, this time he won’t be sleeping with his employees—or Alice Huang, the alleged Chinese intelligence asset he had an affair with. Not that they would particularly care if he was.

But today’s elation will likely prove to be short-lived, for a number of reasons. First, during the brief time Huang was gone, the media began to dig into how the AI boom was being financed—a subject that had previously been the purview of a small number of dissenting voices like Michael Burry and Steve Eisman, both of whom are [on record](https://www.thefp.com/p/will-data-centers-be-the-next-big-finance-business) comparing some of the AI financing tactics to Enron. The *[Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/9b28ed72-aac4-4291-9734-bf60b66645ab?syn-25a6b1a6=1)* [noted](https://www.ft.com/content/9b28ed72-aac4-4291-9734-bf60b66645ab?syn-25a6b1a6=1) that Nvidia is being investigated by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission for possible accounting fraud. It also wrote a series of stories about a [neocloud called Nscale](https://www.ft.com/content/35cc5cc6-f9b6-49cc-8fc2-bd61e09c8c40?syn-25a6b1a6=1), which lost a contract it had with Microsoft and its [sudden need for financing](https://www.ft.com/content/8d5a8642-d0a1-458d-aaa4-c1e7d344fe47?syn-25a6b1a6=1) to fulfill other data center contracts.

Some analysts believe that up to 20 percent of Nvidia’s revenue comes from China; losing that revenue stream would be a big deal for Nvidia’s balance sheet.

*The* *Wall Street Journal* wrote an in-depth story about how Nvidia chips were being [smuggled into China](https://www.wsj.com/podcasts/the-journal/how-nvidia-built-a-backdoor-to-china/4453ea12-dd3b-426b-bf4b-88b5311070a8?mod=Searchresults&pos=18&page=1). *The* *New York Times* reported that Nvidia’s chips [were being routed](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/18/technology/nvidia-alibaba-china-military.html?searchResultPosition=2) from Alice Huang’s company, Megaspeed, to Alibaba, which, the *Times* noted, has an “AI lab with strong ties to China’s military, where researchers have worked on tasks such as automating cyberattacks and simulating war games.”

That scrutiny will surely continue. Once reporters get a sniff of scandal, they can’t stop. And once the Democrats take control of Congress, they are going to conduct their own investigations, demanding to know whether, in sleeping with Alice Huang and selling chips to her company, Jensen Huang was endangering national security.

Trump [told CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/cnbc-transcript-president-donald-trump-speaks-with-cnbcs-eamon-javers-and-squawk-on-the-street-today.html) this morning that his administration had done “a pretty good investigation,” which had exonerated Huang. Losing Jensen, he added, “would be a far bigger threat” to national security. Indeed, he claimed that Huang might take a job in China if he hadn’t returned to Nvidia. In [its statement](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-president-ceo-and-board-director) reinstating Huang, the company’s board said that “in the process of reaching its decision, the Board received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk.”

I can assure you: Democrats will not accept this at face value. Nor will all Republicans. Nebraska senator Pete Ricketts said today that Huang’s reinstatement “doesn’t excuse” him from testifying before Congress. “It also doesn’t absolve Nvidia of answering for national security concerns and unaccounted-for chips. I look forward to Mr. Huang coming to the Senate in December,” [he posted on X.](https://x.com/SenatorRicketts/status/2124919958836781211)

It seems obvious that Huang wasn’t gone long enough for there to be a proper investigation. And in addition to lawmakers wanting to know more about what Huang knew about how Nvidia’s chips wound up in China, there is also California attorney general Rob Bonta. Sure enough, late last week, he issued an investigative subpoena, saying in [a statement](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-serves-investigative-subpoena-nvidia) that “The conduct of Nvidia and its former CEO raises bright red flags.” On CNBC, Jim Cramer said, “I don’t think we’re going to have anything major after this,” referring to new disclosures. I wouldn’t be so sure of that.

---

A second reason it’s not going to be business as usual is that some of Nvidia’s business practices will undoubtedly have to change. On CNBC, David Faber noted that Huang’s agreements with the neoclouds and others were often “handshake deals. Will he continue to be able to make these deals?” People are going to be paying close attention to which companies Nvidia sells its chips to—especially in Singapore, Malaysia, and other countries with data centers that were allegedly used by the Chinese. [Some analysts believe](https://finance.biggo.com/podcast/2895829a16cb370e) that up to 20 percent of Nvidia’s revenue comes from China; losing that revenue stream would be a big deal for Nvidia’s balance sheet.

The final reason things are not going to go back to the way they were is because the world is not going to go back to the way it was. The AI bubble has gotten its first real puncture. The immense risk posed by the debt AI companies have taken on is finally the focus of many investors. “The math just doesn’t work,” said Harris Kupperman, who runs the hedge fund Praetorian Capital. He was referring to the difference between the amount of debt AI companies are using to build the AI infrastructure, and the amount of revenue that infrastructure is likely to generate. He added, “Once people start asking questions, they can’t stop asking them.”

They have a phrase for days like this in the stock market: a dead cat bounce—a brief upswing when a market is in the midst of a downturn. Even with today’s big move, the Nasdaq is still down nearly 10 percent over the last six months, while the S&P 500 index is down almost 4 percent. Can Jensen Huang turn a dead cat bounce into a revival of the AI boom? That’s what Silicon Valley and tech investors are hoping for. But I wouldn’t count on it.
