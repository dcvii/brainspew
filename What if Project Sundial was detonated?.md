In 1954 a US weapons lab started work on a bomb of 10,000 megatons. It was never built. This is what one would do.  
  
Project Sundial was real. In July 1954, with the hydrogen bomb only twenty months old, Edward Teller told a secret meeting about "the possibility of much bigger bangs". His lab was working on two designs: Gnomon, at 1,000 megatons, whose only job was to set off Sundial, at 10,000 megatons. That is 670,000 Hiroshimas in one bomb. The scientists in the room were shocked, and most of the file is still secret.  
  
So we built the vault ourselves and pressed the button. Watch the fireball grow to 50 kilometres wide, the desert turn to glass, the cloud punch out of the atmosphere, and a city 105 kilometres away meet the light, the ground wave and the shock front. Then follow it around the planet: a pressure wave that circles Earth for over a week, harbours flooding on every ocean, fallout across a continent, and a sky that stays dim for years.  
  
Honest maths: the vault, the island and the bomb's shape are fiction, because the real design never left the drawing board. Every number on screen is scaled from real test data and published studies, and those scaling laws were never meant to go this far. Where our numbers are lower than ones you may have seen, we say why: the earthquake comes out near magnitude 7, not 9, because most of the energy goes into the air, and the cooling is 2 to 5 degrees, because a desert has little to burn. The city is not a real place and nobody is in it.
