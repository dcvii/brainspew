The idea that vaccines cause autism rose to prominence after [a fraudulent 1998 paper in](https://web.archive.org/web/20260912043656/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakefield_MMR-autism_fraud) *[The Lancet](https://web.archive.org/web/20260912043656/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakefield_MMR-autism_fraud)* argued that the combination measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine caused a fake condition dubbed “autistic enterocolitis”. The paper was eventually retracted, but the damage was done; public discourse had been seeded with the notion that vaccines might cause autism. Various poor arguments making the case followed and, nowadays, [around 10% of the U.S. population seems to believe that vaccination leads to autism](https://web.archive.org/web/20261009125103/https://www.medpagetoday.com/infectiousdisease/vaccines/123258).

There’s ample evidence that makes it nearly certain that vaccines *do not* cause autism, but the literature on this subject is actually surprisingly limited in terms of how much of it comes from causally informative studies. Most of the extant evidence has to do with mechanistic reasoning, cohort studies, and international comparisons.[^1] Though these are convincing, they’re not enough for certainty. To that end, I’ve compiled a wealth of causal evidence that you can access at this website: [vaccineeffects.com](https://www.vaccineeffects.com/).

---

The way my method works is simple. Different jurisdictions implemented vaccine mandates and rollouts at different times. The first affected cohorts should show jumps in autism rates that remain elevated above the preexisting trend afterwards. Combined with data on actual uptake, these jumps allow us to estimate the extent to which autism rates rose as a result of vaccines being given out, because earlier cohorts won’t have been exposed to nearly the same degree. For those unfamiliar with risk ratios, a ratio of 1 means no difference between groups. Numbers above 1 denote positive effects—increases—in the treated group and numbers below 1 indicate negative effects—reductions. When the confidence interval goes over 1, that means the effect is not statistically significant (i.e., the *p* -value is greater than 0.05 in the case of 95% confidence intervals). As you can see, the effect of vaccination is not statistically significant or even anywhere near being significant: the central estimate is 0.97—a *reduction* in autism, if anything—and the 95% confidence interval ranges from 0.84 to 1.13, ruling out even modest effects in either direction. Or, in other words, strong, causal evidence now shows: **vaccines do not cause autism**.[^2]
