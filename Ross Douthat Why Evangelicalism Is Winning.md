Of all the predictions Alexis de Tocqueville made while surveying the youthful United States, one of the boldest concerned religion. Notwithstanding Protestantism’s apparent American dominance, the Frenchman prophesied that Roman Catholicism would be the long-term victor in a democratic age. “Our posterity will tend more and more to a single division into two parts,” he wrote in *Democracy in America*, with “some relinquishing Christianity entirely, and others returning to the bosom of the Church of Rome.”

The last few years have given that prediction new life. From the spate of traditionalist-inclined [college converts](https://www.thefp.com/p/meet-americas-newest-catholics) to the heretofore secular humanists who have thrilled to Pope Leo XIV’s [critique of artificial intelligence](https://www.thefp.com/p/magnifica-humanitas-ai-pope-leo), it often feels like Catholicism is the strongest religious alternative to post-Christian tribalism, nihilism, and transhumanism. Writing for the Catholic magazine *Commonweal*, Wesleyan University’s Daniel Steinmetz-Jenkins, himself a non-Catholic, envisions “ [a Catholic century](https://www.commonwealmagazine.org/steinmetz-jenkins-catholic-century),” in which the church’s thought becomes a “default option” for people seeking the common good amid liberalism’s crisis and AI-driven dehumanization.

As a Catholic I have a similar reading of the intellectual situation. But it’s also essential to look beyond the intelligentsia to assess the health and future of religion. And in the wider culture, the form of Christianity that’s most adapted to late-modern America is clearly not the Church of Rome. It’s evangelical Protestantism. The vibes of a Catholic moment are real enough, but evangelicalism’s resilience is our era’s more fundamental fact.

You can see that resilience across almost every means of measurement: conversion rates, retention rates, identification rates, church attendance, intensity of belief. [A recent overview](https://www.graphsaboutreligion.com/p/are-evangelicals-holding-the-line) from Ryan Burge, the religion data analyst, showed that evangelicals have maintained a roughly steady share of the U.S. population since the 1970s, while both Catholicism and the so-called “mainline” denominations (Congregationalist, Episcopalian, Presbyterian, and so on) that once dominated American Christianity have seen marked decline. In the 2020s, evangelicals are more than twice as likely to attend church weekly as Catholics. They are also twice as likely to describe religious faith as “very important” in their lives.

One could defend Catholicism’s performance here by noting that Catholic self-identification is strongly bound up with ethnic and cultural inheritance as well as actual belief. By contrast, the “evangelical” label self-selects for intensity and religious seeking: Just to identify as evangelical is to identify with zeal.

But that excuse for the Catholic numbers still leaves evangelicalism with plenty of room to boast of its success. Saying that a form of Christianity is optimized for conversion rather than cultural inheritance is hardly an insult: It seems like a fulfillment of the New Testament’s mandate *and* an essential adaptation to secular conditions. It’s reasonable to worry, as a Catholic writer [suggested a decade ago](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9781439178331), that the evangelical model might be successful “at religious mobilization” but not “at sustaining commitment across a lifespan or across generations.” But right now that writer (it was me) has to acknowledge that the evidence is running the other way: Evangelicalism doesn’t just win more converts than Catholicism; it also keeps more of [its young people](https://prri.org/research/religious-change-in-america/) in the faith.

Not all of them, of course: There are plenty of “ex-vangelicals,” whose vocal disillusionment (and strong representation in elite circles) ensures that any acknowledgment of evangelical success tends to be hedged around by caveats and critiques. Sure, the megachurches may be thriving, the critique runs, but the intellectual tradition is underdeveloped, the aesthetics are tacky, the hucksters are numerous, the hypocrisy is gross, and the partisanship is scandalous.

If they aren’t inherently political, evangelical churches do often struggle to find a place to stand that’s outside contemporary partisan categories and loyalties.

Among evangelical scholars, there’s multigenerational lament over what the religious historian Mark Noll [once called](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780802882042) the “scandal of the evangelical mind.” Among both ex-vangelicals and internal critics, there’s a despair over the captivity of evangelical faith to conservative ideology (and especially to Trumpism). There are persistent arguments that evangelical identity just reduces to white male chauvinism, and persistent fears that it seeks theocratic rule.

Some of these critiques are rebutted effectively by the former *Christianity Today* deputy editor Bonnie Kristian in her recent book *[In Defense of Evangelicalism: A Response to Its Cultured Despisers](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9798217153176)*. There’s little evidence that evangelicalism is uniquely marked by hypocrisy and insincerity, which abound in religious and secular communities alike. The conflation of evangelical piety and white identity ignores the extent to which evangelical distinctives are shared by the black church, adopted by immigrant converts, and widespread across Africa, Latin America, and Asia. Evangelical political engagement is more likely to be defensive than controlling or incipiently theocratic: Fire-breathers, prosperity preachers, and Trump cultists are part of the evangelical fabric but hardly typical expressions of Sunday-morning piety.

In a softer, less splenetic form, though, the critique of evangelicalism makes reasonable points. Evangelical churches do have a strong anti-intellectual current, which is part of why de Tocqueville’s prophecy holds in elite circles, where evangelicalism loses many of its best-educated young people to either Catholicism or post-Christian worldviews. (It’s notable that in the Vatican’s recent [engagement-cum-debate](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/29/us/anthropic-claude-morals-ai.html) with the AI industry, the spokesman for potential machine consciousness, Anthropic’s Chris Olah, was an ex-vangelical.) There is often a lack of theological seriousness, a mild philistinism, a separation from the deeper currents of Christian history and tradition. And if they aren’t inherently political, evangelical churches do often struggle to find a place to stand that’s outside contemporary partisan categories and loyalties.

These weaknesses—manifest just this week in [the editorial turmoil](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/21/us/christianity-today-magazine-layoffs.html) at Kristian’s now-former magazine—may put a persistent limit on evangelical growth and cultural influence. They offer reasons to be doubtful of the Protestant writer Timon Cline’s [recent claim](https://firstthings.com/a-catholic-moment-or-an-evangelical-century/), advanced to counter Catholic triumphalism, that “we may already be in an evangelical century.” They are among the reasons why, despite spending a lot of time in evangelical churches in my childhood, I ended up (with my family) as a teenage convert to Catholicism.

---

But the basic test of any religion, especially any missionary religion, is whether it actually persuades people to practice and believe. And the evangelical model—the entrepreneurial pastor, the adaptable church structures, the emphasis on welcoming seekers and accommodating families, even the suburban and middlebrow aesthetic—clearly works under 21st-century conditions in a way that requires respect and some form of imitation.

The vices of evangelicalism need correction and critique. But there will be no 21st-century revival, for Catholicism or any other kind of faith, that doesn’t reckon with the sources of its undeniable success.
