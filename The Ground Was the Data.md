**AGINCOURT WAS WON ON SUNDAYS · PART 2 OF 3**

On the night of October 24, 1415, rain fell on a plowed field in northern France, and the King of England ordered his army to keep quiet.

Henry V’s men had been marching for more than two weeks. They were short of food, and a larger French army had shadowed them most of the way. Now it stood across the field. Henry ordered silence in camp. A knight who broke it would lose his horse and his armor. On the other side, as Shakespeare tells it, the French spent the night at dice, betting on English prisoners they had not yet taken.

Henry had spent the march reading what was in front of him. After a skirmish at Corbie, on the Somme, his men took French prisoners, and the prisoners talked. The French meant to break his archers with companies of armored horsemen. (A French battle plan from that campaign still survives, and it says the same thing.) So Henry gave an order. Every archer would cut a stake about six feet long, sharpened at both ends, and carry it. On the morning of the battle, each man drove his stake into the mud in front of him, angled toward the horses.

Then it rained all night on freshly plowed clay between two woods. The ground did much of the rest.

Whatever the French plan said, it was written before the rain.

Last week was the back half of the loop: who decides, who acts and who owns the call. This week is the half that comes first. What can the person making the call actually see? And how old is it?

## The Pentagon named the archer

Two days after Part 1 went out, the two men running Project Agincourt published an open letter to the military and to industry. Owen West, who has led the Pentagon’s Defense Innovation Unit, and Max Strasiser, a Navy SEAL senior chief, laid out how they plan to build the Autonomous Warfare Command. That is the new four-star headquarters for drones and robots, due by October 1, 2027.

The letter names two American strengths to build on. One is the lead in advanced AI. The other is the corps of noncommissioned officers (the sergeants and petty officers who train troops and lead small teams), which the letter calls unmatched.

That is the archer.

I would like to think they read Part 1 first. They did not need to. Anyone who has spent a year at a military school knows where the Army keeps its Sundays.

The sentence that matters most for this week is four words long: “Evidence will decide outcomes.” West and Strasiser wrote that competitions, exercises and combat results will decide which systems grow and which get cut. They also wrote that every dollar spent should be auditable, and should lower either the cost of destroying a target or the risk to the troops.

I agree with every word. The trouble sits in the first one. Evidence is data, and most of it will come from the machines themselves.

## What telemetry means outside the brochure

Telemetry is the data a machine reports about itself and the world around it: where it is, how much fuel it has left, which fault codes it is throwing, what its camera saw and when. A digital twin is a software copy of a real plant, vehicle or battlefield, built from that data and meant to stay current with it. At AUSA (the Army’s annual trade show in Washington), both were for sale by the acre.

John Boyd, the fighter pilot turned strategist whose loop of observe, orient, decide and act ran through Part 1, treated the second step as the center of gravity. Orientation is the picture you build from what you see, filtered through what you already believe. A telemetry feed is one input to your picture. A digital twin is a picture somebody already built for you.

Both have the same weakness. A twin is only as good as the last time someone checked it against the ground.

Henry checked. The French plan never got the chance.

## The Army’s own admission

On May 20 the Army Applications Laboratory, the Army’s office for buying new technology quickly, posted a notice asking industry for help with a basic problem. Units need data from their vehicles (maintenance faults, fuel, water, ammunition) in front of the commanders who plan around it. The notice said that getting the data off the vehicle remains a challenge when the network is jammed, cut or thin.

The lab’s briefing for industry was blunter. Units rely on delayed reports entered by hand. So they react to breakdowns instead of getting ahead of them, and that slows down combat operations.

On October 5 the lab and the 1st Cavalry Division at Fort Hood, Texas, picked a five-company team led by Detroit Defense to put sensors on wheeled vehicles and predict failures before they happen. Soldiers will test it through next summer. My favorite part is what the team calls the soldier as a sensor. The maintenance records soldiers enter at the point of work count as data the truck’s own sensors cannot produce.

***The archer shows up again. This time he is holding a tablet.***

Six Army captains had already explained why that is harder than it sounds. In May, Gage Callahan, Thomas Canchola, Timothy Naudet, Olivia Beattie, Bonvie Fosam and Adam Knapp, all at the Army’s AI Integration Center in Pittsburgh, published an analysis in Army Sustainment. They studied the maintenance records for 4,097 Caterpillar D7 bulldozers, 196,516 readings in all.

More than 82 percent of the readings were useless. About 62 percent of the dozers showed obvious anomalies, such as odometers that ran backward. In a typical month, more than half of the mechanics’ time entries claimed the job took six or twelve minutes.

The captains did not blame lazy soldiers. They blamed incentives. Commanders are graded on the numbers the reports produce, so the reports produce good numbers. Their diagnosis belongs taped to every operations monitor in America: enforcing reporting rules “increases compliance, not accuracy.”

If you run a fleet, you already know which of your reports that describes.

## The record has to remember without advantages

Shakespeare saw this coming too. In the St. Crispin’s Day speech, Henry tells his men that old soldiers forget, “But he’ll remember with advantages / What feats he did that day.” A veteran’s story improves every year he tells it. In a man at a tavern, that is forgivable. In a database, it is how a bulldozer drives backward.

A machine’s record has to remember without advantages: what it saw, when, from where, and the date someone last checked it against the ground. Last week I wrote that every fact a machine relies on should carry that date. This week I have to amend my own rule, because the public reporting on one case this year suggests the date alone is not enough.

I want to be careful here. The February 28 strike on the Shajarah Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab, Iran, is under investigation. I am not going to guess at what that investigation concludes. What matters for this series is the mechanism, and parts of it are on the public record.

In July, CNN reported, citing three people familiar with the decisions, that the targeting databases in use during the first days of the Iran war carried automated warnings. The warnings flagged some target records as years out of date and in need of review. According to those sources, strikes on some of those targets were approved anyway, including the one that hit the school. US officials neither confirmed nor denied the report. Satellite images reviewed by CNN show the school inside a military compound in 2013, and fenced off with its own entrance by 2016. Reuters found painted murals on its walls in imagery from 2018.

On the facts reported so far, the system had the date. What it lacked was a rule that made the date matter.

The Pentagon’s internal review has not been released. Bloomberg has reported, citing people familiar with it, that the review points to outdated imagery, flawed intelligence and heavy reliance on AI. The Pentagon has said the investigation is ongoing, and a White House official told CNN that the United States does not target civilians. I will leave the conclusions to the investigators. When the report is public, I will read it for two things: *what the system did when someone overrode its own warning, and how that decision was recorded*.

So here is the amendment, and it applies to every system in this series, military or not. A date on a fact matters only if it can stop the loop, or if overriding it takes a named person’s signature, recorded at the moment it happens. A warning anyone can click past is decoration.

## The rule that already answers the question

Since 2012 the Pentagon has had a rulebook for autonomous weapons, DoD Directive 3000.09, last updated in January 2023. Its best-known line requires that these systems let commanders and operators exercise “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force.” Fewer people read the next part. The screen between operator and machine has to give transparent feedback on the system’s status. And the people responsible have to be able to see, audit and explain the data the system relies on.

Put plainly, human judgment is only as good as what the human can see. The Pentagon wrote that idea into its weapons policy fourteen years ago.

The rulebook is now being rewritten. On June 5 the President signed a national security memorandum, NSPM-11, ordering an update within 90 days to keep pace with AI. It is part of a broader push to clear what the memo calls unnecessary barriers to rapid deployment. A week later, Sen. Ruben Gallego of Arizona wrote to Secretary of War Pete Hegseth asking how a compressed rewrite would protect American troops, allies and civilians. In May, Emil Michael, the Pentagon’s chief technology officer, had told a Senate panel the directive “absolutely needs updating,” partly because of lessons learned in Iran. The 90 days ran out in early September. On October 20, Michael told senators the department still plans to address it. The update had not been issued.

Congress is working the same question. The Senate Armed Services Committee’s version of next year’s defense bill (not yet law, and built on text both parties accepted into the committee’s draft) would require autonomous systems to give controllers enough monitoring data. It would require records of the data and logic behind each target selection. And it would send failures, unexpected behavior and near misses to a central repository.

That last provision is the most useful sentence in the bill. Since 1976, pilots and air traffic controllers have reported their own close calls to NASA’s Aviation Safety Reporting System, and aviation learns from mistakes that did not kill anyone. Autonomy needs the same habit.

**One ask, for whoever holds the pen on 3000.09.** Whatever the rewrite loosens for speed, keep the feedback requirement and add two lines. Every fact a system uses to recommend or select a target carries the date it was last checked against the ground. And overriding a staleness warning takes a named officer’s signature, written into the record when it happens. Both take seconds. Both leave a record a stranger can replay.

## Think on the machine, then bring the receipts home

Inference is the moment a trained AI model makes its call: that shape is a truck, this part will fail within 40 hours, that heat signature is a person. The Army’s notice explains why more of that work will happen on the machine itself. When the network is jammed or gone, the data cannot travel to a distant server for an answer. The drone or the truck has to think where it is.

That makes the record harder to keep. A machine that decides on its own has to hold its own receipts and carry them home when the link comes back, with the time and source of every reading intact.

Henry’s silence order was an early version of the same discipline. Part of reading the ground is controlling what the other side can read about you. A machine that thinks for itself can stay quiet until it is safe to report.

## The record travels. The authority does not.

A robotics engineer in Germany watched the Part 1 video and told me I had jumped too fast from soldiers to sheriffs. He was right. The correction belongs here.

The four-part record from Part 1 (a named owner, dated facts, the contrary note and a replay) travels from a division to a county. The authority to use force does not. A soldier fights under the law of war. A deputy works under the Constitution, state law and the department’s own use-of-force policy. A sheriff’s drone can carry the same record an Army drone carries. It answers to a different rulebook and a different public.

Europe draws the line in law. Its AI Act does not apply to AI used only for military, defense or national security purposes. It does treat many police uses of AI as high-risk, which brings duties to keep automatic logs and allow human oversight, starting December 2, 2027, after a delay approved this summer. Under that law, the police will owe a record the army never will.

For an American county, the practical version shows up in year two. It is a telemetry question before it is a budget question. A grant buys the drones. Then somebody has to decide what the video keeps, for how long, who can search it, and who checks that the map the drone flies against still matches the streets.

Schools face the same questions in a smaller building. The drones at Deltona High School in Florida exist for the worst day of a school year. The questions about what their cameras record belong to every ordinary day. Somebody should answer them in writing now.

## Business outcomes: a twin is worth its last check

Strip the uniforms off again. In January, PepsiCo said a digital twin of some of its U.S. plants, built on Siemens and Nvidia software, delivered a 20 percent gain in throughput on its first deployment. ABI Research, an industry analyst firm, added a fair caveat. PepsiCo does not yet have a live twin fed continuously by every machine. The gain came from modeling the plant accurately and testing changes in software before anyone moved a conveyor.

That is Henry’s march done with a laptop. Get the ground right, then move.

The risk runs the other way too. A twin built in January and never checked again turns into the French plan by summer.

If you run fleets, plants, pipelines or job sites, ask how many of your bulldozers drive backward. The Army found obvious anomalies in the records for about 62 percent of its D7s. Nothing about your maintenance system makes it immune to the same incentives. Your people are graded on reports too.

The scorecard from Part 1, the Volatility Absorption Index (a 0 to 100 measure of whether autonomy is making you harder to hurt and paying for itself), has a part for data integration: how much of your relevant data lands in one picture. After this week, I would score that part with one more rule. Any feed nobody has checked against the ground in the last 90 days counts as zero. One fused picture of stale data is how a fast loop ends up confidently wrong.

## What I would do Monday

**If you run a program, public or private.** Pull 20 records from your system this week and check them against the ground: the vehicle, the asset, the site. Write down how many matched. That number is your real data score, whatever the dashboard says.

**If you sell into this market.** Put a date on every fact your product shows an operator. Build the override log before the customer asks for it. They are about to.

**If you run a city, a county, a campus or a company.** Write the retention rule for your cameras and drones before you buy the next one. Then name the person who checks the map against the streets.

## Run a ground-truth check

A first pass takes an afternoon. Use real records, and do not let anyone pick them for you.

1. Pick one system the machines feed: a maintenance system, an asset map, a dispatch database, a plant twin.
2. Pull 20 records at random, not the ones someone volunteers.
3. Check each one against the ground, in person or with fresh imagery. Is the asset where the record says, in the condition the record says?
4. Write down two numbers: how many matched, and the median age of the last time anyone verified each record.
5. Find the override. Can a person act on a record the system flags as stale? If so, who signs, and where is that written down?
6. Repeat next quarter with a new random 20. The trend tells you more than the first number.

**A worked example, with invented numbers.** A regional utility checks 20 poles from its asset map. Fourteen match. The median record was last verified 26 months ago, and nobody has to sign to override a stale flag. Its data integration score stays provisional until it has a field-check schedule and an override log.
