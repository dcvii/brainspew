Alex Gibney’s new four-hour documentary is so determined to expose the billionaire’s failings that it inadvertently showcases the achievements that made him a titan.

In 2023, Alex Gibney, the Academy Award-winning filmmaker, announced that he was working on a documentary about Elon Musk. He asked Musk for an interview. The answer was delivered on X: “Gibney,” Musk informed his 241.5 million followers, “is a douche.”

You could take the world’s richest man at his word. Or you could spend three hours and 55 minutes watching Gibney do his best to prove Musk right.

The director’s new film, released Friday and simply titled *Musk*, brings the same monomaniacal energy to assassinating its subject’s character that Musk himself brings to building reusable rockets or self-driving cars. The scope of Gibney’s ambition is revealed early on in the film: Because Musk wouldn’t sit down for an interview, Gibney coos as the opening credits roll, the director decided to create an animated AI Elon and interview it instead.

In the hands of a filmmaker actually curious about his subject, this decision might’ve generated some genuine insights, especially as Robo-Musk was only fed lines that IRL Musk had spoken in public interviews. Instead, Gibney gives us a cartoonish Bond villain, sitting on a gaming chair and spitting out decontextualized snippets meant to support the film’s not-so-subtle thesis: Unless we somehow stop him, Elon Musk will destroy the world.

Gibney seems to mean it literally: The movie’s poster shows Musk standing atop the globe, having cut it in half with a chainsaw, and the film itself begins with a poorly animated sequence showing the earth exploding in slow motion.

And that’s just the first five minutes.

What follows is every bit as operatic, grandiose, and, frankly, silly. Musk’s ex-wife is trotted out to argue that he was not a very emotionally present husband, a fact that will surprise precisely no one who has ever heard Musk, a self-described autist, speak. Ashley St. Clair, the social media influencer who had a son with Musk, breaks down in tears when she recounts how the billionaire, upon choosing to end their relationship, was so heartless as to offer her $15 million in cash and an additional $100,000 a month for the next two decades. Samantha Power, President Barack Obama’s former United Nations ambassador, cries on camera, too. The irony is palpable: A woman who won a Pulitzer for writing a book calling on the West to intervene and stop genocides—and then did effectively nothing as Syria’s Bashar al-Assad slaughtered more than 600,000 of his own people—sobs dramatically as she assails Musk of shutting down the United States Agency for International Development and accuses him of starving millions of African children.

If all of the above sounds ridiculous, it’s because it very much is. “Musk,” Gibney informs us at some point during his magnum opus, “didn’t just move to Texas—he colonized it.” How? By starting companies, Gibney continues dramatically, and employing 26,000 people in the Lone Star State. By the same logic, Imagine Entertainment, the Hollywood powerhouse that partly owns Gibney’s production company, is colonizing Beverly Hills.

Gibney might have probed some legitimate critiques of Musk. He might’ve explored what happens when one man achieves the level of wealth, power, and technological capabilities previously reserved exclusively for nation states. He could’ve taken a hard look at Silicon Valley culture and its weirdo beliefs (see under: effective altruism). He could’ve even looked in earnest at Musk’s very real achievements, and asked himself why the self-styled real-life Iron Man succeeded where so many others have failed. But Gibney has no patience for such trifles. Instead, he drills down on what he argues were Musk’s real crimes against humanity: He hyped his products on *Saturday Night Live*. He launched a company dedicated to interplanetary exploration without first taking the time to consider crucially important questions, like what sex would feel like in space or whether or not women in orbit may lose some bone density. He spoke out vocally against the government’s Covid-19 lockdown policies, which, as we all know, were uniformly flawless, compassionate, and wise.

Hilariously, the director’s own footage often works against his narration. At one point, for example, Gibney, reporting on Musk’s release of the Twitter Files, concedes that the company, prior to being purchased by Musk, did sometimes discriminate against conservatives. Still, he adjudicates, “the old Twitter was not a wokeathon.” We might’ve believed him if he hadn’t just shown us the old Twitter’s offices, which had “#Ferguson” painted in giant letters on the wall and a closet full of T-shirts that read “#StayWoke.”

Watching these absurd accusations unfurl one after the other, I found myself wondering if Gibney wasn’t secretly pulling a brilliant prank, reinventing himself as the Andy Kaufman of documentary film and seeing how much drivel the audience would take if it came from an acclaimed director and was spoken in the all-knowing tones of prestige nonfiction film. Walking into the theater, after all, I was handed a pin that read “I saw *Musk*,” suggesting that the director himself considered sitting through his four-hour film a feat meriting some sort of medal.

My suspicion grew stronger with each of the film’s sections describing the different eras in Musk’s life. The world’s first trillionaire, Gibney argues with increasing frothiness, is a miserable failure who stole Tesla from its original and humble founders; used SpaceX mainly as a front to siphon off fat government grants; ran Twitter into the ground because he was too dumb to realize that advertising money was more precious than free speech; and accomplished nothing with his Department of Government Efficiency save for increasing the national deficit when he fired all those gallant IRS agents who were very close to collecting princely sums from delinquent individuals and corporations. One of the film’s interviewees, an editor of a no-longer-relevant magazine, perfectly sums up the film’s account of its subject: Musk, she says smugly, “has no particular skills.”

The new film brings the same monomaniacal energy to assassinating its subject’s character that Musk himself brings to building reusable rockets or self-driving cars.

Y’know, other than building mechanical arms that can catch massive rocket boosters mid-air, one of Musk’s many accomplishments Gibney fails to mention.

The only segment that approaches something remotely recognizable as real journalism is brief: Musk, Gibney argues, knew that his Teslas weren’t yet capable of safe and efficient self-driving, yet released and promoted the feature anyway, leading to dozens of deaths. Because Tesla settled nearly all cases out of court, however, Gibney has little to go on, and so he substitutes bluster for facts, accusing Musk of “selling us dreams.”

The accusation is Gibney’s leitmotif. He even commissioned David Byrne to write an original song, “ [Let Me Sell You a Dream](https://youtu.be/wcFdiEcQdTc?si=zJb67GLOrofwBgKr),” that captures his main gripe, namely that Musk is too obsessed with ushering in the future to worry about the consequences of his creations.

Which, again, could’ve made for a fascinating film: The tension between brilliance and hubris, after all, has always been at the core of some of our greatest works of art, from *[I, Robot](https://bookshop.org/a/93116/9780553382563)* to *Citizen Kane*. But Gibney is profoundly disinterested in these questions. Instead, he’s troubled by others, as he makes all too clear throughout the film.

Why, he muses early on, did we let Musk grow so wealthy? And how, one of his interviewees wonders out loud, could anyone simply be allowed to make hundreds of billions of dollars?

To most Americans, these questions are easy to answer: In a free market, we neither allow nor prevent anyone from making money—we leave it to hard work, great talent, and a little bit of luck. We call this system “capitalism,” and though it has its flaws, it has worked out pretty well, especially considering the alternatives.

But Gibney and his interviewees seem to live on a different astral plane. On their planet, successful entrepreneurs are necessarily corrupt and inept psychopaths, because success itself is suspect. As are men: The film’s largely female cadre of commentators often remark on the fact that there’s something particularly and toxically masculine about wanting to build spaceships, say, or cut government waste. Or have children: Sure, Musk’s habit of having many children with many women in order to make sure America is swirling with beautiful, bright babies is eccentric, but Gibney’s talking heads made it sound like it was the act of having babies itself they oppose: “This obsession with procreation,” sneers tech writer Kara Swisher, “is just strange.”

If these opinions themselves aren’t sufficiently alien—and alienating—Gibney makes the truly inexplicable decision of shooting his interviews against a green screen and then juxtaposing the same sort of virtual backgrounds we’ve all come to know and hate from Zoom. The result is making everyone on camera look like they live in large, sunny, fake houses that don’t and can’t exist in reality.

Which, in a way, is a perfect visual metaphor for the world as Gibney sees it. The real America, the one we know from discernible and observable facts, is nowhere on display. In Gibneystan, no record exists of Musk the champion environmentalist, hailed by the left until he refused to continue and parrot its platitudes. Nor is there any mention of Big Tech’s frequent and egregious collusion with Democrat-controlled administrations; the sin of seeking proximity to political power is unique to the devilish Musk himself.

And Gibneystan might’ve been an amusing place to visit for a few hours if it weren’t for the fact that its denizens wish to impose their strange worldview on the rest of us. As each of Gibney’s interviewees makes clear, theirs is an America in which we oughtn’t to be proud, oughtn’t to work hard, oughtn’t to attempt audacious things. Walking out of *Musk*, one suspects that had Gibney turned his attention to the moon landing instead, he would’ve given us a portrait of Neil Armstrong as a privileged cisgender white male tragically colonizing the moon.

None of this, however, is to say that *Musk* isn’t an effective or an entertaining film. See it, and it’s very likely that even if you entered the theater skeptical about its subject’s achievements, you’ll leave it with a strong, inexplicable desire to dash down to your local Tesla dealer or Starlink retailer and spend some cash to support Elon Musk.

That’s because whatever else he may be, Alex Gibney is a proficient filmmaker, and the choice he gives us in his latest epic is simple: We can live in a world governed by mutually accrediting mediocrities who obfuscate to enforce their strange and zealous ideological beliefs on the rest of us, or we can live in a world where ambitious, curious, and patriotic Americans dream big and go for broke. For most of us, the choice is easy.
