There’s a specific kind of person who does everything right and still loses.

They’re smart. They work harder than the people around them. They read the books. They take the courses. They pick up the extra shift. They optimize every hour of their day. By every measure the world usually rewards — intelligence, effort, discipline — they should be crushing it.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!XbB-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F44c657f5-d10e-4e6e-a641-d06fc96a22c3_1332x866.png)

But they aren’t.

They watch people with less talent and less discipline somehow end up with more freedom, more money, and more control over their own lives. Meanwhile, they stay stuck. Grinding harder each year. Wondering what they’re missing.

The answer is a concept called game theory. And once you understand it, you cannot unsee it in your own life.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth game theory reveals: **most smart people don’t lose because they’re not smart enough. They lose because they’re playing the wrong game — and their intelligence and effort make them lose faster.**

That sentence contradicts almost everything you’ve been taught. Most success advice says “try harder.” Game theory says trying harder inside a broken game is the worst thing you can do.

Let me show you why.

#### The Auction Where Smart People Paid $40 to Win a $1 Bill

In 1971, economist Martin Shubik ran an experiment that has haunted game theorists ever since.

He auctioned off a single $1 bill. One rule: the highest bidder wins the dollar, but the second-highest bidder still has to pay their final bid — and gets nothing for it.

The auction started normally. Someone bid a nickel. Someone else bid a dime. Basic stuff.

Then something strange happened.

Once you’re in second place, the math changes underneath you. You’re no longer bidding to win a dollar. You’re bidding to avoid paying money for nothing. So the logical move is to bid higher and get back to first place. The other person does the same. Both bidders are being completely rational at every step.

Shubik watched smart people pay as much as $40 to own a $1 bill.

These weren’t dumb people. They were clever, competitive, well-educated. They got sucked into a game where every rational move made their situation worse. The harder they tried, the more they lost.

Economist Gordon Tullock gave this pattern a name: **rent-seeking**. It happens when people pour real resources — time, money, effort, years — into competing for a prize, and the total spent chasing it becomes bigger than the prize itself.

Once you see this pattern, you see it everywhere:

- The bidding war on a house where two families talk themselves past their own limits
- The credential arms race, where everyone gets the extra degree, so the extra degree stops meaning anything but the debt stays
- The price war where two businesses undercut each other into bankruptcy
- The 60-hour weeks at a company where hitting the target just means getting a higher target
- The dream job at Google that 40 other qualified people also want

In every one of these games, working harder isn’t your advantage. It’s the fuel the game runs on.

The people who lose the most are the ones who tried the hardest, refused to quit, and stayed in the longest. Being the best bidder is exactly how you end up paying $40 for a dollar.

The reframe is uncomfortable because it contradicts what we’re trained to admire. We admire the person who tries harder. But in a rigged game, trying harder is a sign that you haven’t understood the rules yet. The real skill — the rare one — is looking at a game, seeing how it actually works, and choosing not to play.

Working harder was never going to fix it. The $1 bill was never the point.

You were.

#### The Nobel Prize Winner Who Solved Traffic

William Vickrey won the 1996 Nobel Prize in Economics. He died three days after it was announced.

He’d spent much of his career on a problem so ordinary that most people never see it as a problem at all: the traffic jam.

Vickrey’s insight was that nobody in a traffic jam is doing anything wrong. Every driver on the road at 8:15am made a sensible decision. They need to be at work at 9. The roads are free. Leaving at 8:15 is the rational choice. And precisely because it’s the rational choice, everyone makes it. The jam is the sum of everyone’s rational decisions collapsing into one shared disaster.

Now think about how people try to beat traffic.

You leave earlier. Smart. For a while it works — until enough other people reason the same way, and the peak shifts earlier to meet you. You learn the back routes. So does everyone with a GPS app, and the shortcuts fill in until they’re as slow as the road you left. You drive more aggressively. You arrive at the same time, more tired.

Every move you make gets copied by everyone else making the same move. The jam quietly reforms around you.

You cannot out-effort a jam. Because the jam is made of effort exactly like yours.

Vickrey’s actual solution wasn’t to tell drivers to try harder or wake up earlier. He knew no amount of individual cleverness could touch the problem — because the problem lived in the structure, not the drivers.

His solution: congestion pricing. Change what it costs to drive at peak times, and you change the game everyone is playing. Alter the structure and the behavior follows. Tell people to try harder and nothing changes.

Most financial advice is the equivalent of telling someone to leave for work earlier.

Cut the coffee. Hustle more. Pick up the side gig. Optimize. Grind. Some of it helps a little — the way leaving 10 minutes earlier helps. Until the game takes it back.

The driver who actually beats traffic isn’t the one who tries hardest. It’s the one who notices the jam is a game with rules — and asks whether there’s a different road, a different hour, a different job, a different game entirely, where the same effort isn’t immediately absorbed by the structure that produced the jam.

The high-value skill was never driving harder. It was seeing the game for what it is.

#### Money Is a Game Almost Nobody Studies

Here’s a pattern that has quietly cost most people millions of dollars over their working lives.

They spend decades earning money. They almost never spend a single week studying what money actually is, how it’s created, or how the people who control the money supply benefit.

Modern money isn’t backed by anything physical. It’s numbers on a screen. Central banks can create it. Governments can print it. When they do, the money you already own is worth less — even though the number in your bank account stayed the same.

There’s a word for this: inflation. It’s a polite way of saying your money got quietly devalued by the people who create it.

Most retail investors think about return in nominal terms. They see their portfolio go from $100,000 to $110,000 and feel good. Meanwhile, the actual buying power of that money may have dropped, because the total money supply grew faster than their returns.

Working harder to earn more nominal dollars is the traffic jam version of investing. You’re grinding to win a race whose finish line keeps moving away from you.

The people who compound real wealth over decades understand this early. They don’t just try to earn more. They try to own assets that appreciate faster than money is being created — stocks, real estate, productive businesses, things that hold value when the currency doesn’t.

If you don’t understand this game, the money you earn melts like an ice cube while you’re holding it. If you do understand it, your entire financial strategy shifts.

Not more money. Better positioning.

That’s the difference between smart-and-broke and quietly wealthy.

#### Incentives Run the World

If you learn one thing from game theory, learn this: see the incentive, and you can predict the outcome.

Every professional in your life has incentives that shape the advice they give you. Not because they’re bad people. Because incentives quietly bend every judgment humans make.

- **Lawyers** charge by the hour. They have a subtle incentive to recommend one more revision, one more meeting, one more filing.
- **Realtors** earn a small percentage of the sale price. Getting you $20,000 more on your home might cost them 2 extra weeks of work for only $400 in extra commission. They’d rather close fast at a lower price.
- **Wealth managers** get paid on assets under management and trading fees — not on your actual returns. They win when you stay invested and trade more, even if simpler strategies would serve you better.

Warren Buffett summed it up in one line: *“Never ask a barber if you need a haircut.”*

Most smart people spend their entire lives asking for advice from people who have an incentive to give a biased answer. Then they wonder why their outcomes don’t match their intelligence.

Three rules protect you from this trap:

1. Understand a person’s incentive before asking for their advice.
2. Run your own small experiments instead of trusting expert opinion blindly.
3. Fail and get rejected yourself so you develop real judgment instead of borrowed judgment.

Once you start reading every conversation through this lens, you notice how much of the advice you’ve been taking was never truly aimed at helping you win.

It was aimed at helping someone else win, disguised as help.

#### How I Made $70K a Month, Then Walked Away

For years I wrote on a platform called Medium.

The game was simple. Readers paid a $5/month subscription. The money got divided among writers based on how many views their content got. I studied the game early, wrote viral content, got it published in the biggest publications on the platform, and made friends with the staff so I could see changes before anyone else.

At the peak, I was earning $70,000 a month.

I thought I was a genius.

Then game theory started sending warning signals. The staff’s egos got bigger. They announced publicly that their number one company goal wasn’t revenue — it was spreading political causes. They hired old-media people who applied strategies that didn’t work online. They started launching high-brow publications that consistently failed. They began publicly telling writers how to write, essentially saying, *“You’re all wrong. Trust us. We know better.”*

Every one of those signals meant the same thing. The people running the game were shifting the rules against the players who built the platform.

I left in 2021.

People told me I was crazy to walk away from that income. But the game had changed underneath me. The rules that had made me successful were being rewritten by people who didn’t understand what actually worked.

Within a couple of years, the platform became irrelevant. Every major writer left. The revenue collapsed. What I had earned there became laughable compared to what I built afterward on a platform with cleaner incentives.

This is the pattern game theory teaches you to see.

Games change. Incentives shift. The people running the platform, the industry, or the company will always eventually optimize the rules to serve themselves, not the players. Your job isn’t to be loyal to a game. Your job is to notice when the rules are turning against you — and leave before you become the one paying $40 for a dollar bill.

Loyalty to a rigged game is how smart people end up broke.

#### The Reframe That Separates Winners From Smart-and-Stuck

Here’s the paragraph worth screenshotting.

Working harder inside a broken system is the worst response to that system.

Because a system is never truly broken. It’s producing exactly the outcomes its incentive structure was designed to produce — whether by intention or accident. Working harder increases your input inside that broken structure. But it doesn’t change the structure itself.

The correct response isn’t more effort. It’s to identify whose interests the current structure serves — and either position yourself in favor of those interests, or leave the game entirely to play a different one where your effort isn’t immediately handed back to whoever controls the rules.

Stop optimizing for the game you wish existed. Start playing the game that actually exists.

That single mental shift is worth more than any skill, credential, or work ethic you’ll ever build. Because without it, every skill you build gets absorbed by a game designed to consume it.

#### The 7 Rules High Performers Use to Master Any Game

If you internalize nothing else, internalize these.

**1\. Treat your life like a video game.**

Successful people who grew up gaming picked up game theory by accident. They learned systems thinking, feedback loops, resource management, and pattern recognition through play. If you never gamed, start now. If you did, apply that same mindset to your career. Pick your avatar. Build a personal brand. Acquire monetizable skills. Set clear KPIs. Play with the same intensity you played Warcraft or Zelda. Almost every career game has been won before — reverse-engineer what the winners actually did and copy the moves that work.

**2\. Train yourself to see patterns others miss.**

Pattern recognition is one of the most valuable — and most stressful — forms of intelligence. Once you see how games really work, you’ll notice patterns that others actively deny. Money printing. Corporate loyalty theater. Educational credential inflation. Career ladders that lead nowhere meaningful. You’ll feel like you’re going crazy sometimes. You aren’t. Most people will never see what you see. Learn to be okay with knowing without expecting others to see it. Just quietly position yourself around those patterns and let them do their work.

**3\. Stop working harder. Start building leverage.**

Leverage is when you get more output from the same effort without needing more resources. Modern leverage sources include AI, code, capital, labor, media, and audience. Working harder inside a bad game increases your output slightly. Adding leverage multiplies your output by 10x, 100x, or 1000x. The high performer’s job isn’t to work harder than everyone. It’s to attach leverage to whatever effort they’re already putting in.

**4\. Study the foundations of game theory.**

Read *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* by John von Neumann. Watch *A Beautiful Mind* about John Nash. This takes a weekend. It changes how you see every decision, negotiation, career move, and investment for the rest of your life.

**5\. Pick games you can actually win.**

Most smart people jump into games without checking the odds. They enter fields where the top 0.001% take almost all the winnings and the other 99.999% get crumbs. Before committing years to any game, study people who’ve already won it. Ask them: *was winning worth it? Are you actually happier?* When I did this with corporate executives who reached the top, almost every single one admitted the climb hadn’t made them happier. Back-to-back meetings. No time with family. Zero control over their own calendars. The game they’d won was a trap. Choose games where the winners are actually thriving — not just visible.

**6\. Learn which games to walk away from.**

This is the rarest skill of all. I walked away from Medium at the peak. Shortly after, I walked away from the corporate ladder. Both decisions looked reckless at the time. Both were correct. Playing bad games long enough to see them collapse in real time is one of the most valuable education experiences you can have. You’ll never fall for those games again — no matter how tempting they look from the outside.

**7\. Find the one move that makes the whole game easier.**

Every game you might play has a hidden lever — one specific move that quietly makes 80% of the effort go away. For me it was social media. Once I mastered it, I never had to worry about leads, opportunities, or income again. For someone else it might be sales, code, distribution, or capital allocation. Your job in any game is to identify that one leverage point and become obsessed with mastering it. Everyone else grinds. You unlock the shortcut.

---

Game theory has been around since 1944. It isn’t new. It isn’t secret. It sits waiting on the shelf for anyone smart enough to notice it.

High performers all around you are quietly using it. They aren’t smarter than you. They just took a proven framework and applied it to their lives.

Working harder is what people do when they haven’t understood the rules yet.

Studying the rules, seeing the incentives, spotting the rigged games, walking away from the losing ones, and stacking leverage on the winning ones — that’s what people do once they finally see how the world actually works.

You’ll know you’ve mastered game theory when the people around you say *“you got lucky.”*

You’ll know they haven’t when they keep grinding at $40 for a dollar bill.

The choice is always yours.
