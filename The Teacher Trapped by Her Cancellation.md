Back in 2020, Tiffany Riley was fired from her job as a school principal over a Facebook post. The world seems to have moved on from that era, but Riley says she can’t.

In the heart of Vermont lies the small town of Windsor—population: 3,559—otherwise known as the “birthplace” of the state. It was here that Vermonters adopted their first constitution in 1777. “The people have a right to freedom of speech,” it declares, “and of writing and publishing their sentiments.”

On June 10, 2020, in the febrile weeks after George Floyd was murdered, Tiffany Riley, the principal of Windsor School, availed herself of that right—then watched in horror as her life was torn to shreds. It began, as so many cancellations did that year, with a Facebook post. “I firmly believe Black Lives Matter,” she wrote, on her private page. “But I DO NOT agree with coercive measures taken to get this point across.”

“Just because I don’t walk around with a BLM sign should not mean I am a racist,” [she went on](https://www.wcax.com/content/news/Board-considers-future-of-Vt-principal-after-social-media-posting-571271571.html). “While I understand the urgency to feel compelled to advocate for black lives, what about our fellow \[Americans in\] law enforcement?”

The backlash was swift, and brutal. Within 48 hours, the superintendent of the school district had told her she was being put on paid administrative leave—“while we negotiate an exit strategy”—and the school board had sent an email to all parents and staff at Windsor School decrying the “ignorance, prejudice, and lack of judgment” in Riley’s post. Some parents disagreed with the board, but Riley didn’t know this at the time. In Windsor, she felt like a pariah.

From there, her story morphed from a small town scandal into what Riley calls a nationwide “firestorm.” National outlets like the *[New York Post](https://nypost.com/2020/06/15/vermont-principal-placed-on-leave-over-facebook-post-on-blm/)* covered it, while a national free speech advocacy group [rallied to defend](https://vnews.com/2020/06/25/anticensorship-group-calls-for-windsor-principal-to-be-reinstated-34925425/) Riley’s right to keep her job. The media portrayed her as a villain, or as a hero, depending on which slice of the political spectrum they were catering to. “People’s reactions, regardless of the side, just felt so blown out of proportion,” she said.

Besides, she was more concerned about her immediate reality. Riley decided to sue the school, filing a lawsuit on June 25, 2020, that accused it of placing her on administrative leave “without seeking any input or explanation from” her, and without “considering any other information including her past performance and activities promoting equity.” Additionally, Riley argued that the school district “created reasonable expectations” that she would “not be able to find future employment as a principal or school administrator.”

Unfortunately, Riley’s expectations proved accurate. That September, she said, she started applying to new jobs in districts within a 40-minute radius of Windsor—and didn’t get a response from a single school. So, she told me, she expanded her search to districts across the state—and, when none of those yielded any prospects, to neighboring states. In October 2020, with her lawsuit still pending, the school board fired Riley— [despite concluding](https://apnews.com/article/Ddb8251472b5a7bf0817faaa32010614#:~:text=White%20principal%20fired%20for%20post%20about%20'Black,Poll%20MLB%20NBA%20WNBA%20NHL%20Soccer.%20Entertainment.) that “there is no evidence that Employee personally holds racist beliefs.“ She estimates that she has applied to over 70 jobs in the years since, but in most cases, she said, she never heard anything back, not even a rejection email or a phone call asking for additional information. Despite years of experience, she hasn’t had a single interview.

The reason, to her, is obvious. On the portal where districts post jobs, SchoolSpring, applicants must check a box on their profiles stating if they’ve ever been terminated from a job. Riley had no choice but to check *yes*, generating what she called a permanent “red flag.” But even without the red flag on her applications, the moment you google Riley’s name, the first links to pop up are all about her firing. “If two people or more are competing for the same position,” Riley told me, “who would an interviewer go with?”

---

“The lower risk,” she shrugged. “Now I’m considered higher risk.”

This is despite the fact that, in March 2021, the court ruled that the school had retaliated against her for her political views, breached their employment contract, and violated her right to due process. Windsor School was later forced to pay Riley $650,000 in the settlement. But after the legal fees and the emotional toll—and given the apparent impossibility of finding a new job in education—Riley said the money hasn’t made much of a difference.

When I spoke to her a few months ago, she had just been rejected from yet another teaching job, this time a special education role. “It doesn’t even call to me anymore,” she told me over the phone. Today, Riley is still grieving her career. “I’m trying to move on,” she told me, “but I do think there is long-term damage that is irreparable.”

I’ve told stories like this one before. In 2021, a [17-year-old in California](https://www.thefp.com/p/he-was-falsely-accused-of-blackface) had his life derailed when someone found an old picture of him wearing a dark acne mask—and claimed it was “blackface.” In 2022, a [12-year-old boy in Illinois](https://www.thefp.com/p/evanston-hate-crime-haven-middle-school-children-branded-racist) saw his friend tying a jump rope into a noose in the schoolyard and asked if he was okay—only to be accused of a “hate crime.” When I first spoke to Riley last year, I wondered if there was any point telling another story about an ordinary American whose life was ruined for a nonexistent crime in the wake of the Black Lives Matter movement. I also wondered how many more there were to tell.

But since then, two things have become clear. First, this phenomenon is not a thing of the past, and it’s not just those who question progressive orthodoxy who are met with a rush to judgment, and face harsh consequences—whether it’s [getting fired](https://news.syr.edu/2025/10/23/the-great-divide-understanding-us-political-polarization/) for Facebook posts or [decades-old inappropriate tweets](https://www.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/50084279/trail-blazers-fire-new-announcer-inappropriate-posts). But at the same time, there is the attempt by some, especially mainstream Democrats, to pretend that the woke era was nothing more than an embarrassing phase they do not endorse. That effort was best represented over the summer, when Congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez laughed about the excesses of this patch of American history. “I have a local city councilman that has this saying that ‘Woke 1 was crazy,’ ” she said, when asked about things said at that time—as if everybody who fell afoul of the mob has now received a full apology, and those to blame have taken accountability. When the fact is, it’s only the lucky few who can bounce back from cancellations. “If you take comedians, and artists, and musicians who have been canceled, they have a platform that allows them to change that narrative, to speak up about cancellations and to make people rethink the way that they behave,” said Riley. “Unfortunately, I’m not on that platform.”

For every famous canceled person who has been redeemed, there are countless people like Tiffany Riley—ordinary Americans unfairly found guilty of bigotry, who might briefly become a national story, before they are forgotten, left to pick up the pieces of a life.

“It wasn’t just a loss of my job. It was a loss of everything: a loss of my professional circles, a loss of friends, the loss of my identity,” Tiffany told me. “It felt like a hole in my heart.”

Before there was the Facebook post, there was an argument about an American flag.

In May of 2020, Riley was busy planning a Covid-safe high school graduation ceremony in the school parking lot. To inject a little hope into the occasion, the school’s groundskeeper painted the Stars and Stripes on the grass of a nearby hill, in view of where the ceremony would be held. But a few days before graduation, which was set for June 5, Riley got an email from a recent black alumna of the high school, who wanted the flag to be erased because it had “been used as a shield for white America.”

Riley tried to reassure this former student. “We are all outraged by the injustice and inequality that continues to exist in our Nation,” she wrote. But Riley also said the flag was meant to “represent respect, unity, and peace as a Nation,” and, as such, wouldn’t be going anywhere.

In the seven years she’d been principal of Windsor, Riley had proven that she supported efforts to fight racism. Around the time she received this email, records show, she was in discussions to implement a mandatory “Institutional Racism” course in the district’s schools. She was also a member of the Vermont Equity Practitioner’s Network, a professional development course made up of educators from across the state who were working on creating diversity, equity, and inclusion programming.

So, she was eager to find some sort of solution that might make her former student feel heard. Riley suggested, in an email to the alumna, that the school add a phrase underneath the flag to reflect the current political situation: “United We Stand? All Means All? Stop the Violence? Equity for All? What would you propose?” Those phrases, the ex-student responded, “are politically correct ways of saying all lives matter. This statement ‘all lives matter’ has been used to take away from pointed efforts to save black lives.”

By the evening, Riley had decided to delete her Facebook post. She was “reluctant” to do so, she said, in part because she didn’t regret the sentiment she expressed in the post.

I reached out to this alumna multiple times, but never got a reply. Because her emails were part of a private correspondence with Riley and other administrators at the school, I am choosing not to publish her name. At the time she was corresponding with Riley, according to the school, this young woman was living with a couple who actually worked at Windsor School: Erin and Kabray Rockwood. (Erin, who is white, was a student support staffer at the school; Kabray, who is black, is a basketball coach.) Riley reached out to Erin for advice. She asked her if the family had a Black Lives Matter flag the school could borrow to display during the graduation ceremony, or if the couple’s children wanted to make some posters.

Erin didn’t reply until a couple days after graduation, when [she wrote](https://www.mychamplainvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/61/2020/10/Riley-16VSA243dDecision-signed.pdf) that she was “feeling broken” by Riley’s request. “Asking kids of Windsor to make posters to represent their race because the school didn’t take the steps to do it just seems so off,” Erin wrote. Riley said she was “taken aback” by her response. “I knew she was hosting a Black Lives Matter rally that weekend in the community,” Riley said, “so my thinking was, *perhaps they have something that we could use*.” (Erin did not respond to multiple requests for comment.)

While considering what to do about the flag, Riley spoke with David Baker, the superintendent of the school district. According to internal records, Baker and another member of staff “agreed that ‘Black Lives Matter’ should not be painted on the hillside at the graduation site because of “concern about bringing politics into graduation.”

The event went off without a hitch.

But the alumna wasn’t done. On June 9, she wrote Riley again, copying Baker into the email, to say she was “truly saddened” that the flag had remained during the ceremony. “You chose not to hear me much like America has chosen \[not\] to hear black and brown people across the nation,” she wrote. The alum also asked why neither Riley nor Baker had attended the town’s recent Black Lives Matter rally. (Riley told me she couldn’t attend because she was helping her elderly mother move into a continuing care facility at the time.)

“After talking to a select board member last night, we will be pushing for more,” the email warned. “You will be held to a higher standard in the future.”

Baker defended Riley. “I hope your heart grows so that you can have more faith in the school system of which you are an alum and in the community of which you are a part,” he wrote, to the ex-student. But his support fell away when, two days later, Riley made her Facebook post.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IV4v!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fce0727f0-c1eb-47cf-8109-43e2b688c88d_1320x30.png)

When Riley woke up the morning after venting on her personal Facebook page, she saw that she’d received a handful of messages from people in the community. “You have a lot of explaining to do,” read a message from one of the school’s track and field coaches, Riley said. Later, people she’d never met began sending her messages on Facebook. She remembers one that read: “racist cunt.”

Internal emails reviewed by *The Free Press* showed nearly a dozen people wrote into the school board to express their disappointment in how it had treated her.

“I wasn’t using it as a platform for some formalized stance about Black Lives Matter,” she told me. “When I wrote the post, it was just out of frustrations and shock and disbelief” about some of the violence she had seen on the news, she explained. Plenty of her colleagues, and members of the school board, had made posts on their own Facebook pages about Black Lives Matter, she said. *So*, she thought, *why couldn’t she?*

Riley’s post was only visible to her roughly 250 Facebook friends—but one of those friends was Kabray Rockwood, who had taken a screenshot of her post and shared it on his own page, describing Riley as “not an administrator I can support” and criticizing her for not attending the town’s Black Lives Matter rally. Within hours, his post racked up more than 100 comments condemning Riley. (Kabray did not respond to requests for comment.)

At 5:35 p.m., Baker called her. “Your statement was inflammatory; it was incendiary and quite frankly it was racist,” he told Riley, according to transcripts produced by the school. He asked Riley to take down the post. She initially refused. “I can’t just remove my post and act like I never put it there,” she told me. “I need to own it. I’m not gonna deny that it ever happened.”

By the evening, Riley had decided to delete her Facebook post. She was “reluctant” to do so, she said, in part because she didn’t regret the sentiment she expressed in the post. But, “I didn’t want to make anything worse,” she told me—and she wanted to “be sensitive to the people who were offended by it.” In its place, she wrote another statement. “I recently made a public post that unintentionally offended many people,” it read, in part. “I understand the struggles of the black lives community and stand with them in the fight against racism.”

But the damage was done. The next day, June 12, Baker told Riley over email that the school board would be placing her on paid administrative leave. The board sent an email to all parents and staff stating that, “Ms. Riley’s continued role as our school leader damages the school and its students.” She would be officially fired in October.

When I reached out to members to ask for comment, then-board chair Elizabeth Burrows, now a Vermont state representative, emailed me the 50-page conclusion of the school board’s investigation into Riley, which served as the basis for her dismissal, and declined to comment further. Although the document acknowledged the complete lack of evidence that Riley is racist, it described this fact as “irrelevant to the point that the content of the post contains messaging reasonably susceptible to being construed as espousing racist views.”

“Even if it were a close issue whether the content of the post can be considered racist, it is clear that the post fails to advance equity ideals in a manner consistent with the District’s and Employee’s work,” the document stated. “That is, even if the post does not advance racist views, it fails to advance equality for non-white community members.”

At the time of her firing, Riley might have had more supporters than she realized. Internal emails reviewed by *The Free Press* showed nearly a dozen people wrote into the school board to express their disappointment in how it had treated her. Some of the objections were political. “What was wrong with her post? I feel the same way!” one parent wrote to the school board. Others emphasized that citizens should have a right to say what they believe. “The impulsiveness to penalize someone for their thoughts rather than engage in a conversation and attempt to understand \[their\] point of view is disappointing, especially from an institution that should \[be\] educating, not indoctrinating, students,” another parent wrote.

“It just felt like she was being punished for having a different view,” Misty Boutin, a mom of four kids in Windsor, told me. “Even the people who were defending her didn’t feel like they could come out and say it.”

“I’m sure she didn’t know who had her back at that point,” she added.

Has there been any kind of reckoning in the town since? Any recognition that Riley’s banishment was unfair?

“Nobody really talks about it,” said Zach Dragon-Greenwood, a Windsor resident who grew up there. “I think a lot of people almost kind of forgot it happened.”

Even after losing her job at Windsor School, she held out hope that, through the legal proceedings, she and the district might be able to “work things out.” When she [finally reached](https://vtdigger.org/2022/10/19/former-windsor-school-principal-settles-lawsuit-over-firing/) a settlement with the school district in October 2022, she felt relieved. But by this point, she’d started to realize how hard it was going to be to get another job in education—the job application process throughout this time was “defeating,” she said. The payout didn’t feel like enough (an outside analysis hired by her legal team estimated she lost anywhere from $2.1 to $3.4 million in future income and benefits due)—and Riley was still a pariah in town.

So, a couple months later, in the summer of 2021, Riley and her husband sold their dream Victorian home in Windsor County, used the cash to buy an RV, and moved into a campground in Maine for a fresh start. For a time, she and her husband operated a food truck making smashburgers.Riley said she has thought about going back to school to become a mental health counselor. But “the idea of being 54 and taking out a college loan is depressing,” she said. She’s trying to build a business as a life coach helping people manage grief—a feeling she says she relates to. In the meantime, she’s picking up odd jobs where she can—selling flooring, bartending, teaching yoga. It’s been six years now. Letting go of teaching has hit her hard.

“I was grieving the loss of my job and all that,” Riley told me last year. “But then I started to grieve the loss of my identity, because my identity and my job were so intricately intertwined, because I immersed myself so much into my work.”

She’s tried to restore her reputation— [going on a podcast](https://open.spotify.com/episode/5WTghR7cTE68MJENibRM0G) to speak about her cancellation and reaching out to some old acquaintances in Windsor. But mostly, she said, she just wants to move on. “I’m trying to re-create myself,” she said.

---

Riley said she doesn’t hold a grudge against those who screenshotted a post she shared on her personal Facebook account and thrust it into the national discourse. “I can’t hold any anger toward that person,” she said. She also told me that she understands “why some people reacted the way that they did,” given “where we were socially and politically at the time.” She’ll even admit that the post itself “was insensitive toward some people.” But: “I wish that they picked up the phone and said: *Hey, I’m just curious what you meant by your post because I found it offensive*,” she said. “I wish we could have had that conversation, but we didn’t.”

She doesn’t seem angry; she seems tired. “It’s like, okay, my post offended several people,” she said. “I understand that it was written during a time that was a lot of turmoil.” She repeated over and over, “I understand that,” as if she’s acknowledging a lecture she’s already heard a thousand times before. “But when we react and respond to something, we have to ask ourselves: *Is our reaction worse than the initial action we were reacting to?*”

A lot of people admit, now, in the broadest sense, that Woke 1.0 was crazy. But that doesn’t meaningfully change things for people like Riley, or any of the people I’ve spoken to who had to leave their communities because they were unfairly accused of racism in that time. It doesn’t give them their lives back. And their specific stories don’t get a lot of airtime. There aren’t consequences for the people who piled onto them.

“I feel like the world is trying to forget about 2020,” said Riley, “and there’s still not a lot of acknowledgment about some of the damage that was done to people like me.”
