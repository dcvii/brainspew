German leaders enabled the rise of a populist party through their failures. Now those leaders cling to power by branding their challengers as extreme.

Nothing thrills journalists more than throwing around the term *far right*. In addition, they relish each chance to describe the far right as being “on the march.” That is the gift that headline writers have been granted again this week with the victory of the Alternative for Germany (AfD) party, which finished in first place in an election in the eastern region Saxony-Anhalt. The party won almost 44 percent of the vote, which, as most of the English-language media have pointed out, gives a “far-right party” the first opportunity to govern a state in Germany since the end of World War II.

If all this were true, it would be a cause of great concern. But is it?

For people concerned with the future of Europe, the AfD is a vital as well as a special case. As such, it requires special attention. Unfortunately, most of the people writing about the party from outside Germany are intent on performing this feat of political analysis with all the care of a brain surgeon attempting to operate while wearing boxing gloves.

To start with, it is not the case that the AfD is a far-right party. Which is not to say that it does not have people within it who could be described as far right, or that it could not become such a party in the future.

There is a simple explanation for why the AfD has risen to become the leading opposition party in Germany: mass immigration, legal and illegal. But it took years for that issue to dominate. When the AfD was founded in 2013, it was mainly focused on addressing the fallout from the Eurozone crisis. The new party rejected the European Union’s extraordinarily optimistic belief that a single European currency could combine Greek balance sheets with German bookkeeping.

---

But in 2015, Chancellor Angela Merkel decided to open the gates of Europe and Germany to anyone from North Africa or the Middle East who wanted to walk in and call it home, particularly those fleeing the Syrian civil war. Normal border protocols were abandoned. More than a million migrants arrived in a matter of months. Germany, and in particular its welfare state, was overwhelmed by the surge and attempted, too late, to stem it. The vast majority of the arrivals from those years remain a net drain on the German state. In addition to this economic reality, a small but significant number of arrivals have brought extremism, terrorism, and petty as well as large-scale criminality. It might also be noted that the movement has brought a [mass importation of antisemitism](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/to-douse-growing-anti-semitism-germans-call-for-holocaust-education-for-recent-migrants) into a country that did not exactly need a boost in this area.

Given the extraordinary consensus that existed over the past decade among the mainstream parties of the right, center, and left, it was inevitable that some political movement would arise in Germany to point out and oppose the folly of Merkel’s policy. The AfD was well-placed to do so, and, in time, the economists who had dominated the party in its early years were replaced by people who believed that immigration was the number one challenge facing Germany.

Their success in the years since 2015 has been remarkable. In 2017, they entered the German parliament with 94 seats. Then they doubled their share of the vote between 2021 and last year.

During this period, almost everything that could be done to stop them has been done. The party’s leaders have been the subject of intimidation and occasional [public assaults](https://www.dw.com/en/germany-far-right-afd-candidate-attacked-in-mannheim/a-69271485). The German domestic security agency—the BfV— [has listed them (and then de-listed them)](https://www.dw.com/en/german-intelligence-must-pause-extremist-label-for-afd/a-76140587) as an “extremist” group and therefore a national security threat. Political opponents have attempted to portray the party as essentially indistinguishable from the Nazis. And regular press stings have attempted to hole the party below the waterline by noting its [friendliness toward Russia](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2026/09/07/germany-afd-putin-russia-friend/), supposed [ties to extremist movements](https://www.tagesschau.de/investigativ/br-recherche/afd-bundestag-rechtsextreme-mitarbeiter-100.html), and [episodes of corruption](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cd13114xwkqo).

Along the way, there are several details that have been lost on the German political elite and the foreign press. The first is that the AfD would never have risen as a party if it were not for the failure of the mainstream parties in Germany. After overloading the German asylum and welfare system, it seemed to be impossible for the center-right Christian Democrats and center-left Social Democrats and others to acknowledge the mistakes of the Merkel era. Aside from their own complicity, it seems to be their calculation that conceding the failures of Germany’s immigration and integration systems would benefit the AfD, when it would more likely take some air out of the party’s tires.

If all opposition to the migration crisis can be said to be the work of German neo-Nazis, then Europe is lost.

The second thing that is deliberately missed is that the AfD itself is plainly a very mixed bunch. There are leaders within it like Beatrix von Storch, who would be a completely mainstream Conservative, if she were in Britain, and a centrist Republican if she were in the U.S. Yet the party also has figures like [Björn Höcke](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/frontline/article/germany-afd-bjorn-hocke-far-right-thuringia-election/), who is the party’s leader in Thuringia and who would plainly be regarded as being on the radical edge of politics in any country, and who has called it a “ [big problem](https://www.wsj.com/articles/german-politicians-comments-about-hitler-stoke-debate-1488912569) ” that “one presents Hitler as absolutely evil” and referred to the national Holocaust memorial as a “monument of shame.”

AfD’s national co-leader Alice Weidel and other party heads plainly have a problem here. It is a problem that many popular right-wing movements across Europe have suffered from. As soon as they are founded, they are denounced as “far right,” “fascist,” and “neo-Nazi.” At which point it is assumed, correctly, that many people, including potential voters as well as members, will stay away. But there are also genuine far-right movements alive in Germany and across Europe, which inevitably sidle toward any party that has been cast as far right, whether or not the label is warranted.

It would be enormously convenient to the mainstream parties if the AfD were simply dominated by neo-Nazis and other far-right extremists. And so they help make it so by defining AfD that way. The parties that failed the country so badly in the 2010s have only one way of surviving the disasters they have brought on their country: delegitimize the only alternative.

It is for these reasons, among others, that the foreign coverage of Germany’s political shifts is so unsatisfactory. In the aftermath of this weekend’s election, the easiest thing in the world is to say—as almost every headline writer has—that “the far-right AfD” won the regional election. The fact that these same publications, from the BBC to *The New York Times*, rarely, if ever, use the same scary monikers to describe [far-left parties gaining support](https://www.wsj.com/world/europe/europes-far-left-is-having-its-moment-862ec533) across the continent is a matter for another day. But the hope appears to be that the public will be sufficiently uninterested in the details and that the prospect of the Nazis returning will be enough to spook readers into reflexive opposition.

---

When Germany was reunified after the fall of the Berlin Wall, there were prominent figures, including British prime minister Margaret Thatcher, who were opposed to German reunification. They knew that Germany would always be too strong, too influential, and too domineering within Europe. All those fears have come true. Germany does indeed dominate Europe’s economy and its politics. Where it goes, the rest of Europe is likely to go.

If all opposition to the migration crisis can be said to be the work of German neo-Nazis, then Europe is lost. Because no party of opposition will be untainted. If Germany can be saved, then Europe can be saved. But in order for that to happen, people—including the international press—need to listen to what is actually happening in Germany, take notice, and recognize the situation as it is, not the strange Nazi-haunted fever dream that they curiously seem to wish it to be.
