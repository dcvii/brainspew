One day, I was playing catch-up with my e-mails. I am always behind and I consider it a real triumph to be caught up. I came across an e-mail on Ancestry.com. As is my habit, I clicked on the message from a distant relative. I did not recognize the name. The name was like a name picked out of a phone book. No tinge of the familiar. No haunting glow of a family I might associate with childhood memories from running around Grandma’s backyard. Just nothing.

I pulled up the Ancestry page and what struck me was the closeness. The relative was a second cousin which meant we shared the same great-grandparents. Now, I was intrigued. You see, those who inhabit the zone of first and second cousins are known quantities. I know their family names like the back of my hand. Be you Twyman or Womack, Brown or Dodson—I know your name and your people. Your people are my people more or less. We are the fruit of the same four grandparents, the same eight great-grandparents.

A second cousin whose family name was a complete mystery posed a mystery, a puzzle to figure out. I am not naive. I had fifteen uncles and four aunts. Throw great-uncles and great-aunts into the mix and an unknown second cousin should not be a complete surprise. My second cousin was from Virginia which made perfect sense. What befuddled me was his family name. I had never seen this name before in my life. Quick research revealed my cousin’s name originated near Carlisle in Northern England. The name was Anglo-Saxon of rural roots.

Isn’t it odd how we feel a connection upon sight of close family names? My cousin’s name was not lighting up those neural pathways for me as opposed to a family name like *Twyman*.

Adam Coleman wrote a recent essay about discovery of a lost father. *[I Accidentally Found My Real Father](https://www.adambcoleman.com/p/i-accidentally-found-my-real-father)* Coleman writes that every child wants and deserves to know where they come from. In my excitement, I shared Coleman’s surprising story of a found father with my wife this morning. She was unimpressed. However, I immediately thought about the creative associations between discovering a lost father and a lost grandfather.

When my second cousin with the English name reached out to me, he wanted to know more of his past. My great-uncle Walter Dodson did a bad thing and a good thing. He left a baby daughter in Woodstock, Virginia without a father. She was denied knowledge of her heritage, her people down in Southside Virginia. That little daughter would be lost to her uncles, aunts and first cousins, including my Mom. On the other hand, Walter had the sense of self to leave memory of his family name, Dodson, in Woodstock. But for that tip of the hat to unborn generations, my second cousin might still be lost to his Dodson kinfolk.

I know the name Dodson. And now my lost second cousin knows his grandfather, Walter Dodson. Ancestry.com is a true blessing as we fill the holes in too many family trees.
