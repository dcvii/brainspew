“Are we going to church tomorrow?” I asked Catherine on Saturday evening, as I do most Saturdays, already knowing the answer.

“Yes”, she said. But then, as is often her custom, she asked a deeper question. “Why do you go to church?”

“Because it is what we do,” was my answer. “No,” she said, “that’s not good enough. There has to be more.”

In this exchange, we each exposed our elemental selves. Let me explain.

Catherine is deeply philosophical, deeply introspective, and very much a ponderer. Rigidity, schedules, and expectations exert little influence over her. Deeper meaning is always sought.

I am a man of schedules, rituals, habits, and routines. While it was incomplete of me to give the answer I did, it was not incorrect. Things become schedules, rituals, habits, and routines with me BECAUSE there is deeper meaning, a larger goal or objective to be had. The appearance of some repeated practice in my routine is not a sign of its banality, but of its centrality.

But, recognizing both that Catherine does not process as I do AND that there was a legitimate question there, I accessed the file in my brain that dealt with “why going to church needs to be in the schedule”, and mixed with a basic understanding of New Testament scripture. I said something like “gathering with like-minded Christians means God is there.” Recognizing the potential rhetorical and theological trap I had worked myself into, I added, “God is everywhere, but He is actively present in that room.” This seemed to do the trick.

### Church Today

Imagine my surprise at church today when I speed-read ahead in the program to see the Gospel was from Matthew, Chapter 18 specifically the words “For where two or three are gathered in my name, I am there among them.” Barbecue sauce!

As I’ve said before, I am a Catholic who attends an Episcopal church because it is the childhood/lifetime church of the most important person in the world to me. One of her direct descendants founded it. When I get communion, I see the name of two others chiseled into the altar. It is where we took our girls, where they attended pre-school, and where they were confirmed. I have made friends here, and I have pre-existing friends who attend here. Catherine is deeply involved in one of the various ministries, and I help out with the food ministry now and then by making deliveries. All of this is somewhat unimportant except to say that sitting in church today was a very different experience than it has ever been before, and it is because Catherine asked me that question last night.

We attend the 0800 service, which is conducted in what Episcopalians call “Rite 1” with its “thy’s” and “meet and rights”. It is mostly attended by old folks, which we increasingly are, albeit more recent additions to the club.

As I looked around me, I thought about the “two or three” others gathered in His name. I thought about the man sitting in the pew in front of me, a man who I mentioned a few weeks back whose wife had just died. I sat there thinking about his sadness, and I was sad for him. Then I thought “Whoa! What about that woman standing next to you whose husband and father of your children was murdered a quarter century ago this week?” I thought about her sadness, and it made me sad.

I looked off to the right and saw an old friend, bathed in heavy, early sunlight. He used to be very active in the church, then left for a while, but is now back. At certain times of year, our north/south oriented sanctuary exposes its eastern windows to great gobs of sunlight, and this man was awash in it. It seemed right to me. God is light.

That light did not used to exist, or at least, it did not penetrate into the church. Years ago, during a major renovation, the stained-glass windows were taken down for safekeeping and replaced with clear windows. I remember not liking it initially, but apparently most of the church did, as the stained-glass never returned. I would not have them back, as beautiful as it was.

Two rows up are two sisters, both in their early seventies I reckon. They both have the most beautiful blue eyes. They chitchat like schoolchildren before the service. I find them fetching.

Several rows up are a couple we know. Early in the first summer of love some nineteen years ago, I attended her 60th Birthday party. It was the first time I had ever been served a lollipop lamb chop as an hors d’oeuvres. I am particularly thrilled with each repetition of this wonder. They are splendid people, and I almost always run into them on Christmas Eve in the local seafood shop as we grab the lobsters we both serve at dinner. I thought a lot about them today, about how the span of time that has passed since our meeting has landed me in about the place she was when I met her, and how I have had the joy of watching them age for nearly twenty years. He is a member of my men’s lunch club in town, and I am always happy to run into him there.

Off to the right, in the same row as these two, are another couple, contemporaries of the two I just wrote of. Lovely people, with just about the most beautiful private residence I’ve ever been in. I’m told they hosted a wedding shower for Catherine decades ago. As with the previous couple, these two have been great examples of love and devotion to each other across the years, examples I try to follow. He’s also a member of my lunch club. Both of them always have a smile and a good word for you.

We are really fortunate in our church to have three priests, a rector and two assistants, all of whom are talented preachers. I don’t imagine we’ll be able to hold onto all three forever, but I do hope we have this happy circumstance for a while. The one who preached today is also a very talented writer, and I run across his work in local outlets now and then. I am grateful for his scribbling, which always seems to come from a place of great contentedness and wisdom.

There’s a man in our row whose name I must learn, as he is there whenever we are. He took to fist-bumping the Sign of Peace during COVID and has maintained the practice. I used to be a little off-put by the way this congregation does the Peace. It’s kind of a full-blown conversation break, and whereas I was used to greeting those in my immediate vicinity in my Catholic Church days, where there was no sojourning across the aisle or to the front of the church as there is here. I am growing to like this way, especially as I grow more convinced that I am part of it all.

At the end of the service, the celebrants trooped down the aisle to the rear of the church to dismiss us, and most of the congregation pivoted as they walked past. I remained facing forward, eyes fixed on the south end of the church where stained-glass remains (so too on the north end). There, the face of Jesus serves as my concentration point. Wherever two or three are gathered.

### Labor Day Weekend

It has been a busy summer for visitors, most of whom have been my people. Catherine has been a trooper, but I think that when the last bunch left, we both took a big, deep, breath and looked forward to a little break. This Labor Day Weekend was that break. We took a boat ride to the neighbor’s house on Saturday afternoon for a very casual dock party, which was lovely. We went with two friends from Alexandria who have a weekend place out here, then spent a little time with them in our yard. But that was it. For the rest of what was a glorious weather weekend, Catherine and I just hung out together. It was wonderful.

Dash 2—working about an hour away these days—made some noise about coming home, but ultimately was visited by a friend from high school, so no Dash 2. I was a little disappointed, I don’t mind saying, but I’ll soon enough be driving up to see her coach her JV Field Hockey Squad.

### The Week Ahead

There is a lot going on this week, but nothing will be quite as important as commemorating 25 years since that terrible day. The passage of 9,125 days has lessened the day to day impact of Chris’s murder, but the anniversary (what an odd word to commemorate something so horrible) tends to be filled with emotion. We who experienced it from afar all have our stories about how we became aware of it, how we spent that day, how we were glued to the TV. I have heard Catherine’s account of that day start to finish one time, and it is gutting. She, her husband, and her babies (2 and four months) had moved into the house I now live in only four days earlier, and when he left on Sunday to return to New York, moving boxes were everywhere.

Catherine is the strongest person I know, the strongest person I have ever met. To pick up one’s life after such a stunning tragedy takes superhuman effort and great faith. I have the great pleasure of knowing a lot of people who were there for her, and who are still there for her. There is a special place in heaven for them.

That day aroused something great and wonderful in this nation, perhaps the only good thing about it. We have lost most if not all of that feeling, and it is very sad.

But I have to always remember, that how I experienced that day, and how I responded to that day, is fundamentally different from what that day means to my girls. I can never know what that day was like for Catherine, and how that day was for the rest of us is of no concern to her.

We will be quiet on Friday.
