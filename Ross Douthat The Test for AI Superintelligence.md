Last Friday we learned that Anthropic, one day into its existence as a publicly traded company, has in its employ a genie capable of resolving one of the hardest problems human mathematics has created. The stock market saw this and decided that Anthropic’s stock was somewhat overpriced.

On Monday we learned that Jensen Huang, an adulterous tycoon in a leather jacket who presumably could not solve the Riemann hypothesis if he had many lifetimes to work on it, would be restored to power at Nvidia. The stock market saw this and decided that, not only was his company suddenly vastly more valuable than it had been without him, but the entire tech sector was [suddenly worth $2.5 trillion more](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/stock-traders-brace-for-more-volatility-after-tech-driven-rout).

To many observers in Silicon Valley, the power demonstrated by Claude in mathematics heralds [the dawn of superintelligence](https://x.com/alexandr_wang/status/2124636606959345776). Any power Huang wields belongs to a mere mortal, whatever Wall Street traders may believe.

But the extraordinary reaction to the returning CEO is a useful example to keep in mind as we wait to find out whether the powers that artificial intelligence (AI) exhibits in mathematics extend to less computable areas of human competition.

At the very least, Huang’s return sets a useful standard as I try to devise my own personal test for superintelligence. I think it would make sense to declare an AI model comprehensively superhuman once its capacities are such that it would have made more sense for the Nvidia board to replace Huang with an AI CEO. And the capacities that would justify that choice are also the kind of capacities that could give AI sovereignty over our wider future, vindicating fears of AI doom and human disempowerment.

Two capacities, especially: prophecy and persuasion. Leadership in human affairs requires an ability to predict and to convince, to see around corners, and to induce other people to go along with you—even when they can’t see quite as far. This is how you win battles and elections, take over markets and create networks of influence, build and reclaim power in unexpected ways.

There is an element in these skills that is inherently embodied: People respond to physical leadership, to evocative voices and transfixing eyes and sex appeal. Maybe Tesla’s Optimus robots will evolve into charismatic Blade Runners, but, for now, an AI cannot be superhuman in its embodied presence. So our potential AI CEO would need to compensate with superhuman performance elsewhere.

---

One benchmark for that performance would be betting. Betting lines are a reflection of collective human wisdom sharpened by powerful incentives, and right now they tend to predict the winner of professional sports games between 55 and 65 percent of the time. Thus far, AI models working from the same public information [have not shown](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17765) any superiority in picking winners. But if a frontier model started predicting 75 percent of NFL or World Cup winners, say, it would have achieved the kind of prophetic power that you’d want in a truly superhuman CEO.

A second benchmark would be understanding mass-market preferences. If you have spent any time hanging around with book editors, you know that the publishing-world equivalent of solving the Riemann hypothesis would be consistently predicting which original stories will become bestsellers. (Sequels and celebrity memoirs and the like don’t count.) Let’s assume that AI is able to consistently produce novels that blend in with the human-authored literary middlebrow. That’s impressive but not superhuman; the superhuman test is whether the same AI can produce mass-market novels that invariably *outsell* the human competition—an inexhaustible supply of Colleen Hoover-esque bestsellers in a data center, each one selling millions of copies no matter what name is on the cover.

A third benchmark would be historical interpretation. This sounds a bit academic and abstract, but I think it’s a good test for the mixture of insight and persuasion that you often see from human trailblazers—the capacity to both discover what others have missed and win converts for that insight.

Imagine taking the existing sum of human knowledge, giving it the comprehensive frontier-model treatment, and emerging with a decisive argument about the identity of Jack the Ripper, or the date of the Gospel of Luke, or even a slam-dunk proof of some claim heretofore considered crankish (that William Shakespeare didn’t really write his plays, say, or that the CIA killed JFK). I’m not suggesting that a superhuman AI should be able to produce an argument on a vexed topic that convinces everyone; obviously, human nature often precludes that kind of agreement. But using the same historical data that human scholars and obsessives have been fighting over a long time to achieve a paradigm shift or forge a new consensus seems like a good example of superhuman capacity in action.

Then a final benchmark: reading and predicting individual people. This is obviously an important leadership skill, and it’s also a place where humans can display some pretty interesting powers. To be truly superhuman, for instance, an AI would need to outperform a mentalist like [Oz Pearlman](https://www.ozpearlman.com/), who steers his subjects but also clearly reads them—their personalities and backgrounds and physical tics—when he anticipates their choices.

Here are my two personal tests for superhuman reading—maybe unreasonably difficult ones, but it’s important to have some genuinely challenging benchmarks if you’re going to grace an intelligence with a godlike label.

First, if I gave an allegedly superhuman AI all of my published writing, all of my wife’s published writing, maybe some writing by my parents, and all relevant biographical data prior to the birth of our fifth child in 2024, would it be able to accurately predict the name that we gave him?

Second, if I gave the AI all of the above and also a copy of this essay that cuts off at this sentence, would it be able to predict [the quotation](https://www.folger.edu/search/?q=What%20a%20piece%20of%20work%20is%20a%20man&area=works&work=hamlet) that I’ll leave you with, which might usefully adorn the desk of any superhuman CEO?

*What a piece of work is a man, how noble in reason, how infinite in faculties, in form and moving how express and admirable; in action how like an angel, in apprehension how like a god: the beauty of the world, the paragon of animals—and yet, to me, what is this quintessence of dust?*
