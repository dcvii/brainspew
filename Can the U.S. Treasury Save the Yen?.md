Investors, politicians and economists said they wanted a world of fiat currencies with floating exchange rates, and they’re getting it good and hard in [Japan](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/japan). Surprise, now they want off the currency roller coaster.

The U.S. Treasury in recent days has joined with Japanese authorities in a rare coordinated currency intervention to stabilize the yen. The two sides have engaged in large-scale yen purchases, likely to the tune of tens of billions of dollars. Washington also is making available a line of dollar credit [Tokyo](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/tokyo) can tap by using U.S. Treasury debt it holds as collateral.

The idea is to give Japan more scope to intervene in the foreign-exchange market without having to sell U.S. Treasurys, which otherwise would push up American interest rates. This fear—that sudden exchange-rate swings in Japan would trigger tidal waves across global financial markets—is a primary justification for Treasury to join the interventions. And the fear is legitimate.

The intervention “worked” over the weekend to the extent that officials reversed the yen’s recent steep declines. As of Monday, one dollar bought about ¥156, a substantial improvement over the Japanese currency’s 40-year low of ¥164 per dollar last week.

Notably, however, no one expects the intervention to have much staying power. The coordinated show of force probably will deter speculators for a time. But there were reasons for investors to sell yen before and none of those have gone away.

Fiscal policy is an obvious culprit. Tokyo is indebted to the tune of 230% of GDP. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi this spring pushed through a record ¥122.3 trillion budget for 2026, and already has supplemented it with a few billion dollars more for energy subsidies amid the war in the Middle East. An aging population and chronic economic anemia raise questions about whether Tokyo ever will repay the debt, a worry that all else being equal suppresses demand for Japanese assets and thus the yen.

The [Bank of Japan](https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/bank-of-japan) also bears much of the blame. Despite much hullabaloo about monetary normalization in recent years, Japan’s short-term policy rate remains 1%. Unusually for major central banks, that’s negative in inflation-adjusted terms. It’s hard to say when the [BOJ](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/JP/XTKS/8301) might resume rate increases—possibly in September, investors believe—at what pace, or to what level. Meanwhile, the BOJ continues quantitative easing at a pace of ¥2.5 trillion in asset purchases every month.

BOJ officials and their political jeering squad (which includes Ms. Takaichi) appear to believe Japan’s economy still needs monetary support, despite ample evidence in recent decades that abnormally low rates have weighed on the economy. Tokyo also worries that a withdrawal of monetary support for the government-bond market might trigger a financial panic.

That latter point may even be true, but officials have traded one panic for another by setting in motion exchange-market wobbles. And instead of causing disruption to the bond market, they’ve stoked a politically poisonous bout of inflation that’s exacerbated by this spring’s energy shock. No one told Japanese voters that domestic inflation is supposed to be one of the “macroeconomic adjustment mechanisms” under a floating-exchange regime.

The longer the BOJ persists in its go-slow approach, the more dangerous the currency market becomes. Recent interventions won’t have a lasting effect on the supply of yen as long as the central bank keeps rowing in the opposite direction. A September interest-rate increase by the U.S. [Federal Reserve](https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/federal-reserve) in particular would widen the gap between Japanese and foreign interest rates and cause more investor flight from the yen.

A better yen salvage plan would involve less government spending and renewed reforms to spur economic growth—making Japan a more desirable place to invest. It also would require the BOJ to move more aggressively to normalize monetary policy.

The good news is that Scott Bessent’s Treasury has signaled its determination to work with Tokyo to stabilize markets. This global cooperation is welcome. But it’s no substitute for Tokyo’s commitment to fix itself. Absent that, no swap line in the world can save the yen.
