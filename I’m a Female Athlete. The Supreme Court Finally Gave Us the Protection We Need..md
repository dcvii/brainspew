When Lia Thomas, a biological male, joined Penn’s women’s swim team, my coaches told me to accept it. The Supreme Court’s ruling on transgender athletes vindicated me.

A man walked into my locker room at the University of Pennsylvania, ready to compete on the women’s swim team. He was six foot four, and had spent three years swimming on the men’s team. We were told to call him a teammate, and the adults in charge expected us to act like it was normal. Girls who didn’t were treated like the problem.

That’s what happened when Lia Thomas (*né* William) joined the Penn women’s swim team in 2021.

My teammates and I raised our concerns to our coaches and the school administration. Repeatedly. We said how unsafe it felt to shower and undress in front of a man, how unfair it was to practice and race against one. Whatever he believed about himself, it should not have been our job to affirm it.

Talking to the people running our program got us nowhere. Instead of protection, we were offered counseling to help us come to terms with undressing in front of a male. The athletic department called a meeting. Lia being on the team was non-negotiable, they said. Speak out and regret it for the rest of your life. We needed safety. Instead, we got something out of George Orwell’s *1984*: Sit in the room long enough, until you agree the man in front of you isn’t really a man.

I was 21years old, and I had to figure out how to protect myself on my own, while the people who were supposed to have my back looked the other way. Girls around the country have found themselves in similar situations for years, facing life-changing injuries, losing titles and awards, forced to share intimate spaces with men, sometimes without even knowing it. All because the institutions that should have stood up for them have been too cowardly to confront the mob. This week, the highest court in the country did what they couldn’t.

In a 6–3 ruling, the Supreme Court [upheld laws](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/supreme-court/supreme-court-upholds-state-transgender-sports-bans-rcna261384) in West Virginia and Idaho banning biological males who identify as transgender from competing in women’s and girls’ sports, giving states with similar bans already on the books firm legal footing to enforce them. It forces universities like Penn and sports leagues across the country to face the truth: The safety of the majority matters more than the comfort and convenience of a sick minority.

---

Swimming was my whole childhood. I was in the pool 20 hours a week by middle school, giving up countless Christmas holidays, weekends, and social events to chase a spot on a Division 1 team. Getting into Penn was the moment it all paid off.

When Thomas joined the women’s team, the gap was immediate and obvious. His personal best times in freestyle events outpaced the women’s NCAA records—not because he was a star athlete, but because he was a man. On the men’s team, he was unremarkable. But put him on the women’s team and that same male body became unbeatable. The biological advantage Thomas had over us girls was significant, and not something hormone replacement therapy could ever erase. That advantage made him a women’s NCAA champion.

He is just one in a wave of male athletes who have moved into women’s sports, forcing them to compete against male bodies while being told the competition is equal.

I am grateful for the justices who sided with biological reality and stood up for the safety and fairness of our spaces and arenas. The three who dissented failed America’s daughters, buying into an anti-woman agenda hook, line, and sinker.

Every time a male athlete is celebrated for breaking barriers in women’s sports, a woman is quietly pushed out the back door.

Justice Sotomayor [argued that](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-43_2b35.pdf) sex classifications must survive “heightened scrutiny” and that states must offer “an exceedingly persuasive justification for letting the sex classification stand.” She wants more evidence. Here is one: A [volleyball spike](https://www.congress.gov/119/meeting/house/118215/witnesses/HHRG-119-GO16-Bio-McNabbP-20250507.pdf) from a male player left Payton McNabb, a North Carolina high schooler, with permanent neurological damage.

Sotomayor also argues that Title IX “makes room for individuals to live in the gender they choose; it cares not just about sex assigned at birth but also about individuals’ ability to match (or not) their gender presentation to their gender identity.” Decisions made according to this view have cost American girls in more ways than Sotomayor seems willing to count.

The cost is not abstract. Every time a male athlete is celebrated for breaking barriers in women’s sports, a woman is quietly pushed out the back door. At the California state high school track championships in May, AB Hernandez, a male competing in the girls’ field, [dismantled the competition](https://www.latimes.com/sports/highschool/story/2026-05-30/transgender-ab-hernandez-wins-state-track-medal-amid-muted-protest) —not by a narrow margin, but by a distance that would have captured Olympic medals half a century ago. Girls were forced to line up against a different category of athlete entirely and told to call it fair.

Elle Patterson saw her hard-earned [volleyball scholarship](https://www.foxnews.com/outkick-sports/ex-sjsu-volleyball-player-speaks-alleged-scholarship-deception-coach-trans-scandal) at San Jose State vanish when a roster spot went to a male recruit. Her dedication, talent, and financial lifeline were casually discarded. Equal opportunity is not on offer for girls in Sotomayor’s world.

---

The architects of these rules hide behind the word *compassion*. But their empathy is selective. It evaporates when you bring up Payton’s injuries, the stolen medals in California, or Elle’s lost scholarship. It certainly didn’t exist for me and my teammates at Penn, who were told to shut up and smile for the cameras. “Fairness” built on the erasure of female excellence is not fairness.

Tuesday’s ruling is a real victory, one that has been years in the making. But the work is not finished. Three justices chose not to stand behind vulnerable young girls, and they are not alone. There are others like them across state and federal government who care more about ideology than about the girls in their districts. Twenty-three states still lack legislation protecting girls’ sports. This decision is their opportunity to change that. America’s daughters have already been waiting long enough.
