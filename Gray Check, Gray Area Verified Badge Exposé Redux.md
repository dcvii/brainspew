Near the end of April 2025, Glennon Doyle opened, and then rapidly closed, her Substack account. All after a pile-on from Substack users, mostly jealous writers who saw a millionaire—with a huge email list and a bunch of structural advantages—getting something the native Substackers thought she hadn’t earned. One critic noted that Doyle had a Bestseller badge within minutes of announcing her arrival. Substack staffers had welcomed Doyle and heralded her debut. That only exacerbated the anger from the mini mob. The pile-on started slow, then exploded, and Doyle was gone within a week. It was a PR disaster and small black-eye for the community.

Months later, gray “Verified Creator” checkmarks started appearing beside famous names: [Pete Buttigieg](https://open.substack.com/users/284251312-pete-buttigieg?utm_source=mentions), [Jimmy Carr](https://open.substack.com/users/110580902-jimmy-carr?utm_source=mentions), [FT Alphaville](https://open.substack.com/users/444688-ft-alphaville?utm_source=mentions), [Joseph Gordon-Levitt](https://open.substack.com/users/29221451-joseph-gordon-levitt?utm_source=mentions), [Lizzo](https://open.substack.com/users/130628499-lizzo?utm_source=mentions). They’re documented nowhere. They were announced…nowhere either. And they cut against Substack’s own promise that “paying readers, not Substack,” decide who gets status and badges on their platform. That promise came back in November 2022, when the Bestseller badges first dropped. They where promoted as an alternative to capricious, black-box verification schemes at places like pre-Elon Twitter or the emerging pay-to-play rumblings at post-Elon X and even Meta. They were fair, or so the thinking went.

But then someone moves ably within that framework and gets flamed into oblivion. And months later, Substack decides, due to its ongoing fetish for celebrities, to quietly do a workaround. I don’t think the sequencing is accidental. My read? Substack watched what happened to Doyle and built a credibility badge that doesn’t advertise a celebrity’s paid-subscriber rank. As a corporate instinct, that’s almost defensible. Celebrity launches are sometimes a matter of *damned if you do, damned if you don’t*.

What isn’t defensible is the silence. On October 11 of 2026, I emailed Substack’s press inbox five questions and a deadline of October 16. I got nothing but a generic auto-reply on October 12th. The radio silence wasn’t just deafening, it matched a pattern I’d gotten from the vast majority, (tho not all) of the verified accounts that I reached out to. With Substack, I call it weaponized indifference. With the accounts, I call it a code of silence, one I suspect is encouraged by corporate. Cost of doing business.

[Last year I wrote about the verified program](https://edokwin.substack.com/p/substacks-secret-verified-badges), mostly just to explain that it exists and that seemingly no one was talking about it. Today I’m gonna tell you, through a mix of original research, extrapolation, and conjecture, how it actually works and *why*.

---

## Receipts — Such That We Have Any

I’ll start by saying that I want to make the paper trail impossible to dispute before any argument. Whether I succeed in that is for you to judge. Let’s start with my original article and my calls, both public and private, for information on this program.

I talked about how and why verification was invented at social media firms. I noted, in my section “An Imposter Problem That Wasn’t“ that we don’t have some rampant issue of people impersonating celebrities here, at least not yet. Maybe that’s changed in the several months since the program started, but I doubt it. Substack is still a super niche platform, all thing considered. And it had other options.

In my “Ain’t Broke, Don’t Fix It“ section, I explained how the bestseller badges solved the problem that verification was developed to tackle on Twitter, Facebook, et al over a decade ago. All without Substack having to administrate it directly, since readers picked who the “real” version of someone was by paying for subscriptions. I noted how celebrites could promote their Substacks in un-fakeable external channels.

Imagine the alpha, the free publicity, and so on, from a Notable going on legacy media and mentioning their Substack page? They get subscribers (and money), sure, but the platform gets a whole new influx of visibility and audience. To quote moi:

“The Bestseller badge is supposed to measure something real: whether an audience values your work enough to pay for it. It’s democratic. It’s measurable. It rewards the ‘deep relationships’ over corporate caprice.“

Alas, that’s a choice and an ethos they ultimately seemed to increasingly renege on. As I detailed in the “Pattern of Opacity“ section, plus my platform accountability reporting before and since, Substack has quietly disregarding their own rules and norms for at least a few years now. The introduction of Flower badges, undisclosed algorithm changes, the Native Sponsorships (ads) push, discovery tool removal, leaderboard changes, the Pangram integration, and so much more.

### Questions, Questions — So Many Questions

I ended on the prophetically titled section “Questions Substack Won’t Answer,“ and the following…

> **Here are the questions Substack should answer:**

> 1. What are the criteria for receiving a gray verification badge?
> 2. What role does Instagram, and its parent Meta, play here?
> 3. How many people have been granted these badges?
> 4. Why was this system created?
> 5. Why no public announcement?
> 6. Why isn’t it documented in help files or support pages?
> 7. How does this badge system align with the stated value that “paying readers, not Substack, decide who gets a badge”?

I also implored the public (i.e. my readers) to ask around and report back to me, either in the comments or in private. But being the journalist that I am, I obviously didn’t stop there. I asked dozens of verified accounts, plus the principals and/or their management teams, for comment either on the record or on background.

Most didn’t answer. I will repeat: *most*. Like, the vast, overwhelming majority. But I got a few nibbles despite the code of silence. It’s clear that Substack doesn’t want this talked about. It’s similarly clear that these accounts, tho “official,” aren’t being used directly by the named person or business per se. They’re managed, which is entirely common, even standard, for celebrities in the digital age. But it does have certain implications, which I’ll touch on more below.

Back to the questions. Let’s add in, and compare, the ones from my 2025 article with the email I sent Substack corporate:

> “1. What are the criteria for receiving the gray Verified Creator badge?
> 
> 2\. What role do Instagram, LinkedIn, et al and their parent companies play in granting or validating it?
> 
> 3\. How many accounts currently hold the badge?
> 
> 4\. Why was it introduced, and why has it not been publicly announced or documented?
> 
> 5\. How does this badge align with Substack’s stated position that paying readers, not Substack, decide who gets a badge?“

Shorter, but largely the same basic ideas. I’ll tackle each so you have better context.

#### Criteria & Company Connection (#1 & #2, both lists)

Currently, all we know is that a verified badge can be attached, at Substack’s discretion, to any account and usually has a readout like the below:

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!sLXm!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbd107d42-4abe-4797-ad67-82163d536243_1080x1009.webp)

I changed/added “Linkedin” because I’ve found examples of accounts linking back to a Linkedin (parent company Microsoft) rather than a Meta platform such as Instagram. I’ve even seen some linked to X (formerly Twitter), often politician accounts. Obviously, these tech companies—of which I’d naturally include Substack—all communicate with each other via back-channels. It would be nothing for Substack to get assistance, or just tips and tricks, on how to run a verification scheme from Meta, Microsoft, or even X Corp. Perhaps from former employees, perhaps from current ones. A lot of folks bounce around these firms throughout their careers, fun fact.

But that’s just explaining the *how*. The biggest question is “why.” Like, why not just run their program and stipulate that they verified the account in-house, with no external validator? My guess is twofold. First, it provides a bit of distance and plausible deniability. They can say they’re **not** running a verified program in the traditional sense, they merely ensured that this account *linked* an account that just happens to be verified on a separate platform (with a separate owner, separate staff, and its own in-house verifications). Second, it may look more “convincing” than simply saying, “we verified them,” and it promotes an account that the holder wants to promote.

#### How Many Verified Accounts Exist (#3, both lists)

This is actually the easiest question to answer with something non-specific and/or noncommittal, like “this program is ongoing and we onboard new accounts everyday.” I’m confident Substack could provide a *numerical* answer that would be roughly accurate, rounded to the nearest hundred. But even with that, admitting that they’ve done this for over a hundred people would be a significant confession in itself.

Without a number, Substack can always fallback on the notion that it’s a special exception they’re only doing for a small handful of people. The observable reality shows that’s bullshit, but they could play pretend. As it stands in October 2026, I believe Substack has applied the verified badge to at least 150 accounts, with plans to add another 100 to 150 within the next 6 months. I base on this on their longstanding pattern of reaching out to pre-existing Notables and getting them to join up.

Most of the major Substack creators who had a media or entertainment job prior to opening up shop here, all the way back to the late 2010s and early 2020s, were contacted by Substack directly. Matt Taibbi, Matt Yglesias, (lotta Matts,) Heather Cox Richardson, so on and so forth. Even in my neck of the woods, Atlanta journalists were asked to join and given upfront “advances”—sort of like author advances in publishing—on their future bestseller revenue, which they’d split with Substack for the first year.

All the verified program did is take the money out. Notables come, get verified, and get to do whatever they want. If they choose to monetize, fine. If they don’t, or simply fail to hit the 100 paid sub minimum for the basic bestseller badge, no sweat. But I’m maybe jumping ahead to #4, so apologies. Let’s cover that next.

#### Problems the Verified Program Solves (#4, both lists)

As noted above, the Doyle episode was probably the catalyst, amongst a number of other things. Her arrival, with a paid tier, 217K subscribers total (most of them free and email list imports), and a big red carpet rollout from Substack corporate, rubbed some people wrong. That led to the dogpile backlash that fueled her departure only a week later. Substack wants people like Doyle. But they don’t want controversies like hers. They don’t want Notables to feel unwelcome. So their solve is removing money.

Like I mentioned in previous essays, [money sickness](https://edokwin.substack.com/p/artists-money-sickness-how-romanticizing?utm_source=publication-search) is endemic in arts spaces, where a creator charging *at all* can become a scandal. Critics framed Doyle’s debut as a “toxic” combination of celebrity and capitalism. Now let’s go back to those advances I mentioned. In 2021, Vox reported on a “Substack Pro” program and how it triggered a similar fight over money, audience share, strucural support, and who’s making it on a platform that ostensibly prides itself on an independent, community-led ethos.

McKenzie gave the Ankler an interview in June of 2025 dismissing outrage over celebrity arrivals as a “scarcity mindset.” I could say something about how leadership will talk about celebrities to friendly press and won’t answer a press inquiry about their own products, but let’s leave it aside. The point is, Substack wants celebrity users, and they’ll do all manner of things to get them. From upfront payments, to speed-running bestseller status, to these new verified badges. If celebs don’t want to charge for subscriptions, or fear blowback to doing so, the verified program is a cheap fix.

#### Why the Radio Silence? (#4, #5, & #6, first list)

This one’s kinda easy, given all the above. Substack is fundamentally unaccountable, and they certainly don’t like answering questions that will prove awkward or embarrassing. (Least of all to peons like yourself truly.) The verified program isn’t essential infrastructure, it doesn’t need to be outlined or delineated to the public, who won’t have access to it the way they do the bestseller badges or flower badges.

*Should* Substack be transparent? Sure. The same way oil companies shouldn’t pollute and industrial agriculture companies shouldn’t abuse land or animals. But reality is what it is. I can only report on what I find, not wave a magic wand to change it.

#### Verified Programs vs Values (last question, both lists)

I’ll be blunt as ever here. Let’s say the question is some variation of following: `did Substack renege on their bestseller badge promise from 2022?` The answer is obviously and indisputably “YES.” There’s no debating that point. All we have left to argue over is how we feel about that fact. I’ve said before that I’m not leaving anytime soon, certainly not over something like this. But that does raise another question: **how many of us feel like that, about a lot of stuff, and what does it do to us?** And to society, at scale? What happen when we all collectively shrug our shoulders at bad behavior? When we complain, in public or private, but do nothing otherwise?

Freddie deBoer, psycho tho he might be, warned Substack reps privately that some of the company’s recent moves would backfire. Even favored daughter Emily Sundberg has been iced out on occasion, when her question wasn’t conducive to the PR strategy that Substack wanted to push. I’m nowhere near the level of either person. I don’t have nearly the sunk costs. I could leave, and I might just yet, tho not over a badge.

Still, I know that [lying is immoral](https://edokwin.substack.com/p/the-virtues-of-radical-honesty?utm_source=publication-search), as is being unaccountable to your customers or the larger public. There are folks out in various corners of Substack-land (LitStack, PhilStack, etc) who think I’m an asshole for holding the line on certain things, like plagiarism, or facts, or basic human decency. It is funny to see what people will rationalize as “just how things are” vs what they’ll castigate as a mortal sin.

## Damned If They Do, and Why

Let me really build the defense. Substack’s strategy, even tho I disagree with it, has a logic to it. They need Notables to grow, and they need to grow as a way of making money. I don’t think they need infinite growth, and I don’t think their 2010s strategy should be their 2020s strategy, as regards creator acquisitions. But let’s make the case anyway, just so we can say we were fair to the counterarguments, come what may.

Substack recruits top talent, coordinates celebrity launches, and helps fund the initial runs of certain creators (or at least used to). This means the people they bring have a reason to join, to stay, and to prosper. That means more money, more visibility, and more social cachet for Substack, at least in the long run and on average. (Some will still leave or drift regardless of initial incentives.) Having celebrities helps, and programs or processes geared to their proliferation are good for “everyone” in a way.

I personally have, at least according to Substack, benefited from drafting energy, and subscribers, off the winds of bigger accounts like The Free Press and Jonathan Haidt. I can say definitively that accounts like Free Black Thought have helped me. Bigs help smalls insofar as bigs are longstanding presences and create or grow communities that wouldn’t scale otherwise. They’re attractors, and anchors, like having a major brand name at a mall. Come for the Weiss sisters, stay for some guy named Quinn?

### Problematic Program Payoffs

Now that we’ve setup something close enough to a steelman, let’s break it. First and foremost, the “Pro” program longitudinal results. Whilst the many creators who Substack paid came here, build out their brands, and stayed, we actually don’t know how many dipped after the checks stopped. I can tell you I know at least a few whose posting dropped off precipitously. Even with a bestseller badge, they weren’t motivated to keep up the output or the quality. Especially as Substack started reaching out to celebs who, unlike journos or authors, could bankroll themselves from the jump. “Pro” is largely a thing of the past now, and that’s a past-as-prologue sign.

Compare with verifieds. A common trajectory is that someone will join, post a few articles over a few weeks or months, then get bored and dip. Charli XCX—who, interestingly, chose to have her verified badge removed earlier this year—hasn’t posted since May of this year, and her post before that was in December 2025. Lizzo hasn’t dropped an article since March (tho she does post the occasional note, as recently as September). Other celebs, like Julia Louis-Dreyfus, turned on subscriptions and just earned a regular bestseller badge. Which is probably a net win overall.

Point being, most folks who are begged to come here will treat it like a fleeting trend and abandon it just as fast. Meanwhile, the program exists as an open secret and weird question mark, when a new entry in the Help Center page takes minutes to write and to explain. And hiding it to dodge backlash guarantees worse backlash later.

Most of all, I think the silence implies that Substack’s defense, such that they even have one formulated in their own minds, *wouldn’t survive daylight*. They certainly haven’t earned the benefit of the doubt, tho I did still try to see things from Substack’s perspective, and to outline the use cases and utility. Speaking of…

### Politician Particulars

Another reason for the program, which I nearly neglected, is that some of the folks Substack has started reaching out to might legitimately and non-neurotically want to avoid charging money for content, on ethical or professional grounds related to their industry. I’m talking, ironically enough, about politicians. All jokes about their morality aside, folks like Josh Shapiro, Bernie Sanders, and so on probably don’t want the optics headache of explaining why they’re charging money for their thoughts online.

I could say it’s a somewhat arbitrary concern relative to hawking books or getting board seats from companies they used to regulate, but still. Verification can serve as a useful stopgap measure that ensures accounts are “real” without needing to be paywalled. I can understand the desire to find a compromise in this way.

Politicians such as Buttigieg and Shapiro also do, in some cases, have the odd habit of staying longer and being more active than random celebs. So for that reason alone they seem worth catering to, even if doing so runs counter to previous policies.

## How the Program Works (a hypothesis)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bwEz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa18463a8-3543-4011-a88d-a1d702bf2dd5_1216x864.jpeg)

What I’m about to share is a mix of original research, disclosures I got from anonymous sources, and extrapolations from general industry norms. I won’t claim it’s “exactly” how the verified program works, but I’d be willing to bet it matches up well with what most verified account managers would admit if subpoenaed and called to testify under oath. (A scenario I admit is highly unlikely at present.)

**Step one:** Someone at Substack reaches out to the management team of a celebrity/Notable that isn’t here yet. A dialogue begins, leading to a firm commit that the named principal will “join” Substack (with their team managing the account day-to-day).

**Step Two:** A coordinated launch, with linked accounts and verification via whatever third party platform the team and Substack agree is best (could be Insta, Linkedin, or X). The badge tracks the fact that this person was verified off-platform, not that Substack verified them directly; even tho this distinction is, in practice, highly irrelevant, since a ton of backend coordination has already happened.

**Step Three:** Once the account is live and ongoing, the team and the principal work together to handle usage and posting duties. The specifics of who writes what, who replies to messages (both publicly and in DMs), who monitors metrics, et cetera, is all gonna vary by team. I am *not* alleging that the principals affirmatively ==don’t write their own content==. I *am* stating that content is probably a team effort, and might be transcribed. All of which is, again, super common and industry standard.

**Step Four:** Really more of a fork in the road moment. Fork Path A, let’s call it, is that I’d say anywhere from 60% or more of these verified accounts will, after around 6 months, stop being meaningfully active on the platform. That will vary by person, but the pattern is super durable at this point, and predates the verified program. Path B is something like “going commercial,” where the account turns on paid subscriptions and gets a white/orange badge instead of gray/silver. Path C is turning all badges off, like Charli XCX, which is super rare. (Notably, Paths A and C can intersect.)

The strongest framing question remaining might be: *who at Substack is telling intermediaries not to discuss it?* And the honest answer could be something like “it’s just understood.” Most of these account managers are already under contracts not to disclose their status as the “real” runner of a celebrity’s socials. They’re like a ghostwriter, an assistant, and confidant all in one. It would be bad optics if they were *too open* about who their clients are or what their operations look like. The verified badges just get folded into a larger code of silence the industry follows.

## Closing

I’ll say the boilerplate: “Substack did not substantively respond by the deadline,” not “Substack refused to comment.” Silence isn’t technically a refusal until the subject(s) say so explicitly, even if reasonable minds can make fair inferences. Like we looked at, maybe the pitchforks were coming for celebrity monetization, and a quiet checkmark felt safer than a public fight. But we gotta wonder why. And whether a platform that promised to put writers in charge should even get to settle that argument in the dark.

I gave them a week and every opening to explain. I think decent answers are possible, and I gave you some indications of what those would look like. But Substack still haven’t given us that. Silence like this? I think it’s more than caution, or uncertainty. I think it’s a strategy. And I’ll leave you to decide what you think of that strategy.
