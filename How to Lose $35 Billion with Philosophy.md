![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hhy7JUinlu0)

This tyro’s investing strategy looks more like gambling addiction than high finance. Aschenbrenner borrowed to the hilt, using four times leverage to buy AI stocks by the truckload. Then he added to his risk by shorting companies he was sure AI would replace. In other words, instead of hedging his AI position—which is standard practice (and why they call them *hedge* funds), he doubled down, setting himself up for a failure of Biblical proportions.

When you take chances like this, even a small downturn in AI stocks will wipe out your entire capital. That’s pretty much what happened last week.

I wanted to learn more about the young man behind this collapse—so I went to his Wikipedia page. There I discovered his connection to fraudster Sam Bankman-Fried and the latter’s Effective Altruism philosophy.

But, of course, none of this is coincidence.

I [wrote about Effective Altruism back in 2023](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/why-i-ran-away-from-philosophy-because), and shared my own history with consequentialist ethical systems of this sort.

---

I really could have pursued a career in philosophy—as hard as that is to believe. You’re asking: Can anybody get hired in that field? But as a young man, I managed to beat out a dozen Rhode Scholars when I took a first in the subject at Oxford. I even got exempted from the oral exams on the strength of my performance. I could have parlayed that into a PhD and a sweet teaching gig.

[Lord Quinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Quinton), president of my Oxford college and a famous philosopher in his own right, was the first person to congratulate me. But I could tell he was a little shocked. He knew me as an eccentric jazz musician from Los Angeles, and here I was making a mark in analytic philosophy.

![Oxford exam results for Ted Gioia](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nbMI!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8ea582ed-35e6-4f97-a41d-c1f9814716a5_934x770.png)

At age 23, I got my name in The Guardian for my first in PPE—pretty good for a working class kid from Hawthorne—but my encounters with consequentialist ethical systems soured me on philosophy

But I walked away from all this—because I‘d grown disillusioned with analytical philosophy, and especially the misguided ethical systems that were at the core of my studies. These were the predecessors of Effective Altruism. That term didn’t exist back then, but the worldview did—these systems are all variants of utilitarianism, a starry-eyed moral calculus invented by Jeremy Bentham two hundred years ago.

By the way, Bentham also invented the [Panopticon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panopticon), a prison concept that is the model for today’s surveillance capitalism.

That isn’t a coincidence either. This worldview is not only susceptible to abuses, it practically invites them.

That’s because the essence of Effective Altruism and other utilitarian spinoffs is an unshakable conviction that ends justify the means. The practitioner is allowed to do the most manipulative and destructive things today—in pursuit of a more perfect world tomorrow.

I could give many examples. In my 2023 article I showed how an Effective Altruist would justify selling granny to human traffickers.

> Effective altruists don’t look at the actual actions at hand or their consequences today—hah, that would be too obvious. They only think about *long-term holistic results*, and hope to maximize pleasure and good feelings in the aggregate:
> 
> So it stands to reason that:
> 
> 1. Granny is old and doesn’t have long to live, so she can’t experience much pleasure even under the best circumstances.
> 2. But the sex traffickers could use Granny to increase the pleasure of many of their customers.
> 3. Hence….
> 
> I’m not going to spell it out for you, but you can guess where this is heading.
> 
> You just better hope that, if you’re ever a grandparent, your progeny aren’t Effective Altruists.

This may sound like a crazy and unlikely example. But the reality is that I can use the tenets of Effective Altruism to justify almost any terrible move today—provided I promise a sufficiently high payoff in the future.

The scariest thing about Effective Altruists is that they tend to seek out the *highest* payoff. They’re typically obsessed with ethical behavior as an *optimization scheme*. As a result, this philosophy nurtures a narcissistic savior complex among its advocates. Just treating people kindly and decently today is too boring for them—they’re seeking much bigger results than that.

Do you think I’m exaggerating? Well, check out t [his revelation about Sam Bankman-Fried from his trial](https://www.businessinsider.com/sam-bankman-fried-coin-flip-destroy-world-caroline-ellison-trial-2023-10):

> Imagine, if you will, a coin.
> 
> If you flip it and get heads, the entire world improves by more than double.
> 
> If you get tails, the world is destroyed.
> 
> Sam Bankman-Fried said he would flip the coin—and urged everyone else to do so, too, Caroline Ellison testified in court Tuesday.

That’s almost a perfect example of how Effective Altruists think. You combine all these three key elements: (1) Willingness to run risks (or even destroy) on a massive scale, (2) Grand dreams of world improvement, and (3) Total vagueness when it comes to details.

Aschenbrenner’s investing strategy sounds a lot like SBF’s coin clip, doesn’t it? There’s tremendous irony in the fact that he called his hedge fund Situational Awareness—which is the one thing he definitely lacked.

Let me repeat, none of this is happenstance or just bad luck. Whenever you see stupid or terrible things justified by the *possibility* of future benefits, you should be on the lookout for consequentialist ethics.

I remember how shocked I was when, as an undergraduate, I heard a prominent intellectual defend Pol Pot’s genocidal executions in Cambodia. His justification was essentially an Effective Altruist one. The ends justify the means. Or, in popular parlance, if you want to make an omelette you need to break some eggs—around a million of them in the case of Pol Pot.

This craziness in justifying trade-offs was evident from the very beginnings of Utilitarianism. Bentham talked about making decisions based on a *calculus of pleasure*. By my measure that’s also how Bill Cosby and Harvey Weinstein made their choices. The only difference is that their exploits don’t get taught at Oxford.

I shouldn’t have to say all this—because my common sense musings ought to be obvious to everybody. You don’t sell Granny to traffickers under any circumstances, because she’s Granny. How hard is that to figure out? But we sadly live in a world where this is no longer obvious—at least not to a surprisingly large number of people.

Instead we’re all living in a broken world where ends-justify-the-means thinking has gone mainstream. So I’d guess that almost one-third of voters now embrace it to some degree. They will even put democracy and human rights at risk in pursuit of their world-improving plans for the future.

---

If I were giving a public lecture on this subject, somebody would interrupt me at this point and ask: “Okay, wise guy, if you don’t like Effective Altruism, what can you offer that’s any better?”

I have lots of answers to this. There are plenty of better ethical systems than EA. I won’t bore you with the details, but you would be much better off with Kant’s categorical imperative, or Aristotle’s virtue ethics, or Thomistic natural law, or (this will shock you) the Ten Commandments.

Even Kurt Vonnegut had a better option when he said: “There’s only one rule that I know of, babies—God damn it, you’ve got to be kind.”

I’ll close by putting it as simply as I can:

1. If you want to do good, you start by doing good.
2. You don’t pursue it as a *consequence* in the distant future, but in the *reality* of your actions today.
3. Your efforts don’t focus on on some abstract goal, but directly on helping the people around you and improving the places where you live and work.

You don’t need a philosophy degree or membership in a college Effective Altruism club to understand this stuff. It’s all pretty basic. That’s how a moral code should be—not just for elites but accessible to all.

If you can’t manage those three things, all your speculations about a calculus of pleasure and imaginary coin flips are worthless.
