Below I continue my survey of 50 essential western films. ([Click here for part one](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/the-50-essential-western-films).) I’m sharing these in chronological order. In this installment, I cover films released from 1955 through 1968.

It was a strange period for cowboy movies. The mass audience would soon abandon the genre, turning instead to space operas, disaster films, and superhero movies. The whole idiom sometimes now felt like a tired string of clichés—almost the antithesis of the free and untamed ethos that had originally made the Wild West so appealing.

But this shift in preferences also allowed—or even required—directors to take more chances when dealing with western themes. So in some ways, this end-of-an-era mentality produced many of the most innovative films in the genre’s history.

### Bad Day at Black Rock (1955)

This film is as tightly constructed as a Greek tragedy, and even abides by the three Aristotelian unities of time, space, and action. The entire drama plays out over the course of a single day in Black Rock, a small desert outpost, where a World War II vet named John Macreedy (Spencer Tracy) attempts to repay a favor to a Japanese soldier who saved his life on the battlefield. But he finds himself unexpectedly caught up in a criminal investigation, with the entire town conspiring against him.

The film features five past or future Oscar winners—Tracy, Ernest Borgnine, Lee Marvin, Walter Brennan and Dean Jagger—who between them would eventually earn eight Academy Awards for acting. That’s essentially half of the cast, and it ensures a gripping ensemble performance rarely surpassed on screen.

![Movie poster](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WER4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F118549e2-7662-4330-a687-b97ca15f24f8_1000x1481.jpeg)

Movie poster

Together they create a slowly building tension finally resolved in a fiery desert confrontation. The widescreen CinemaScope technology is artfully employed, and André Previn makes full use of its stereophonic capability for his brash, electrifying score. This film has been unfairly forgotten, but rewards repeated viewings.

### The Searchers (1956)

John Wayne was often criticized for playing the same role over and over. But that’s not entirely fair. As we’ve already seen with *Red River* (1948), he sometimes took parts that undercut the simple heroism of his public image, defying the standard formulas of the genre.

In *The Searchers*, Wayne plays a Civil War veteran on a mission to find his niece, abducted by Comanches. But he grows obsessed with vengeance and hatred—no doubt shocking moviegoers unready for this ignoble side of the Duke. Yet *The Searchers* undercuts its disruptive story lines with the sheer beauty of its delivery. Director John Ford, taking full advantage of Technicolor technology, never made a more visually stunning film.

Even from that first scene, when the cabin door opens, you know you’re in for a magical experience. It’s like that other Hollywood doorway leading to Oz, or the journey to Narnia from the back of a closet. You almost feel as if you’re leaving your seat in the cinema and entering a spectacle from a different time and place.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PWrvXvW-VHw)

### The Tall T (1957)

Ford and Wayne had new rivals in late 1950s, when director Budd Boetticher and actor Randolph Scott enjoyed box office success with a series of classic westerns. They collaborated on seven films, sometimes called the Ranown Cycle (named after Scott’s production company), that deserve a better fate than their current obscurity.

During a stagecoach heist, a gang takes a newlywed couple hostage, along with a struggling rancher (played by Scott). As they await the payment of the ransom, it becomes increasingly clear that the kidnappers will kill their captives once they get the money. The story is compact and nuanced, with more psychological gamesmanship than shoot-em-up theatrics, and Scott is like a chessplayer as he anticipates each move of his adversaries.

*The Tall T* screenplay was written with Wayne in mind, but he had to pass due to his commitment to *The Searchers*. Scott was an ideal replacement, with a stoic and ruminative demeanor well suited for this plot—in which he spends most of the film held captive by desperados. Wayne would have bristled, with little chance to swagger, but low-key Mr. Scott feels right at home.

Some have claimed that this actor’s best moments came when he was standing still—perhaps that’s why they call him the “Granite Jaw.” A decade later, Clint Eastwood would hone a similar onscreen stance, and ride it to global stardom. But you can already see the playbook for this laconic approach in *The Tall T.*

### Rio Bravo (1959)

I still don’t know why this movie is named *Rio Bravo*. That brave river isn’t mentioned until the song over the closing credits. But don’t let that stop you from enjoying this reworking of a timeless story—namely, a town that isn’t big enough for its two feuding parties.

John Wayne played this role so many times it’s second nature to him. But he is assisted by an unusual crew, including two singers (Dean Martin and Ricky Nelson) serving as deputy sheriffs. But Walter Brennan almost steals the show, and reminds us why he earned three different Oscars in a supporting role (still a record today).

### The Magnificent Seven (1960)

The western genre would lose most of its audience during the 1960s—after studios saturated the market, both in film and on TV, with too much formulaic product. But in 1960 that humiliation was all in the future, and gunslingers still strutted and preened through those decrepit sets like movie stars.

Of course, it helped if real movie stars signed on as cast members, and *The Magnificent Seven* delivered the goods with a high testosterone lineup—including Steve McQueen, Charles Bronson, James Coburn and Yul Brynner.

The story is based on Kurosawa’s *The Seven Samurai,* but its translation to the Wild West feels natural and un-derivative. Audiences loved the spectacle, but a lot of credit goes to composer Elmer Bernstein who delivered one of the greatest theme songs of the era (although he’s lucky Aaron Copland couldn’t sue back in [those days before](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-today/summer-2015/coda/richard-niles) *[vibe](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-today/summer-2015/coda/richard-niles)* [and](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-today/summer-2015/coda/richard-niles) *[feel](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-today/summer-2015/coda/richard-niles)* [were copyrightable](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-today/summer-2015/coda/richard-niles)).

This kind of simple heroic western would not survive much longer, but it really was magnificent while it lasted.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yulmgTcGLZw)

### The Alamo (1960)

We are now in the final stages of the western film as American myth-making. This Homeric ambition reaches its peak with *The Alamo* (1960) and *How the West Was Won* (1962). But legend-building films of this sort wouldn’t survive the decade, and it inevitably got harder to separate heroes from villains in all those shootouts and saloon brawls. Myth would get replaced by moral relativism, or even outright nihilism.

But in 1960 you could still showcase John Wayne as Davy Crockett and put him alongside Frankie Avalon, Richard Widmark, and Laurence Harvey in a depiction of the biggest Texas border controversy of all time.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3MuC!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7850c1d3-7ea5-49d1-aa29-5d711c49edf5_580x900.jpeg)

### The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance (1962)

Can you really build a Western film around a hero who doesn’t like guns and shootouts, and wants to resolve everything peacefully in a courtroom—where nobody gets hurt?

That’s a great role for Jimmy Stewart, and we love him for his high principles. But he is lucky to have John Wayne as his friend. That’s because Liberty Valance (played by Lee Marvin) is also in town, and Mr. V doesn’t care much for rule of law. Somebody will have to take out that bad boy. The only question (as the film title hints) is who pulls the trigger.

### How the West Was Won (1962)

In terms of star power, this was the most ambitious western ever filmed. The poster boasts a cast of “24 great stars” in a spectacle that aimed to match the epic scope of *Ben Hur*. The movie was filmed with the innovative widescreen Cinerama process, and I’ve had the opportunity to see it on the huge curved screen required by these panoramic productions. So I can attest firsthand to its visual delights.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MM_UUB6J-Fw)

The best scenes are breathtaking, and you can’t complain about a film that serves up John Wayne, Henry Fonda, Gregory Peck, Debbie Reynolds, James Stewart and Lee J. Cobb, among others, with narration by Spencer Tracy. Despite that, the grandeur of the landscape often steals the show at the expense of continuity in the storytelling.

### Hud (1963)

How can the wild and open West ever feel claustrophobic? But that’s the degraded state of western living in *Hud*, set in a dead-end Texas town where the only future is in plotting an escape. Those who stay behind waste their days in mindless pursuits, singing folk songs at a dilapidated movie theater or roping greased pigs at a Kiwanis club meetup.

The title character Hud, played by Paul Newman, looks on this decline with despair—aggravated by too much boozing, brawling, and balling. His only hope is inheriting his father’s ranch, but now hoof-and-mouth disease has put the herd in quarantine, and the state veterinarian orders its slaughter. That’s almost a symbol for the entire film—which undermines every ingredient of the great myth of the open West.

![Movie poster](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!n3xn!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F261cae06-297d-44a9-ae5d-12e74bb48d61_732x1000.jpeg)

Movie poster

The future for Texas is now oil drilling—a dirty business where there’s no place for cowboys and their independent ways. Hud realizes this and is willing to make the necessary compromises, but his father holds on to romantic notions from the past. Those give him moral strength, but won’t pay the bills. And in this film, every bill comes due sooner or later.

The film is based on a novel by Larry McMurtry, who started writing it when he was still a student at the University of North Texas. It’s hard to imagine a bleaker depiction of small town Texas life. But McMurty himself matched it five years later when he published *The Last Picture Show*, which served as the source of another soul-shaking anti-Western movie.

It’s hard to believe that, just a few months earlier, the same theaters had showcased *How the West Was Won*. But it’s now 1963, the President would soon get shot, and films would change more in the next five years than they had in the previous three decades. For the first time ever, dark or even fatalistic films like *Hud* would draw large audiences.

Is this the kind of western we deserve? I’m still not sure—but it’s the kind we would now get.

### A Fistful of Dollars (1964)

The western genre needed fresh juice—but who expected that it would come from Italy? Or that it would arrive on the scene with a low budget production and a (then) little known actor named Clint Eastwood. Give credit to Sergio Leone, who cut through all the sentimentality and romance of Hollywood, and delivered a rough and tough new style of cowboy for quite a few dollars less.

That wasn’t the original plan. Eight different actors were considered for the lead role. But the big names declined so Leone had to pick an unproven film actor. The director later quipped that Clint “only had two expressions: with hat and no hat.” But that’s why the movie was so impactful. That detached, emotionless guise haunted audiences, and spurred countless imitations.

As I wrote in [my 2025 assessment of Eastwood](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/is-this-the-dominant-personality):

> Just think of all those Arnold Schwarzenegger movies where the dialogue became famous because it was delivered so mechanically. Or consider Anton Chigurh in *No Country for Old Men* —a performance praised by experts for its authentic portrayal of a psychopath. Or even Bryan Cranston in *Breaking Bad*, who became more emotionless and detached with each passing season.
> 
> After Eastwood, this exact same playbook worked for both heroes and villains. An extreme example is *Terminator 2* —where both the good guy (Schwarzenegger) and bad guy (a T-1000 killer robot) battle to see who can achieve the most expressionless persona.
> 
> But the defining villain of this style remains Darth Vader. Eastwood had a face like a mask, according to Leone, but Darth Vader wears a literal mask. Not only can’t you see his face, but you aren’t even allowed to hear his natural voice—which has been processed to sound as inhuman as possible.

That Eastwood stance might even be described as the dominant personality type of our time. That’s probably not a good thing in the real world, but it works like a charm in the *reel* world—as the later evolution of the Western would make clear.

### Cat Ballou (1965)

Susan Sontag had just published her essay “Notes on Camp,” and now Hollywood introduces the campy western. You can tell from the very opening that this is a different take on the Wild West—when the statuesque woman in the Columbia Pictures logo morphs into a gun-toting animation of Cat Ballou (as played by Joan Fonda).

Everything here breaks the mold, from the garish colors to the strolling musicians who keep intruding on the action to sing “The Ballad of Cat Ballou.” In a surprising twist, tough guy Lee Marvin provides the best comic touches (in two separate roles)—and earned an Oscar for his efforts. This style of western never caught on, but there’s no reason why it couldn’t still delight audiences today.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdq8s4UrvYs)

### For a Few Dollars More (1965)

After the surprising success of *A Fistful of Dollars*, Sergio Leone brings Clint Eastwood back for an encore—and makes even more dollars from the second film in his trilogy. All the Leone trademarks show up on screen: the sweaty faces, the long staredowns before the shootout, the sparse dialogue badly dubbed, and a society strangely devoid of women, who are rarely seen and never heard. All this is underpinned by another outstanding Morricone score, complete with bullet percussion (provided by an actual Winchester rifle).

If it wasn’t so violent, Leone’s film might seem campy. But so many desperados get killed in this film, that Eastwood struggles to count them all at the end. But in the mid-1960s, Americans had gotten used to random violence in the news, and perhaps welcomed a film that made no pretenses about the scales of justice (although the *New York Times* griped, in its review, about the movie’s “killer bravado” and “glee in frantic manifestations of death”).

As the title indicates, everything here has a price, and the drama moves ahead as two bounty hunters (played by Eastwood and Lee Van Cleef) track down a sadistic bank robber, both of them looking for a big payoff. They decide to collaborate—but can these two protagonists really trust each other in this Nietzschean version of the Wild West?

### Django (1966)

The story here follows the same plot as *A Fistful of Dollars*. A mysterious stranger arrives in a town where two rival gangs fight for dominance, and finds himself at the center of an escalating conflict. But both directors borrowed this tale from Akira Kurosawa’s *Yojimbo* (1961). Even so, director Sergio Corbucci adds his own special twists to *Django*, most notably a lavish coffin that the title character drags with him wherever he goes.

I won’t tell you what’s in the coffin, but it’s my favorite plot reveal in the entire spaghetti western genre. Few people, however, got to enjoy it when *Django* was first released. The film’s excessive violence (by mid-1960s standards) prevented widespread distribution in the US, and led to an outright ban in the UK—which wasn’t lifted until 1993. By today’s standards, the visuals aren’t quite so disturbing, but controversy will continue to follow this film until the end of time.

Despite these constraints, *Django* has enjoyed an impressive afterlife. More than thirty later films tried to capitalize on a protagonist named Django—most notably Quentin Tarantino’s *Django Unchained* from 2012. For a long time, it seemed like half the westerns from Italy featured characters named Django or Ringo (both drawn from musicians, in an odd twist). But this is the granddaddy of them all, with a tightly controlled plot and abundance of mud and blood. Even Leone’s sweaty films feel clean by comparison.

### The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (1966)

The three lead actors play, respectively, the Good (Clint Eastwood), the Bad (Lee Van Cleef), and the Ugly (Eli Wallah). But we’re really splitting hairs here. None of these dudes can be trusted—especially by each other. Their only motivation is greed, as they search for $200,00 in missing gold. People who get in the way are killed without hesitation or remorse.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IFNUGzCOQoI)

If the Wild West is the great American myth, then Sergio Leone is the myth-buster. Maybe we needed an outsider, born in Rome, to demolish our ideals so ruthlessly. There are no heroes here. There’s not even a hint of romance—good luck trying to find a woman who gets any dialogue in this three hour film. There’s only crossing, double-crossing and—in the final dramatic scene—triple-crossing.

The western genre may not have been over at this point, but its comfortable formulas were as dead as a failed gunslinger after Leone. The next generation would have to take extreme measures to resuscitate it.

### The Big Gundown (1967)

The spaghetti western reached its peak in the work of three Sergios—Sergio Leone, Sergio Corbucci, and Sergio Sollima. The latter had an unconventional career, fighting in the Italian Resistance during the war, and later making an uncharacteristic ascent from movie critic to film director. Sollima was an inspired storyteller, but enjoyed the least visibility of the trio, largely because he only made three westerns over the course of two years, and then moved on.

But *The Big Gundown* ranks among the finest films about frontier justice, reversing many of the familiar tropes. A bounty hunter named Jonathan Corbett ( Lee Van Cleef), tries to bring to justice the fleeing Mexican criminal Cuchillo (played with scene-stealing skill by Tomas Milian), but is constantly thwarted in a protracted cat-and-mouse game. Cuchillo is easy to underestimate. At first glance he seems an unwitting buffoon, but we gradually learn that he is the ultimate trickster, always a step ahead of his over-confident adversary.

### Once Upon a Time in the West (1968)

If Wild West films were Wagnerian operas they would look like this movie. Characters stride across the screen as mythopoeic deities heading for some Valhalla of the desert. Desperadoes stalk their victims like avenging Valkyries. And Charles Bronson keeps playing that four-note harmonica melody as if it’s his own Wagnerian motif.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lyuwBW9lNa8)

Of course, the length is also Wagnerian. *Once Upon a Time in the West* clocks in at close to three hours—almost unprecedented for a genre built on quick thrills and fast action. But I wouldn’t cut any of it. This is a film of grand sweeping gestures, but also small details—you won’t find another cowboy movie which dwells so lovingly on clocks and boots and weapons and the like. And, most of all, hot sweaty faces.

Despite the excessive violence, this is a love letter from Sergio Leone to the American West of his own imagination. It’s a shame that America itself received it with such hostility. But *Once Upon a Time in the West* gradually earned its place as a classic, praised by Scorsese, Tarantino, and others who mined its tropes for their own fertile works.
