Well into the second half of my life (given actuarial tables for a non-smoking, non-drinking, white male whose parents lived to be 90+), I have had an epiphany, or more to the point, a series of epiphanies, that leave me in a different place than before they occurred.

I have spent my life with a preference for conservatism. My adherence to a politics of limited government, free-markets, individual liberty, and strong national defense has been consistent and deeply set. It still is. I think it will always be.

Alongside those policy preferences—but likely always just a tad behind in importance—came my affinity for what I considered to be my sense of the trustworthiness of the person, their embrace of principle. Very difficult to discern (especially in someone without a track record), but for someone WITH a track record, taking principled stands mattered to me a great deal. Obviously, when I agreed with the principle behind the stand, I considered the act even more worthy.

The final trait—personality—was important to me, but less so than the first two. Is this person someone I would want to work with, go for a beer with, have as a fraternity brother or in my wardroom, or want to have join my family in some manner.

Let’s take a look at two successful politicians and apply these traits/tests to them. Barack Obama comes to mind here. On trait one (policy), we had vast, deep disagreements, which is why I voted against him twice. On trait two—I had no reason to believe he was unprincipled, but nor did I see him as an exemplar. He was fine. Trait three? Off the charts. He seemed like a good hang. He loved his wife and daughters. He’s sometimes just plain funny.

Now, let’s do George W. Bush. This is easy. Knocks it out of the park on all three, which is why it was easy to vote for him twice. That said, POLICY agreement was the thing that had to be there. No ifs, ands, or buts.

This election and the appearance of a number of VERY LIKEABLE (Democratic) candidates with whom I had policy differences (and of course, the Trump era) has caused me to re-evaluate the priority I place on these traits. Bottom line? Policy differences mean far less to me than they once did.

Here’s an example. Senator-Elect El-Sayed in Michigan. I would not have voted for him. I probably could not vote for him. But I began to think about his stance on “Medicare for All”, a POLICY stance with which I have had great disagreement. And then I did a little soul-searching.

I get my medical care through what are essentially socialized means (Tricare and VA). My medical care has ALWAYS been socialized to some extent, but it was easier to swallow philosophically as a condition of employment. So I began to think about socialized medicine in this country. I think it is a bad idea with a ton of bad incentives. But what we have now leaves a lot of people dissatisfied, and I have to believe there are approaches that are not the USSR or the status quo. There are MANY countries I respect and in which I LOVE to be, that maintain highly socialized medical systems. The residents do not appear to be breaking rocks in the hot sun.

Now back to American politics. I’ve had a change of mind on healthcare, and that is, I am not willing to let support for a more socialized system be a knockout punch anymore. Because I don’t want to let ANY policy disagreement (or agreement) be so important that it allows me to overlook the unprincipled (or not highly principled) and the anti-social. I reserve the right to rule out candidates with policy preferences that are obviously unconstitutional, but things that fall within the playing field of politics? Game on.

Because I have lived under a government of the unprincipled and the anti-social, I know now how vile this can be. And so the next epiphany is…

I will vote for a person going forward based on my understanding of who that person is. That understanding will be informed by their policy stances, but will not be dominated by them. I will prefer conservatives, but I will vote for someone not conservative if I find them to be of good cheer and high honor, and I will allow their policy preferences—even those I abhor—to win or lose in the political process. The “ [Buckley Rule](https://www.nationalreview.com/corner/re-buckley-rule-avik-roy/) ” (support the most viable conservative candidate)—by which I have largely lived since 1984—no longer works for me.

### “Jimmy”

Friend of 25 years General Dan and I went to see the new Jimmy Stewart biopic the other night, and it was exceptional. No, I don’t think it will make anyone’s Best Picture list, but for those who admire Jimmy Stewart for his service and character (see above), this movie delivers.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4nrB_T3F53Y)

Most importantly, if you are a fan (or in my case, a fanatic) of the movie “It’s A Wonderful Life”, you will LOVE this movie. Most fanatics of the movie already knew of Stewart’s heroic war service, his difficulty in transitioning back after the war, and the degree to which his war service colored his approach to acting and his performance. But we’ve never SEEN it. We’ve never seen a movie that so artfully weaves his service into the background music of the performance. The man playing Stewart NAILS the voice, not in a mimicking, comedic way, but with care and emotion.

I once wrote about a movie called “The Passion of the Christ”, as part of an argument that the film was virtually unreviewable. Here’s part of the argument:

> A believer seeing "The Passion of the Christ" could not help but be moved, could not help but dive deeply into one's own spirituality and religiosity, could not help but walk out of the theater not thinking that they had just watched a movie--but that they had just had an experience. Someone in this condition could not possibly write a fair and dispassionate review of the movie--I knew then that I could not that night--and to this day, I would be unable to.  
>   
> To the unbeliever, "The Passion of the Christ" was weird--the Aramaic and Latin, the over the top brutality, the subtle and not so subtle antisemitism, these elements would conspire to work against a positive review. That said, what would virtually seal the deal--is the near certainty that an unbeliever simply would not get it. Their non-belief, their hostility to the Bible story, their blatant irreligiousness--whatever the case may be--rendered them incapable of understanding what it was about or why what he or she was seeing might move those around him or her.

To an obviously lesser extent, “Jimmy” is the same thing. If you don’t love the greatest movie ever made with the heat of 10,000 suns, you cannot possibly understand why “Jimmy” is so wonderful. If you do love “It’s a Wonderful Life”, you cannot possibly be even-handed.
