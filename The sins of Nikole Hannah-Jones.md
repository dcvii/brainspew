Last week, Nikole Hannah-Jones wrote the bombshell essay, “ [In fighting for every black child, did I betray my own?](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/20/magazine/ny-public-schools-black-students-segregation.html),” about how she deliberately kept her daughter in a terrible black school as a kind of political experiment. And just to shelve the opening question, yes, Hannah-Jones, you absolutely did betray your daughter. You also betrayed the brave generations of black parents who fought, marched, sued, and sometimes literally risked their lives for the simple right to send their children to better schools. Those parents fought all the way to the Supreme Court to get their kids out of inferior, segregated classrooms, and you inherited that hard-won freedom. You were [bused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikole_Hannah-Jones) to predominantly white schools in Waterloo, Iowa, through a voluntary desegregation program, and by your own account, that education is what allowed you to flourish. Then, like a spoiled brat who doesn’t appreciate what her parents gave her, you threw it away, choosing educational deprivation for your own child as a demonstration of your virtue. Only someone who never had to fight for every scrap of opportunity could treat it with such disdain. You are a narcissistic black elite who has made a fortune larping as a member of the oppressed class, and you arrogantly believed you could discard those benefits for the sake of the greater community, or your own vanity project, or whatever the hell this was.

You, Nikole Hannah-Jones, came up in a world, at least in the waters you swim in, where being black confers a kind of automatic authority, the doctoral expertise of lived experience, and where institutions offer nothing but affirmations, step-ladders, and cushions. You have only ever known a world in which blacks are unfairly advantaged in academia, where failing to even speak grammatical English is [celebrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebonics_\(word\)). American universities had explicitly considered race in undergraduate admissions for roughly half a century by the time the Supreme Court [ended racist admissions](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf) in 2023. Harvard’s racist policies [roughly quadrupled](https://www.the74million.org/article/court-documents-racial-preferences-massively-boost-black-hispanic-applicants/) a black applicant’s odds of admission compared with a white applicant with similar academic credentials. At the University of North Carolina, where you went to school, out-of-state black applicants in the middle academic tier were [33 times](https://www.the74million.org/article/court-documents-racial-preferences-massively-boost-black-hispanic-applicants/) more likely to be admitted than equally qualified Asian Americans. Off campus, [more than half](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2023/05/17/diversity-equity-and-inclusion-in-the-workplace/) of American workers say their workplace now holds DEI trainings or meetings, some of which are [cartoonishly racist](https://x.com/wesyang/status/2104650027985682648?s=20) and unlawful.

Maybe you assumed the racist cushion you have helped stitch would catch your daughter, too. Or maybe, despite cosplaying as someone who opposes classist segregation, you rolled the dice knowing you could yank the parachute cord at any time. As you put it, “Her father and I knew that we could provide for her tutoring and any other assistance to make up for what her school may have lacked.” But you couldn’t. As I [wrote](https://substack.com/@volodzko/note/c-343355159?utm_source=notes-share-action&r=qna) on Notes:

> I would never sacrifice my child to my politics. The one thing you’re supposed to do as a parent is give your child the best opportunity you can in life. Not risk their future so you can run a political experiment on them. What kind of person decides to make their own daughter go slumming so they can wash themselves in the moral glow? And then pimp them out for content? Good on her for owning the mistake, I suppose, for whatever that’s worth. But as a parent who actually works hard to give his child everything he can in life, I cannot imagine the thought of this ever entering my mind.

But I want to address something I have not seen anyone online address. I want to address the actual logic behind Hannah-Jones’ decision not to send her daughter to a white, affluent school. Instead, she put her child in pre-K at P.S. 307, the Daniel Hale Williams school in Brooklyn’s Vinegar Hill neighborhood, a high-poverty public school where almost all the students are black and Latino.

P.S. 307 performs extremely poorly on standardized tests. In 2024, only [28%](https://www.schooldigger.com/go/NY/schools/0009102811/school.aspx) of students met the state standard in English and 27% in math. In 2023, it was only 17% in English, against a state average of 49%, and 27% in math against 52%. Out of 4,346 schools in the state, P.S. 307 ranks [4,031st](https://www.publicschoolreview.com/p-s-307-daniel-hale-williams-profile). In other words, the school Hannah-Jones chose for her daughter is one of the worst in New York. It wasn’t always, which I’ll get to shortly. But making matters worse, she actually campaigned against a “yuppie invasion” of kids from whiter areas into her daughter’s failing school. Here’s *The New York Post* ’s [report](https://nypost.com/2026/09/25/us-news/nikole-hannah-jones-campaigned-against-a-yuppie-invasion-of-kids-from-whiter-areas-to-failing-school-they-sent-daughter/):

> She and her husband crusaded against letting more students from affluent backgrounds into their daughter’s struggling, mostly-black Brooklyn elementary school — while decrying New York’s hopelessly “segregated” public school system … Hannah-Joneses stood firmly against a Department of Education rezoning initiative, which seemed poised to flood their kid’s school with local tots from wealthy backgrounds. This despite the couple themselves being well-off non-native New Yorkers living in Bed-Stuy in a completely different school district. At the time, nearby public school PS 8 was overflowing with students, at 148% capacity and had a multi-year waitlist. PS 307 … was underutilized, at just 50% capacity.

The change would have relieved the overcrowding at P.S. 8, desegregated both schools, and brought together kids whose paths might otherwise never cross, bridging a gap between rich and poor, black and white. It would also likely have brought more resources, more funding, and more parental clout, the very things that could have helped lift those poor black and Latino kids up, the very things Hannah-Jones claims she put her own child there to accomplish. As Hannah-Jones herself [bragged](https://x.com/marcportermagee/status/2102133202882044109): “A *New York Times* reporter in a high-poverty school fucking changes the school, right? Like, there are certain things that just are not gonna happen in that school. I can go to that school and say there is a problem and it gets fixed immediately.”

But if you read her essay, you find that this was arrogant bluster. She did not have the power to transform the school, and the result of her political experiment is that she badly harmed her own daughter, who is now a teenager scrambling to fill in math she should have learned in elementary school. She also harmed all the other kids at P.S. 307, the very kids she claimed she was helping, by sacrificing her own child, by working against desegregation and against more parents just like her coming into the same school, something that might have actually produced the result she claimed she wanted. Yet in her recent essay, she writes, “We had connections and time and status that should not matter, but that we all know do. And more than that, we believed strongly that if families like ours went into schools like this one, it would challenge other families with similar values to do the same.”

The incoming school, the *Post* reports, “had rapidly become one of the borough’s best and most desirable.” But Hannah-Jones’ husband, Faraji, was furious and opposed the plan. If this was desegregation, and from a highly successful school, the academic benefit to students at P.S. 307 could have been revolutionary. So why oppose it? Well, at the time, 85% of P.S. 8’s pupils were white. Faraji even said of incoming, affluent parents — right after she bragged about the change affluent parents like her can make happen — “When those affluent parents come in, we do not want to alienate the parents who live across the street in the Farragut.”

Listen to how she justified it. She didn’t want to “alienate” black Farragut parents by having them send their black kids to a school with white kids. Writing to the *Post* this month, she explains she wanted to “slow the process down to ensure parents had input.” But Americans have heard that language before. In 1956, 101 Southern congressmen signed the [Southern Manifesto](https://www.crmvet.org/docs/560312_southern_manifesto.pdf), warning that desegregation was “destroying the amicable relations between the white and Negro races” and that “parents should not be deprived by government of the right to direct the lives and education of their own children.” The segregationists who fought *Brown v. Board of Education* rarely said they wanted to keep black kids out. They said they wanted to go slow, protect community harmony, and give parents a voice. The Manifesto did not attack blacks. Instead, it spoke of keeping little white children in an environment that “confirmed their habits, traditions, and way of life,” meaning of course, a white way of life. Agree or disagree, that is the heart of the segregationist position, and as you read on, I’d like you to ask yourself whether Hannah-Jones truly wants desegregation, as she claims, or simply a black version of the exact same thing I just quoted.

Not only did Hannah-Jones and her husband work to ensure that the kids at P.S. 307 would keep suffering, she then claimed she did it for noble reasons. But according to Ebony Richardson, a former PTA chair and current volunteer at the school who is quoted by the *Post*, Hannah-Jones did the opposite. Instead of making sure parents had input, she acted precisely like the stereotype of the wealthy, pushy white parent she claimed to oppose. “They were trying to take over the school,” says Richardson. “Things that couldn’t go their way, they felt, ‘We’re going to write it down and start trouble.’ They were imposing their will on the school.” Now go back and reconsider the quote above, where she brags about how a big-time reporter can come in and “change the fucking school.”

I was raised by an immigrant mother who grew up in a mostly black family in a mostly black country, and who suffered abuse at the hands of bigoted Texans who couldn’t understand a lick of her thick Caribbean accent. My family includes the framers of the Bahamian Constitution, the architect of the Jamaican military, and multiple members of Bahamian parliament. There were frighteningly intelligent, highly educated, politically powerful, proud black people. Not insecure New York elites. My mother is a very nice lady, but she’s also a tiger mom, and the one line you cannot ever cross with her is messing with her kids. I’ve seen her put the fear of God into Texan men twice her size. My wife, who is Latina, is almost too kind, but also again, fuck with her family and you can almost see her eyes roll black like a shark’s. I know my daughter is safe with her. As one of my friends recently said when I told her what Hannah-Jones did to her kid, “Yo, fuck this mom.”

Hannah-Jones opens the essay by saying her daughter Googled her school and its ratings, and “confronted not only the reality of the city’s deeply segregated educational landscape but also that the Brooklyn school they all attended existed firmly on the wrong side of the divide.” Again, that would give you the impression that she opposes segregation. She then explains:

> When Najya was entering pre-K, my husband, Faraji, and I decided to send her to the type of school that wealthier families with choices uniformly avoid — one that is low-income and mostly Black.
> 
> In New York, a combination of housing segregation and “academic” sorting, called school choice, that begins as early as pre-K means most Black students — even middle-class ones — are forced to attend racially segregated schools with high rates of poverty.

She puts “academic” in quotes to suggest that when schools say they sort kids by academic merit, using test scores, grades, and entrance exams, what they actually sort by is race and class. She’s half right. They track both, because the two are interlinked. The measures they call “academic” track family advantage — test prep, tutoring, parents with time and know-how, stable homes, parents who care enough about their kids’ education to seek out good schools — and some “academic” screens aren’t about learning at all, such as attendance, punctuality records, and interviews. She goes on to say:

> Being given access to the most resourced public schools in my hometown allowed me to flourish academically, and I am convinced that the integrated education my husband and I received set the foundation that has allowed us to become the successful upper-middle-class family that my daughter was born into.

So she knows the importance of a good education, she already had the resources to give one to her child, and she denied that benefit to her own blood. In the next passage, she talks about segregation:

> New York is considered one of this nation’s most liberal cities, while operating a school system that is among the most racially segregated. This segregation exists because policymakers and affluent parents — even those who profess a belief in integration and equality — shed their advocacy at the schoolhouse door and refuse to take or support any substantial action to integrate the city’s schools. Faraji and I decided that our role in this struggle meant upending the status quo and sending our child not to a wealthy white school but to a poor Black one.

She ignores that schools are also segregated because parents with resources want their kids in safe schools with strong learning environments and high-achieving peers. It isn’t polite conversation, but in New York, those schools are overwhelmingly wealthy, white, and Asian, and parents of every race who can get their kids into them do.

It also concerns me that Hannah-Jones has now written about her daughter’s education more than once, first to [brag about putting her daughter in a shit school](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/06/12/magazine/choosing-a-school-for-my-daughter-in-a-segregated-city.html), and now to make herself look good for realizing she made a mistake by putting her daughter in a shit school. I disagree with [Coleman Hughes](https://www.theradicalist.com/p/LINK) and others who think we ought to give Hannah-Jones credit for admitting she fucked up. I’m shocked by how many people in my life don’t seem to understand why honesty is valuable, or what its value even is. As if she is redeemed for doing a despicable thing because she confessed to doing it. Honesty only matters in relation to the thing you are being honest about. You don’t get points for being honest about a shitty thing you did. You might get points if you genuinely reform and come clean, but Hughes and others jump to that conclusion with seemingly no evidence. Or rather, when the evidence seems to point the other way. I am not convinced, even if we agree coming clean and reforming is a good and noble thing, that she is doing all this for such admirable reasons.

If I had to guess, I’d say her mea culpa is yet another calculated use of her daughter for her own narcissistic purposes. Namely, content for her book on school segregation, which she has been working on since at least 2018, when Chris Hayes [said](https://www.nbcnews.com/think/opinion/investigating-school-segregation-2018-nikole-hannah-jones-podcast-transcript-ncna896116) on his podcast:

> She’s writing a book now that I think is gonna be incredible, it’s being edited by an incredible editor named Chris Jackson who edits Ta-Nehisi Coates and Alex Wagner, among others.

Can’t wait to see this piece of shit hit the bookshelves. Her last book was about how America was founded in racism and didn’t become a democracy until blacks made it one. The new one is going to rewrite history yet again, probably by claiming that desegregation never happened. Here is a standout passage from her recent essay:

> We thought by sending Najya to a mostly low-income and Black school, we could help improve it for all the children there. From my past reporting, I knew that P.T.A.s at poor schools often can raise only a few thousand dollars, but Faraji and I could write checks that exceeded the annual P.T.A. budget. I witnessed how dismissive school officials could be to lower-income parents who lodged complaints or made demands for resources and how deferential they were to wealthy ones. I believed Faraji and I could bring some of that power with us.

We also all know that affluence matters, Hannah-Jones. Money means resources and better teachers. It also tends to mean safer schools: less crime, less bullying, fewer fights. So am I to believe that *this* platitude is what she learned after turning her own daughter into an activist project, turned her into content, and then, when the experiment failed, used her for content once again? In the essay, her daughter Najya confronts her devouring mother in rage, unable to comprehend why her own mother would sacrifice her education for politics:

> Glancing between her and the road, I told her everything that I had said a hundred times on stages, in interviews, in discussions since I had made her a symbol of how the choices we make can help sustain — or dismantle — educational inequality. I talked about the deep inequality in New York’s schools and how, like those Black families who came before us, we had an obligation to be part of the struggle of our larger community. Even if her schools were not academically the strongest, I explained, her father and I knew that we could provide for her tutoring and any other assistance to make up for what her school may have lacked.
> 
> But my child just shook her head, her face a mask of disbelief, and something else: betrayal.

Damn straight. This is what has done so much damage to the black community in America, these leftist activists crushing a people in the name of some poorly thought-out conceptualization of equality, instead of just focusing on black excellence and succeeding by *doing better*.

> “So you had money to send me to one of the best schools, and you chose not to?” she said, hurling her words. “Because other kids are disadvantaged, you didn’t want to put me in a great school?”
> 
> Najya was asking what I’d accomplished by not choosing the “best” school for her. And the honest answer is, I don’t know. When it comes to the choices my husband and I have made about public education, these days I feel that I failed both my values and my child.

She doesn’t even have a half-assed answer. Nothing. She simply doesn’t know. And to be honest, she’s lucky this is how everything turned out. If you stick your kid into a poor, mostly black school in New York, things could turn out considerably worse than having a semi-literate child. Here’s a revealing passage in which Hannah-Jones realizes she has put her daughter not just in a bad school, but a violent one:

> Several times, I had to instruct children to stop striking each other and to sit down, at one point physically pulling two boys apart. When I asked one of the aides why the children weren’t being supervised, she told me the lunch period was short-staffed. After I sat back down with Najya, she told me, “It’s always like this, and that’s why I don’t want to go to this school.”
> 
> It was not the children that horrified me — kids of all incomes are rambunctious at lunchtime. It was the callous disregard of the adults. Students respond to the high or low expectations of institutions and the grown-ups who operate them, and it was clear that these children were getting the message.

“Rambunctious” is doing a lot of work in that passage. Children hitting each other, an adult physically separating two boys, and an aide shrugging it off as a staffing problem isn’t kids being kids. Or boys horsing around. What’s even more disturbing is when Najya says, “It’s always like this.” Hannah-Jones sees it with her own eyes, hears the scale of it directly from her own daughter, and still reaches for the soft word, because the hard word would mean admitting what she’d done to her own kid. And this nightmare isn’t even done yet.

> Fourth grade was the worst year. At the first parent-teacher conference in November 2019, Najya’s teachers told us that she was not performing on grade-level in any of her academic subjects … I emailed her teachers and copied the principal … Neither of Najya’s teachers nor the principal bothered to respond.
> 
> Then one day in December, Najya came home and excitedly informed me that she had suddenly jumped three grade levels in reading in one month. Knowing that this was virtually impossible, I reached out to the school’s literacy coach and learned that after we began asking for the reading assessment, Najya’s teacher had instructed the coach to perform the evaluation and that she found Najya was reading just fine. What became clear to me was that Najya’s teacher had most likely never performed the school’s mandatory reading assessments for Najya or any of her classmates but instead had assumed the flagging competency of these mostly low-income Black children.
> 
> Finally, a few days before school let out for winter break, I received a folder in Najya’s backpack with the math assignments I had been requesting since our conference. Once I tucked her into bed, I sat at my dining room table and scanned one page, then another and another. None of them were graded. I started checking Najya’s work and discovered most of her answers were wrong. Najya believed herself to be good at math — it was her favorite subject. But now I understood she thought she was correctly solving all her problems because her teacher was not checking her work. Similarly, Najya’s writing assignments were rife with uncorrected punctuation, capitalization, and grammar errors.
> 
> I sat there silently for several minutes, conjuring the image of my little girl thinking she was soaring in class when she was failing, and my devastation morphed into hot rage.

Hannah-Jones and her husband immediately started private tutoring for Najya — nice — and got the district superintendent involved. But things at school did not improve.

> Our child was suffering, but all these children were — they were languishing in classrooms with educators who did not take their academic potential seriously and largely refused to teach.
> 
> What would happen to those children if we abandoned them as everyone else with power had?

This is a narcissist who thinks she is going to save every kid at her daughter’s school. Worse, if you go back to [that](https://nypost.com/2026/09/25/us-news/nikole-hannah-jones-campaigned-against-a-yuppie-invasion-of-kids-from-whiter-areas-to-failing-school-they-sent-daughter/) *[Post](https://nypost.com/2026/09/25/us-news/nikole-hannah-jones-campaigned-against-a-yuppie-invasion-of-kids-from-whiter-areas-to-failing-school-they-sent-daughter/)* [article](https://nypost.com/2026/09/25/us-news/nikole-hannah-jones-campaigned-against-a-yuppie-invasion-of-kids-from-whiter-areas-to-failing-school-they-sent-daughter/), you find this detail: David Goldsmith, president of the Community Education Council for District 13, which approved the rezoning Hannah-Jones and her husband vehemently fought against, explains that P.S. 307 was, in the paper’s words, “a standout, magnet school at the time Najya enrolled, thanks to former principal Roberta Davenport.” Goldsmith says:

> Roberta had built an amazing school. \[The Hannah-Joneses\] sent their kid to 307 probably because they heard what she was doing. She had a lot of funding, wouldn’t take no for an answer, had all sorts of program and grants.

This is quite a twist. One plausible scenario is that Hannah-Jones heard this school was performing well, reasoned the story would probably break in a few years, and put her kid in it, then started writing about how she was desegregating the school and talking about all the power she had as a big reporter to change things. You might imagine that her plan was, if her daughter and the school turned out great — thanks to Davenport — then Hannah-Jones could claim a victory, steal some valor, and urge more parents to do the same with their children, likely with disastrous consequences. Except none of that came to pass because, as the *Post* continues, “Davenport suddenly retired in June 2015, one year into Najya’s enrollment, and everything the principal built [crumbled under her replacement](https://nypost.com/2026/09/22/opinion/1619-project-scribe-nikole-hannah-jones-has-only-begun-to-face-how-she-failed-her-daughter/).”

So, if that’s what happened, clearly then the narrative had to change. Instead of a victory lap, Hannah-Jones needed an awakening arc — the realization, the confessional, and one more round of putting her daughter on display, possibly followed within a few months by the release of her new book, *Desegregated*, or whatever. Later in the essay, she writes about how Najya believed algebra was the *one* exception, the one class she actually understood. But then she failed the statewide exam and had to retake the entire course. Not only that, she began failing her way through the second time, also. And it started to break the young girl’s confidence in herself and her own abilities.

> Najya gushed about how easy the test had been. “I know I got an A, or at least a B,” she said smiling. A few days later, she came into the house, ran to her room without speaking, slammed the door and sunk to her floor, sobbing. She’d failed it.
> 
> This became an unbearable pattern. She’d come home glowing about an algebra exam because she “really knew the material this time” and my stomach would tie into knots. A few days later, I’d trail her upstairs to find her crumpled on her bed. “I studied so hard. I just feel so dumb.”
> 
> My child’s confidence wilted like an unwatered plant. She started to develop an almost debilitating anxiety around taking tests. She became sullen at school and began to act out. For the first time ever, I got messages from teachers about Najya’s behavior.

At the end of the essay, Najya offers one consolation in this tragedy. “She was glad she had not spent her early years in a white environment as many of the Black students at her high school had,” Hannah-Jones writes, “because she’d developed a strong sense of her identity and had the confidence to speak up against the casual racism they sometimes experienced.” Speaking up against racism is good, but you don’t have to practice segregation to learn that. Also, her daughter is grateful that she went to a black school and grew up in a black *way of life*, as if only by surrounding oneself with others like you can you “develop a strong sense” of your own identity. By that logic, the project of racial diversity in schools would seem flawed, no? Hannah-Jones does not push back, because this was part of her plan all along. She holds this up as the silver lining, maybe the one victory in this whole godforsaken story. But I read it as the final nail in this girl’s coffin. Not only is she uneducated, but now she belongs to an ideology that says it’s better to be among your own race if you want to be truly “Black.”

I’m not saying tribalism is always wrong. But you can’t hold that view as a black parent about black kids and be outraged when a white parent holds it about white kids. Imagine a white mother writing that she passed on a mixed school so her daughter could have an authentically white childhood, and that her daughter, whatever her test scores, was at least grateful to have grown up surrounded by white kids. Or, to reverse the phrasing, “I may have neglected my daughter’s education, but at least she didn’t have to be around blacks.” We all know what we’d call that. Not just segregationist, but disgustingly racist. The *Times* would not run it as a moving confessional. Public intellectuals would not be patting her on the back for her honesty. And the story wouldn’t last more than a few days before we all moved on. I think we have a real choice before us.

Either we discourage that kind of thinking in everyone, Hannah-Jones included, or we accept it from everyone. What we cannot do is call it racism in one direction and a moral awakening in the other. And that points to the real contradiction in Hannah-Jones’ project. She calls it desegregation, but look at what she actually chose. She could have sent her daughter to a whiter, higher-performing school, or sung the praises of her daughter’s white, Asian, Latino, and black school. But instead chose one that was almost entirely black and Latino. When the city moved to rezone kids from a school that was 85% white into hers, she and her husband fought it. She actively fought greater racial diversity because that would have meant more whites, and she practiced segregation while labeling it “desegregation,” the same way she practices racism and calls it “antiracism.” As her daughter’s middle school principal says in the essay, “It is important to not, in our pursuit of being antiracist, lower expectations. Because this is what happens, right? It’s racism under the guise of antiracism.”

Near the end of her essay, for the first time it seems, Hannah-Jones has a warm flicker of maternal instinct, and is compelled to put honest words to the page:

> I felt overcome with regret. Najya deserved better. They all did. But she was mine. This girl, my only child, looked to me to protect her, to give her what she needed to thrive. And I had not chosen her.

∙
