## In an exclusive excerpt from his new book, ‘This Cursed Beautiful Land,’ Evan Gershkovich searches for answers about his arrest while reporting in Russia

**HE LEANED FORWARD** and thrust the papers into my hands. Neatly drawn in black ballpoint pen, the documents displayed what appeared to be official tables of production figures.

“I copied the documents exactly so you’d understand them better,” the man, Pavel Sergienya, said in Russian.

I looked up at Pavel. A union boss for factory workers, he was stocky and well-built with closely cropped brown hair and the hands of someone who had spent the bulk of his 40-plus years in manual labor. We were in Yekaterinburg, a city in the Urals. I’d flown in from Moscow a few hours earlier. We were sitting at a wooden table near the bar at the far end of Bukowski Grill, a kitschy American-style steakhouse, with faux deer heads mounted on the walls. I sat on a couch, my back up against the wall.

It was just after 4 in the afternoon, and the place was nearly empty. My gaze drifted to the bartenders, who seemed bored, waiting for the evening rush. One fidgeted with a glass; the other observed me with a curious half-smile. My eyes flicked back to the pages. I wanted to understand what Pavel was showing me. I took out my notebook. He explained that the production of tanks was rising.

A year earlier, Russian President Vladimir Putin had thrown Europe into chaos when he ordered an invasion of [Ukraine](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/ukraine). The assault was supposed to be a lightning strike. Putin expected to rout the government in Kyiv in a matter of days. But now Russia’s armies were bogged down in the largest ground war in Europe since World War II. Ukraine, under the leadership of Volodymyr Zelensky, had shocked [Russia](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/russia) with the strength and guile of its forces, which were being buttressed by American and European support. The data in my hands suggested Moscow was gearing up for a protracted fight.

Amid the paranoia of wartime, it was dangerous for a Russian citizen to be a source for a foreign journalist. Sources could be accused of violating censorship laws—or, worse, committing treason. There were risks for me, too: Pavel shouldn’t bring any documents to the meeting, my editors advised, and I shouldn’t take anything from him if he did.

I was jotting down my own notes from the papers when I heard the sound of marching feet. I looked up from my notebook. A group of men was moving toward us from behind the bar.

“I believe we booked that table,” one of the men barked at us from across the room. Their sudden appearance, along with the odd statement, was bewildering.

As they drew closer, I realized they were masked, only their eyes visible through their balaclavas. Pavel stood up abruptly. A pang of guilt ripped through me: Had I compromised a source?

But panic immediately replaced that thought. Instinct took over, my hands moving to hide the papers in the gap between the bench and the wall.

The men quickly descended on us. An arm was yanking my neck, pulling me off the couch.

Just before I was forced onto the ground, my face pressed against the floor, I registered Pavel drifting away into the crowd.

Someone shouted: “You are under arrest by the Federal Security Service of the Russian Federation.” It registered immediately that this wasn’t the police. These men were FSB, the successor to the Soviet-era KGB. That told me only one thing: This wasn’t a misunderstanding.

Handcuffs clicked onto my wrists. A black sack enveloped my head. The men were now whisking me outside and forcing me into a van. I sat on a hard bench, facing the sliding door, which slammed shut.

My heart thumped. My mind was void of any thought. I lost track of time. Then the van door opened and shut again. I heard the sounds of shuffling and people sitting down. Finally, the van began to move. The hood came off.

“Who do you work for?” demanded a middle-aged man clad in a black leather jacket, white shirt and black tie. He sat directly in front of me, surrounded by several of the masked men who had now taken off their balaclavas.

I didn’t answer at first, my eyes getting used to the light. I tried to understand what I was being asked.

“The Wall Street Journal,” I said with a nervous laugh.

The hood went back on. One of the men in the van put me into a chokehold while another pummeled my midsection with his fists until I couldn’t breathe. Each blow seemed to come with a message: You are powerless.

Then the hood came back off again. The man in the leather jacket was looking at me again. “I’ll repeat my question,” he snarled. “Who do you work for?”

The darkening city slid by through the dimmed window behind his head. I couldn’t tell if it was already evening. “You don’t have much time left before we get to the station,” the man in the leather jacket continued. “If you want to be treated with leniency, speak up now.”

My mouth went dry. I knew it was pointless, but words began pouring out of me in a hoarse whisper. I explained that I was working on a story about Russia’s military production, that in my passport they could find a laminated card—accreditation from the Russian foreign ministry proving I was a journalist and allowing me, an American citizen, to report in the country.

The corners of the man’s mouth curled into a smile. He cut me off. “What would your government think if our journalists were working on this kind of article in *your* country?” he asked.

The van slowed down. We were at our destination. “That’s it,” the man said. “That was your last chance.”

Now the agents were dragging me, still hooded, up stairs and through hallways, my head and chest bent forward, my shins banging against the stairs. Though disoriented, I knew from years of reporting what usually came next: electrodes attached to various parts of the body, the most sensitive parts targeted if the victim didn’t quickly sign a statement admitting to a crime, real or imaginary. A thought gripped me: I’d rather die than experience what they might do to me.

We stopped abruptly, and they pushed the top of my head into a wall, forcing me to stand still. I stood like that, bent at the waist, for what felt like hours. The metal handcuffs dug into my wrists. My legs ached. The voices around me were muffled. Doors opened and shut. Soft steps padded by me. I tried to focus on my breathing. I tried to slow my racing thoughts.

Suddenly an agent appeared out of nowhere, his voice by my ear, oddly friendly. Where and when was I born? When did I move to Russia? Which media outlets did I work for, and in what years?

I rattled off my biography. New Jersey, 1991. Russia, 2017. The Moscow Times, then Agence France-Presse three years later. Finally, The Wall Street Journal, just before the war, at the start of 2022.

“Not one mistake,” he said.

“I know my own life,” I replied.

“Your *cover story*,” he corrected with a chuckle.

I chuckled too. The exercise had a calming effect. It was easier to imagine that this was a simulation, a game. Surely they didn’t really think that I was a spy.

Maybe an hour later, my legs nearly numb, I heard a woman’s voice say, “I can’t look at him like that anymore.” The agent took me by my arms and sat me down on a bench.

But moments later I was back on my feet. The agent brought me into an office and took off the black hood. The fluorescent light stung my eyes after hours in the darkness. Around me, in addition to the agent, stood three men; at the far end of the office, a man in a black suit sat behind a desk. One of the men told me in English that he was a translator and introduced the other two as witnesses.

“I don’t need a translator,” I answered in Russian. I was born in the United States, but my parents are Soviet émigrés and I grew up bilingual. “I need my lawyers.”

“You don’t have a right to lawyers yet,” the man at the desk said. “You are still in the process of being arrested.”

An agent shined purple UV light on my hands. Then he pulled out the papers from the restaurant and shined the light on them too. The light caught flecks of white on both my hands and the papers.

“See,” the agent said to the man at the desk. “The same substance.”

The room gave off a musty smell—one familiar to anyone who has ever entered a Russian bureaucratic building. On the wall hung a poster of a smirking Putin in a suit and sunglasses along with the word “ *Svoy*,” meaning: One of our own. Through the window I could see the glowing white and red lights of a KFC.

The man in the suit, an investigator, in Russian law enforcement parlance, calmly told me he was charging me with espionage, a crime that carries a sentence of up to 20 years in prison. “Do you understand what that means?” he asked.

I nodded. An odd sense of relief flooded through me. It’s a feeling I’ve been trying to understand ever since.

“If there’s anything you’d like to say,” the investigator continued, “now would be a good time.”

I asked again to see the Journal’s lawyers. The investigator got up and left me waiting with the agent and another who joined him. The agent who had checked my biography and I started talking. He seemed eager for the chance to speak with an American, especially one he believed to be a spy. I was curious to talk to an FSB agent—and to distract myself from thinking about what would come next.

He asked questions about life back home, complained that it took too long for the U.S. to come to the Soviet Union’s aid in World War II, and asked whether I thought Ukraine could mount a successful counteroffensive against Russia’s invading forces in the current war. Much of our conversation on that night in late March 2023 has been lost to the fog of shock, but one moment remains with me with absolute clarity.

“You can’t step in the same river twice,” the agent told me deep into the night, as we were preparing to part. “Your life will be completely different now.”

**I MOVED TO MOSCOW** nearly six years earlier, in the fall of 2017. I had been working as a news assistant in New York, at the New York Times, but was feeling restless to do something more—to become a reporter myself. Though I had drifted away from Russian as a teenager, I had grown up speaking it at home in the New Jersey suburbs. Now, I could see, my language skills might be an asset in my budding journalism career. The Moscow Times, a small, independent English-language paper founded in Russia after the fall of the Soviet Union, had become a training ground for a generation of foreign correspondents. The paper was looking for a junior reporter. The idea of returning to the place my parents had left was intriguing. I jumped at the opportunity.

After the breakup of the Soviet Union, the country had changed, opening itself to business, investment and outsiders. Its citizens had more freedom. But now it was changing again. By 2017, Russia was a kind of soft authoritarian regime. Cosplaying as a democracy, it worked to give the public a sense that it was involved in governing while at the same time ensuring that power, actually, always remained in the hands of Putin, Russia’s leader for nearly two decades. In service of that end, political arrests—or even assassinations—weren’t out of the ordinary, but they typically targeted players in the system.

Several years after I arrived, Americans started getting caught up in the dragnet, too. Paul Whelan, a former marine visiting Russia for a wedding, was accused of espionage. A longtime investor in the country, Michael Calvey, was targeted in an embezzlement case. And Naama Issachar, a tourist transiting through a Moscow airport, was arrested on charges of drug smuggling. There was speculation that, at least in the cases of Whelan and Issachar, they were taken as pawns to be exchanged for high-profile Russians imprisoned in other countries.

The truth is, I never thought too much about what these arrests meant for me. Seeing people rendered meek and powerless was nauseating, of course; the way they were put in a glass cage at court—as if on display as the catch of the day—was a dehumanizing show of the state’s strength. But I was covering these cases as a foreign reporter, watching from an emotional remove. While Russian journalists had been targeted over the years—even murdered—the unspoken rule with foreign correspondents was that we were protected, immune—to be expelled from the country, rather than arrested.

It wasn’t until my name popped up on a pro-Kremlin website that I began to worry at all—and even then, not very much. The outlet was a fringe one, my boss at the Moscow Times and I agreed, so I put it out of my mind.

One afternoon, I attended a press conference with Putin and Egyptian President Abdel Fattah Al Sisi, in Sochi, a port city on the Black Sea. It was October 2019, and Putin was hosting leaders of African nations for a summit, part of his bid to challenge the West for influence in the region. I sat in the third row. Putin was listening to Sisi through an interpretation headset clipped to his ear, while his eyes scanned the crowd. It was my first time seeing him in person, and I watched him intently—this man with so much sway over world affairs who, up close, seemed so small.

Meanwhile, I listened to my own interpretation earpiece and held up my phone to it as an audio recorder to capture the quotes—probably the only person in the crowd doing so. It must have been an odd sight, because Putin suddenly looked at me, then his eyes continued on, and then he looked at me again. We stared at each other like that for several seconds. His gaze then drifted once more, devoid of emotion.

Three and a half years later I was arrested. Had Putin himself been the one to order it?

**IN PRISON,** I met many people who had been arrested in sting operations similar to my own. This method of entrapping people has long been a key part of the Russian security service playbook. Since the founding of the Soviet Union, Moscow’s security services have relied on inducing people they wished to arrest into committing some hostile or unlawful act in order to arrest them. The method has been used to identify and detain dissidents, discredit political opponents, penetrate underground groups, and justify deeper political repressions in the aftermath. The technique is commonly used not only against people who pose real risk to the state but also against civilians who do not—and who may not have committed wrongdoing otherwise.

The result is that Russians generally understand that people can end up in prison without legal grounds. My last cellmate, Nikita, for instance, told me that he believed me that I wasn’t a spy but at the same time felt it was reasonable for Moscow to arrest me given it was wartime and I was reporting on sensitive information. He was a big supporter of Putin’s and was excited when he found out that the Russian president had spoken about me in public.

“No f—ing way,” he said. “That’s crazy. I’m going to tell Mom.”

I reminded him that Putin hadn’t exactly spoken of me in positive terms.

“Who cares,” Nikita said. “Putin talked about you. That’s all that matters.”

After my release in a prisoner swap a month later, in August 2024, several Kremlin officials passed word to me through mutual contacts that they had worried about me while I was in prison. How was I holding up and was I feeling alright? One former Kremlin official even quipped to me, after I had told him I was working on this book, “You know, I’m just glad that Putin has made you financially better off, at least.” Clearly, officials of his ilk did not believe I was a spy. But what about the FSB, the praetorian guard and their leader, Putin?

I have ended up with a mixed answer to the question.

Perhaps the most extraordinary information I learned after my release was that the FSB had a backup plan for my arrest. Christo Grozev, an investigative journalist, had offered me his help in figuring out the lingering questions around my arrest. Tall, lanky and unassuming, his apartment fits the stereotype of a hard-driving, even obsessive investigator. During my first visit, a couple of empty wine bottles stood on the floor in the kitchen; his walls were covered with whiteboards and tack boards filled with the faces of Russian intelligence operatives. At the top of one was Vadim Krasikov, the FSB agent sent to Germany on an assassination mission for whom I was swapped.

Unlike most reporters, Grozev occupies a unique role: part journalist, part fixer. Alexei Navalny, the late Russian opposition leader, had once hailed him as “a modern-day Sherlock Holmes.” Aside from uncovering Krasikov’s real identity and the FSB hit squad behind the first attempt to kill Navalny by poisoning, Grozev had also exposed the Russian agents who poisoned the former Russian spy Sergei Skripal in Britain.

More recently, Grozev had gotten access to the communications of Denis Alimov, a suspected Russian spy, after he was arrested in Colombia in February 2026. He shared with me an email Alimov had received in June 2024, while I was in prison, from a man named Maxim Piunov. Through leaked Russian databases, Grozev found that Piunov was working for the FSB and determined that he was involved in the work of recruiting prisoners for the Wagner paramilitary group. Piunov told Alimov that he had been working on a ploy with an officer whom, he wrote, he had known since his time working in counterintelligence in “NSK,” an abbreviation I knew stood for the city of Novosibirsk.

“Evan Gershkovich was originally supposed to come to me; I’d set a trap for him,” Piunov wrote. But, he added, an officer higher up the ladder “postponed the meeting and Gershkovich went to Yekaterinburg instead, where he was arrested in the end.”

All of this connected the dots. I had planned another interview in Novosibirsk at the time of my arrest, and it now appeared that the meeting had been another dangle the FSB was running in parallel in case the ploy via Pavel didn’t work out. The FSB, it seems, had been looking for suitable ways to lure me into a trap.

The FSB was also, in my view, seeking the best way of packaging the tale for Putin. *That* was the purpose of my first meeting with Pavel, I suspect—two weeks before I returned for a second interview and he handed me the papers. The FSB could go to Putin and sell him on a story that would lead him to believe I was in fact a spy and give the greenlight for my arrest at the second meeting. Putin, it seems to me, fully bought into this narrative.

After my release, I thanked the conservative American commentator Tucker Carlson, who during his highly publicized trip to Russia in 2024 had asked Putin to free me during his interview. When I met with Carlson, he filled me in on an informal exchange that the two of them had afterward. As Putin showed him around the Kremlin, Carlson asked the Russian president about me once again. Putin told Carlson he was confused about something: Why would the U.S. work so hard to free a basketball player but not a spy?

In another meeting after my release, a world leader who had become involved in the swap told me that Putin had once spent over an hour lecturing him on the fact that I was indeed an intelligence operative, that I had been caught red-handed. When the person asked Putin if he hadn’t considered whether it was much too easy to catch me red-handed—because a trained agent might have figured out that there was a sting operation closing in on him, for example—Putin didn’t budge.

“He’s an idiot,” Putin said, banging his fist on the table. “He was much too obvious.”

**I’VE OFTEN WONDERED** about my former cellmates. Two years to the day of my arrest, I was in Qatar, talking to officials involved in helping mediate my release while dining on stuffed pigeons. The meal felt, in a twisted way, cathartic. Pigeons had often come to our cell window in Moscow’s Lefortovo prison.

One spring day in 2025, I opened up the count of Russian casualties being compiled by the exiled Russian media outlet Mediazona and the BBC and saw the face of Nikita Parshin staring back at me. He had died in Ukraine.

Nikita was 20 when we met and had the maturity level of a middle-schooler—cheating at checkers and averse to walks and books. But he had also taught me some of Russian prison’s unwritten rules—and how I could avoid ending up in the lowest rung of its caste system.

At first the news of Nikita’s death didn’t feel real. I furiously googled his name. I found that the day before I was swapped, he had been convicted and sentenced to 4½ years in prison for defrauding the elderly. I reached out to a contact still working in Russia and learned that Nikita died only a couple of months later, in October 2024, likely on his one and only offensive somewhere in eastern Ukraine.

I spent an hour on Google Maps looking at Nikita’s village. He had grown up in a small village house along an unpaved road. I wrote a letter to his mother. She replied that she didn’t understand why he’d gone to the front; she knew all he had wanted was to get home.
