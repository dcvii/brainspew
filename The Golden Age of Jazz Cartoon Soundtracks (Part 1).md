### For almost a decade, stylish jazz sounds accompanied the Flintstones, the Pink Panther, Charlie Brown, and other cartoon heroes

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Llm6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fbucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31a6b95e-810c-4194-8563-0fb003b26144_2400x800.jpeg)

On Friday evening, September 30 1960, families across the United States kicked off their weekend in the most American way possible—by watching TV. To their surprise, ABC inserted a cartoon into the 8:30 PM time slot. It was the first prime time animated show in the history of television.

The series was called *The Flintstones*, and involved a Stone Age family. This was comedy, so it started with a musical joke. Instead of trying to create a prehistoric soundtrack, the producers launched the episode with a big band chart that was halfway between vaudeville background music and swing jazz.

But it isn’t the famous “Meet the Flintstones” song that most of you have heard—one of the most insistent earworms in TV history. Instead, for the first two seasons of the show, the theme was [“Rise and Shine,”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rjAiAqMHE60) composed by stalwart Hanna-Barbera music producer Hoyt Curtin.

This kind of quasi-jazz was fairly typical of cartoon music, going back to its earliest days. The time-honored formula was built on simple syncopations, more sweet than hot, incorporated into a soundtrack that might be best described as programmatic novelty music. Walt Disney had established this approach back in 1928 with his breakthrough Mickey Mouse cartoon “Steamboat Willie”—which legitimized sound cartoons with music.

Disney had to sell his car to help pay for the recording which featured the less-than-hip Green Brothers Novelty Band from Omaha, Nebraska. You will never mistake the Green Brothers for the Modern Jazz Quartet, but they met Disney’s needs nicely, and soon others were imitating this formula. Cartoon music now had a danceable beat, and at least a tiny dose of jazz.

Of course, some did this better than others. When Warner Bros. started using Raymond Scott’s unconventional scores for its Looney Tunes cartoons, they aimed to differentiate themselves from Disney. The House of Mouse was now shifting its emphasis to full-length animated films with Broadway-esque songs, while Scott’s music was more avant-garde, with a slightly unhinged jazz sensibility. Even so, there was still a huge gap between Looney Tunes music and what patrons to a jazz club or swing dance would hear.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r3FLN0iQ9SQ)

In the early 1960s, this formula would change again. Many of the leading session composers and musicians in Hollywood had side gigs in the SoCal jazz economy, and they welcomed any opportunity to introduce hipper ingredients into children’s programming.

The original Flintstone’s theme “Rise and Shine” wasn’t one of Curtin’s finer moments. If you follow the evolution of his music, you can see how he grew more ambitious over the course of the decade. (Later I’ll discuss his breakthrough soundtrack to *Jonny Quest* in 1964—which has some of the hottest writing for trombone you will ever hear on a TV series.) When he got an opportunity to create a new theme for *The Flintstones*, he rose to the occasion, creating one of the most memorable TV songs of all time.

This only happened because *The Flintstones* shifted from black-and-white to color in its third season, and studio execs decided the show needed other changes to signal what we now call a *brand refresh*. So they added a baby (named Pebbles) to the Flintstone family. And, best of all—from my perspective—they wanted a new theme song.

This song, entitled “Meet the Flintstones” was an earworm of demonic intensity. Even people only casually aware of the show would hear it once and remember it forever. Along the way, the score earned laughs by juxtaposing a hipper modern jazz big band sound with the hokey prehistoric setting.

The hot trend in comedy TV back then was a theme song that introduced the characters and set up the back story for the show. *The Beverly Hillbillies* had done that effectively the previous year, and soon a host of other shows—from *Gilligan’s Island* to *Green Acres* —would follow the same formula. (When did this trend end? Was *The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air*, from 1990, the last prominent example? Will back story theme songs ever come back?) As Hanna-Barbera began working on season three upgrades, the studio decided that it’s new Flintstone’s theme ought to do the same.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vHMkgIkUyA)

The lyrics added to the zaniness, with a clever reference to bebop scat-singing—that use of wordless syllables as a kind of vocal equivalent to a horn solo. In this instance, Fred’s trademark phrase “Yabba-Dabba-Doo” is turned into a promise that viewers will have “a dabba doo time” watching the show. Some observers may have also detected a transgressive reference in the final line of the song, which invites the audience to “have a gay old time”? But that didn’t last long—the word ‘gay’ was replaced with ‘groovy’ in later spin-offs.

The real attraction here was the strange music. The harmonies are what jazz musicians called “rhythm changes”—in other words, loosely based on the chord progression in George Gerswhin’s song “I Got Rhythm.” This is the same formula that Duke Ellington had used in “Cotton Tail,” Thelonious Monk in “Rhythm-n-ing,” and Dizzy Gillespie in “Anthropology,” to cite just a few examples (there are hundreds of others).

This was a sly inside joke. TV viewers don’t notice chord changes, but Hollywood musicians found this adaptation very amusing. And even middle America could grasp the comic intent behind the music. Jazz themes were already familiar to TV audiences, but none were as humorously incongruous as this one. The ridiculous contrast between the images, which (for example) show Fred Flintstone propelling his primitive vehicle with his feet, and a swinging modern jazz chart, reminiscent of what Woody Herman would do with hot postwar orchestras, set the tone for the entire series.

But adding to the strangeness, the melody sounds similar to Beethoven’s B theme in the second movement of his *Tempest Sonata*. I doubt that was intentional, but with composer Hoyt Curtin, you could never be sure.

I applaud all this, but I note that the audience for the show continued to shrink in the third season, despite all efforts at refreshing the concept. It survived another three seasons, before getting cancelled in 1966. But like many series from this era, *The Flintstones* had more lasting impact in syndication after it disappeared from the prime time lineup. Numerous spin-offs and sequels appeared over the next half century. There has even been some talk about *The Flintstones* getting updated from the Stone Age into a [Bronze Age version](https://www.thedad.com/flintstones-sequel/) (no, I’m not joking).

Many consider the Flintstones theme music as a negligible bit of pop culture fluff. I rarely encounter anyone who takes it very seriously—but mostly because the producers made it deliberately absurd. But several outstanding jazz cover versions have been released over the year. For example, consider this swinging track from Clark Terry.

But the most striking tribute to Hanna-Barbera’s success came in the form of imitation. Other Hollywood studios soon started updating their cartoon soundtracks with cooler, hipper stylings.
